# The Unconstrained Explainer: Divine Psychology, Ad Hoc Reasoning, and the Difference Between Explanation and Accommodation

## Target length

16–18 pages, with a working target of approximately 17 pages.

## Central thesis

The essay's main claim is:

> **Explanatory power does not arise merely from showing that a hypothesis can produce or accommodate an observed fact. A successful explanation must constrain the space of possible observations such that the actual observation was antecedently more expected than relevant alternatives. Agentic explanations therefore require independently justified information about the agent's motives, preferences, limitations, and decision procedures. Where those constraints are absent—as in many appeals to divine intervention, revelation, miracles, and unspecified intelligent design—the attribution of intentions after observing the evidence risks becoming ad hoc: it provides retrospective narrative intelligibility without corresponding predictive or evidential force.**

Kevin Scharp's idea of **divine psychology** is the exemplary theological manifestation of this deeper epistemological problem.

The essay should repeatedly return to the distinctions:

$$
\boxed{\text{Can explain after the fact} \neq \text{made probable beforehand}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Accommodation} \neq \text{Prediction}}
$$

---

# I. Introduction: When “an explanation” explains too much

**Target: 1–1.25 pages**

Open with the intuitive attractiveness of agent explanations.

We constantly explain events by saying that someone **wanted** something and therefore **did** something. This is perfectly legitimate in ordinary life because human agency is heavily constrained by background knowledge:

- physical capabilities,
- psychology,
- incentives,
- social circumstances,
- past behavior,
- geography,
- available technology.

Problems arise when an agent is introduced whose:

- causal powers are enormous or unlimited;
- motivations are poorly specified;
- possible actions are effectively unconstrained;
- intentions are reconstructed only after observing the event.

The essay's central question:

> **When does attributing an event to an intelligent agent genuinely explain it, and when does it merely redescribe the event through a retrospectively constructed intentional story?**

Introduce Scharp's “divine psychology” as an unusually clear instance of this problem.

Preview the scope:

- miracle claims;
- revelation and divine hiddenness;
- resurrection apologetics;
- Fatima;
- the Shroud of Turin;
- intelligent design;
- probabilistic apologetics more generally;
- ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses and Bayesian model comparison.

End the introduction with the thesis above.

# II. What an explanation has to do: possibility is not probability

**Target: 1.5 pages**

This section establishes the philosophical framework before discussing theology.

## A. Explanation as constraint

An explanation should not merely be compatible with an observation. It should help distinguish an observed outcome from relevant alternatives.

Let the actual evidence be:

$$
E
$$

and the relevant alternatives:

$$
E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_n.
$$

A theory gains explanatory content when it makes some of these outcomes more expected and others less expected.

This is why physics, biology, psychology, history, and sociology are explanatory partly because they tell us:

> these outcomes are relatively expected;

> those outcomes are relatively unexpected.

The central point is that **explanation requires constraints on the space of possibilities**.

## B. Plausibility versus probability

Develop the distinction between logical or narrative plausibility and evidential probability.

“There is a plausible reason God might do \(X\)” is not equivalent to:

$$
P(X\mid G)\text{ is high}.
$$

Still less does it establish:

$$
P(X\mid G)>P(X\mid \neg G).
$$

Words such as:

- plausible;
- fitting;
- unsurprising;
- natural;
- what we might expect;

can subtly move between logical/intuitive compatibility and probabilistic support.

This should be one of the essay's recurring warnings.

## C. Narrative intelligibility versus evidential explanation

Introduce a distinction that can organize the whole essay.

**Narrative intelligibility:** I can tell a coherent story under which \(E\) makes sense.

**Predictive explanation:** my independently specified hypothesis actually made \(E\) relatively expected.

The former is much cheaper than the latter.

# III. Scharp's divine psychology: the exemplary case

**Target: 1.75–2 pages**

## A. What divine psychology asks

The question is not merely:

> Can God do \(X\)?

It is:

> Why would God want \(X\)?

and, crucially:

> Why would God choose \(X\) rather than \(Y\)?

Theism as a bare existential hypothesis:

$$
G=\text{God exists}
$$

does not by itself tell us much about:

$$
P(E\mid G).
$$

Once an argument says:

> “This observation is what we should expect if God exists,”

it has introduced claims about God's intentions.

## B. The hidden intentional structure

For an intentional divine explanation:

$$
G + I + C \rightarrow A \rightarrow E
$$

where:

- \(G\) = existence and attributes of God;
- \(I\) = divine intentions or preferences;
- \(C\) = relevant circumstances;
- \(A\) = selected divine action;
- \(E\) = observed result.

The evidential issue concerns something like:

$$
P(E\mid G,I,C).
$$

But apologetic argument often talks loosely as though that were simply:

$$
P(E\mid G).
$$

## C. Divine psychology and the inscrutability dilemma

One of the most important generalized arguments:

When favorable evidence appears:

> “This is precisely the sort of thing a good God might do.”

When apparently unfavorable evidence appears:

> “We cannot presume to know God's reasons.”

But greater inscrutability broadens the expected outcome space.

Thus:

> **The flexibility that protects theism from counterevidence simultaneously weakens its ability to claim confirmatory evidence.**

This is the first appearance of the broader skeptical problem.

# IV. Ad hoc explanation: from philosophical intuition to Bayesian formalism

**Target: 2–2.25 pages**

This section integrates the analysis of ad hoc reasoning and gives the paper its formal machinery.

An ad hoc adjustment is not merely a hypothesis introduced after seeing evidence. It is a modification introduced mainly to protect or fit a theory in response to a particular anomaly without sufficient independent theoretical or evidential motivation.

## A. Auxiliary hypotheses

Start with:

$$
H
$$

encountering unexpected data \(D\).

Add:

$$
H'=H+A.
$$

Then perhaps:

$$
P(D\mid H')\gg P(D\mid H).
$$

The modified theory fits the data better.

But this does not by itself vindicate \(H'\), because \(A\) itself requires justification.

## B. Bayes' theorem

Retain the standard formalism:

$$
P(H\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid H)P(H)}{P(D)}.
$$

Explain:

- \(P(H)\): prior probability;
- \(P(D\mid H)\): likelihood;
- \(P(H\mid D)\): posterior probability.

The important question is not only:

> Can \(H\) accommodate \(D\)?

but:

> How much probability did \(H\) assign to something like \(D\) before \(D\) was known?

## C. The selection-space problem

Suppose there is a large set of possible auxiliaries:

$$
A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots,A_N.
$$

If one searches this space after seeing the evidence and selects \(A_k\) because it fits, the apparent success of \(A_k\) is much less impressive than if \(A_k\) had been specified independently beforehand.

This is the distinction between **prediction** and **post hoc accommodation**.

## D. Bayesian complexity and the Occam factor

Retain the marginal-likelihood formalism:

$$
P(D\mid M)=
\int P(D\mid\theta,M)P(\theta\mid M)\,d\theta.
$$

A flexible model is not evaluated solely at whichever value of \(\theta\) happens to fit the observation best.

Compare:

$$
Y=10\pm1
$$

with a model allowing:

$$
0\leq Y\leq100.
$$

If the observation is \(Y=10\), both can accommodate it, but the first theory took a much greater predictive risk. It concentrated more prior probability near the actual outcome.

A theory that permits almost anything spreads its probability across many possibilities.

## E. Data-dependent priors and double counting

Suppose we observe:

$$
Y=17.
$$

Then introduce:

$$
Y=10+A
$$

and set:

$$
A=7.
$$

One cannot then pretend that a prior sharply centered on \(A=7\) existed independently of the evidence.

If the value of the auxiliary came from the same data now being used to confirm the model, the evidence is effectively being counted twice.

## F. When an adjustment stops being objectionably ad hoc

An auxiliary can become legitimate if it:

1. explains more than the single anomaly that motivated it;
2. generates new, risky predictions;
3. can be independently tested;
4. connects naturally with established knowledge;
5. successfully predicts observations not used to construct it.

This qualification is essential. The argument should not treat every post-evidential theoretical modification as illegitimate.

# V. Divine psychology as a problem of latent parameters

**Target: 1.5 pages**

This section formally fuses Scharp's divine psychology with the Bayesian discussion.

Let:

- \(G\) = God;
- \(M\) = divine motive;
- \(A\) = divine action;
- \(E\) = evidence.

An apologist may make the following quantity very high:

$$
P(E\mid G,M^*,A^*)\approx1,
$$

where \(M^*\) and \(A^*\) have been selected because they fit \(E\).

But what is needed for theism as an explanatory hypothesis is something closer to:

$$
P(E\mid G).
$$

Schematically:

$$
P(E\mid G)
=
\sum_{M,A}
P(E\mid G,M,A)P(M,A\mid G).
$$

Or, using a continuous parameterization:

$$
P(E\mid G)
=
\int
P(E\mid G,\theta)
P(\theta\mid G)\,d\theta,
$$

where \(\theta\) represents divine preferences, intentions, and decision parameters.

The central point:

> A deity capable of doing almost anything creates an enormous action-space. Unless theology independently constrains the distribution over divine motives and choices, causal power does not translate into predictive specificity.

This yields an important thesis:

> **Omnipotence increases causal scope while potentially decreasing predictive specificity.**

# VI. Miracles as agentic explanations

**Target: 2.5–3 pages**

This should be the largest concrete application.

A miracle, in the relevant apologetic sense, is a particular divine intervention that changes what would otherwise occur through ordinary processes.

That makes a miracle explanation fundamentally a **personal or agentic explanation**.

## A. Anomaly versus miracle

Separate the following claims:

1. something unusual occurred;
2. presently known natural mechanisms do not straightforwardly explain it;
3. some agent caused it;
4. the agent was supernatural;
5. the agent was God;
6. God intended the event as communication;
7. the communication vindicated a particular theological doctrine.

Evidence for proposition 1 does not automatically establish proposition 7.

The label “miracle” is therefore not a simple observational description. It already contains a substantial explanatory and theological interpretation.

## B. The method of exclusion

Reject the inference:

$$
\text{no presently adequate natural explanation}
\Rightarrow
\text{divine intervention}.
$$

An incomplete natural explanation can still compare favorably with an even less constrained supernatural explanation.

The argument is not:

> “Science will explain it someday.”

It is:

> **Explanatory incompleteness must be compared symmetrically.**

## C. Methodological symmetry

Naturalistic hypotheses are often asked:

- What mechanism?
- What causal chain?
- What probability?
- What means?
- What motive?
- What psychological plausibility?
- What geographical constraints?
- What historical circumstances?

Miracle hypotheses should receive the same treatment.

“God did it to vindicate his message” cannot be exempt from explanatory analysis merely because it invokes a divine agent.

## D. Contrastive divine explanation

For every proposed miracle, ask:

> Why \(X\) rather than \(Y\)?

> Why this place?

> Why this time?

> Why these recipients?

> Why this physical manifestation?

> Why this degree of ambiguity?

> Why not a more direct means?

This converts divine psychology into a general methodology for evaluating miracle claims.

# VII. Three case studies: Resurrection, Shroud, Fatima

**Target: 2.5 pages**

Rather than creating three unrelated mini-essays, use the same analytical template in each case.

## A. Resurrection

Grant as much historical data as necessary for the sake of argument.

Then ask:

> If the motive is to vindicate Jesus, why are appearances concentrated among followers and adjacent social networks?

Naturalistic psychological and social processes predict strong localization because of:

- acquaintance;
- expectation;
- communication;
- memory;
- social transmission;
- geography.

God is not similarly constrained.

Thus the observed localization may look more like the signature of natural and social limitations than the unrestricted capacities attributed to God.

Then evaluate means–end fit.

If the objective is universal vindication or missionary propagation, why such a restricted revelation?

The argument is not that God must choose the “best possible miracle.” It is the narrower question:

> Does the action fit the alleged motive at all, given the capacities of the proposed agent?

## B. Shroud of Turin

Naturalistic hypotheses legitimately face questions about:

- motive;
- production technique;
- materials;
- historical feasibility;
- why that particular method.

Apply symmetry.

If God created or miraculously marked the Shroud to vindicate the resurrection:

- Why this artifact?
- Why its historical obscurity?
- Why its ambiguity?
- Why this mechanism?
- Why not a manifestation with clearer evidential reach?

Again, divine ability is not the problem.

**Divine selection is.**

## C. Fatima

This should be the richest of the three examples.

Even granting a substantial unusual event, the intentional interpretation requires propositions such as:

- God values Marian apparitions;
- God chooses these recipients;
- God chooses these children;
- God chooses this historical setting;
- God wants authentication;
- God chooses a localized luminous phenomenon;
- God permits divergent descriptions;
- God does not leave universally accessible or repeatable evidence.

Then ask:

> Where did those propositions come from?

Fatima cannot simultaneously generate the divine psychology and then be confirmed by the psychology inferred from Fatima.

This is where the data-dependent-prior analogy is especially useful.

# VIII. Revelation, hiddenness, and the free-will auxiliary

**Target: 1.25–1.5 pages**

A common theological auxiliary explains ambiguous revelation by saying:

> God preserves epistemic distance because overwhelmingly clear evidence would interfere with free choice.

Apply the same criteria.

## A. Antecedent justification

Why think God values precisely this degree of epistemic ambiguity?

## B. Means–end fit

If God wants meaningful voluntary response, compare this with **informed consent**.

Ordinarily, autonomy is enhanced by:

- relevant information;
- intelligible information;
- evidence that the source is credible.

Ignorance does not normally increase autonomy.

Thus:

$$
\text{free choice}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{weak evidence}.
$$

Strong evidence that someone exists does not compel one to love, worship, or obey them.

## C. Ad hoc escalation

If ambiguous revelation is defended with:

- free will;
- soul-making;
- non-coercion;
- hidden higher purposes;

the issue is not whether these are conceivable.

It is whether they have independent support and yield constrained expectations rather than being successively introduced to reconcile observed ambiguity.

# IX. Religious pluralism and theory-ladenness

**Target: 1 page**

This section prevents the argument from collapsing into “atheist versus believer.”

Different religious traditions possess competing divine psychologies.

A Jewish theology may reject the very motivations Christianity attributes to God in explaining the resurrection.

Likewise, a particular event may be described as:

- Catholic Marian authentication;
- Protestant error or deception;
- Muslim theological misinterpretation;
- generic supernatural intervention;
- non-divine spiritual agency;
- naturalistic anomaly.

Therefore the observation is **theory-laden**.

A person does not simply observe:

> “God authenticated Mary.”

That conclusion depends upon a substantial pre-existing theological framework.

This creates a possible circular structure:

1. infer God's preferences from the religious tradition;
2. interpret the miracle using those preferences;
3. use the miracle to confirm the tradition.

# X. Intelligent Design: adding an agent without adding a theory

**Target: 2 pages**

This should be a major section rather than an appendix.

## A. Why agency sometimes explains extremely well

Do not claim that agency is inherently unscientific.

Human design detection can be powerful because we possess extensive independent knowledge about:

- human goals;
- technology;
- capabilities;
- materials;
- constraints;
- manufacturing practices.

Agency explains because the agent is **independently characterized**.

## B. The unspecified designer problem

Introduce:

$$
D=\text{an intelligent designer exists}.
$$

The relevant question is not merely:

> Can \(D\) produce biological structure \(E\)?

It is:

$$
P(E\mid D)=?
$$

To constrain that quantity, we would need information about the designer's:

- goals;
- aesthetic preferences;
- efficiency preferences;
- tolerance for redundancy;
- willingness to reuse structures;
- interest in complexity;
- interest in consciousness;
- intervention strategy;
- technological limitations, if any.

Without this, \(D\) has enormous causal capacity and little predictive specificity.

## C. Evolution as a constrained comparator

Evolutionary theory is not merely:

> “Nature did it.”

It contains structured mechanisms including:

- descent;
- mutation;
- inheritance;
- selection;
- drift;
- developmental constraint;
- historical contingency;
- population processes.

These mechanisms restrict the possible outcome space.

Evolution may be incomplete in a particular case while still possessing more explanatory structure than:

> “an intelligence selected this outcome.”

## D. ID as a residual explanatory variable

A common structure is:

$$
\text{evolutionary processes}
+
D\text{ where needed}.
$$

If ordinary evolutionary mechanisms explain most of the history and design is inserted wherever difficulty remains, \(D\) risks functioning as an unconstrained residual:

$$
\text{unexplained remainder}\rightarrow D.
$$

This parallels the method of exclusion in miracle reasoning.

## E. Post hoc designer psychology

Efficient structure:

> Designer values efficiency.

Inefficient structure:

> Designer had competing objectives.

Reuse:

> Designer prefers reuse.

Novelty:

> Designer values innovation.

Imperfection:

> Designer works through constraints.

Again, the issue is not whether each story is conceivable.

It is:

> What independently justified theory of the designer predicted these preferences beforehand?

This is the Intelligent Design version of divine psychology.

# XI. The generalized pattern: the unconstrained agent as explanatory wildcard

**Target: 1.25 pages**

Now abstract beyond theology.

Suppose an observation \(E\) is already known.

Introduce an agent \(A\).

Then infer an intention \(I_E\) tailored to \(E\):

$$
E
\rightarrow
I_E
\rightarrow
A\text{ explains }E.
$$

The direction of epistemic dependence is backward.

A stronger explanatory structure would be:

$$
\text{independent theory of }A
\rightarrow
I
\rightarrow
P(E)\text{ high}
\rightarrow
E\text{ observed}.
$$

This yields several general principles.

## Principle 1: Causal possibility is cheap

$$
P(E\mid H)>0
$$

does not establish strong confirmation.

## Principle 2: Agent explanations need decision theory

Agency explains only insofar as we know what the agent tends to choose.

## Principle 3: Power without preference increases underdetermination

More possible actions can reduce predictive concentration.

## Principle 4: Inscrutability has an evidential cost

A hypothesis that accommodates every possible result explains the actual result weakly.

## Principle 5: Retrospective specificity can conceal antecedent flexibility

An explanation may appear highly detailed after the observation while having been selected from an enormous hypothesis space.

# XII. Objections, qualifications, and what would count as successful divine explanation

**Target: 1.25–1.5 pages**

This section is essential for avoiding overstatement.

## Objection 1: “But agents are inherently less predictable than laws.”

Answer:

Yes, but not unpredictably without limit. Human-agent explanations work because behavior is probabilistically constrained by background knowledge.

The relevant standard is not deterministic prediction.

It is **differential expectation**.

## Objection 2: “God's goodness constrains God's behavior.”

Answer:

Possibly.

But then the bridge must be specified and justified:

$$
\text{God is good}
\rightarrow
\text{God probably chooses }A.
$$

That bridge is itself substantive theology.

## Objection 3: “We do not need exact probabilities.”

Agree.

The argument does not require numerical precision.

But comparative claims such as:

> “This is more expected under God”

still require some principled ordering of expectations.

## Objection 4: “Any scientific theory uses auxiliary assumptions.”

Correct.

That is why the distinction between legitimate and ad hoc modification matters.

Auxiliaries gain legitimacy when they acquire:

- independent confirmation;
- theoretical integration;
- novel successful predictions;
- risky tests.

## Objection 5: “Does this disprove God?”

No.

The argument is not necessarily a defeater for theism itself.

It is a broad defeater for a particular explanatory strategy:

> using weakly constrained divine intentions as though they supplied strong likelihoods for observations.

## Positive criterion

A genuinely successful divine explanation would require something like:

1. independently motivated theology;
2. sufficiently precise constraints on divine purposes;
3. specific consequences not derived from the target evidence;
4. risky predictions;
5. clear contrastive success against rival hypotheses.

This makes the argument constructive rather than merely skeptical.

# XIII. Conclusion: from explanatory flexibility to explanatory discipline

**Target: 0.75–1 page**

Return to Scharp.

Divine psychology exposes a much wider epistemological phenomenon.

The question is never merely:

> Could this agent have produced the event?

The explanatory questions are:

> Why would the agent produce it?

> Why this rather than alternatives?

> What constrained that prediction beforehand?

> What would the theory have regarded as surprising?

The conclusion should distinguish two superficially similar properties:

$$
\text{ability to accommodate many observations}
$$

versus:

$$
\text{ability to predictively discriminate among observations}.
$$

A theory can gain the former by sacrificing the latter.

Possible closing formulation:

> **The central mistake is to confuse the existence of a coherent story after the evidence with possession of a theory that made the evidence likely before it occurred. Divine psychology matters because it makes that distinction impossible to ignore.**

# Formalisms to preserve in the full essay

Bayes' theorem:

$$
P(H\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid H)P(H)}{P(D)}.
$$

Auxiliary modification:

$$
H'=H+A.
$$

Improved fit after adding an auxiliary:

$$
P(D\mid H')\gg P(D\mid H).
$$

Selection-space formulation:

$$
A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_N.
$$

Marginal likelihood / prior-predictive expression:

$$
P(D\mid M)=
\int P(D\mid\theta,M)P(\theta\mid M)\,d\theta.
$$

Theological adaptation:

$$
P(E\mid G)
=
\sum_{M,A}
P(E\mid G,M,A)P(M,A\mid G).
$$

Continuous version:

$$
P(E\mid G)
=
\int
P(E\mid G,\theta)
P(\theta\mid G)\,d\theta.
$$

Core conceptual distinctions:

$$
\text{Causal capability}\neq\text{predictive probability}
$$

$$
\text{Plausibility}\neq\text{probability}
$$

$$
\text{Accommodation}\neq\text{prediction}
$$

$$
\text{Narrative intelligibility}\neq\text{evidential confirmation}.
$$

The essay should preserve the methodological symmetry argument, the method of exclusion, means–end fit, the localized character of miracle evidence, the free-will/informed-consent argument, religious pluralism, the Bayesian treatment of ad hoc auxiliaries, and Intelligent Design as integral applications of the same general explanatory problem.
