Part IThe Explanatory Problem

I. Introduction: Changing the Explanatory Question

Discussions about religion are usually organized around individual truth claims. Did God reveal the Torah? Was Jesus resurrected? Did Muhammad receive revelation? Are particular scriptures divinely inspired? Does a given miracle report describe an actual supernatural event? These are legitimate questions, but taken one at a time they can obscure a more fundamental explanatory problem. Before asking whether any particular revelation is true, we can ask what explains the existence, distribution, development, and persistence of religious systems in the first place. The object requiring explanation is not merely a proposition such as “God exists,” nor even a single historical tradition such as Christianity. It is a large and highly structured body of observable phenomena: beliefs, sacred texts, rituals, prophetic and mystical experiences, claims of revelation, institutions, sects, doctrinal disputes, conversions, schisms, geographic distributions, historical transformations, supernatural cosmologies, and the social processes through which all of these are transmitted. These features occur not in one religion but across populations of competing and mutually incompatible traditions.

We can represent this explanandum schematically as a multidimensional system:

\[ \mathbf{D} = ( \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{T}, \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{R}, \mathbf{I}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{G}, \mathbf{H}, \mathbf{Q}, \ldots ) \]

where, for example, \(\mathbf{B}\) represents beliefs and doctrines, \(\mathbf{T}\) texts and textual traditions, \(\mathbf{X}\) religious experiences and revelation claims, \(\mathbf{R}\) rituals and practices, \(\mathbf{I}\) institutions and authority structures, \(\mathbf{C}\) communities and sectarian divisions, \(\mathbf{G}\) geographic distributions, \(\mathbf{H}\) historical transformations, and \(\mathbf{Q}\) claims about supernatural entities and realities. Each component is itself structured across multiple dimensions. Religious beliefs, for example, vary across time, geography, population, language, social class, institutions, and neighboring traditions:

\[ \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}(t,g,p,l,i,c,\ldots) \]

The explanatory target is therefore better thought of as a vector of interacting vectors than as a collection of isolated theological propositions. This change of perspective matters because claims of revelation ordinarily enter theological arguments as explanations. A religion exists because God revealed it; a doctrine is believed because God disclosed it; a scripture possesses authority because it derives from a supernatural source. But from the standpoint of comparative explanation, claims of revelation are initially part of the data requiring explanation. Human societies repeatedly produce prophets, visions, divine messages, sacred books, miracle traditions, charismatic founders, and communities convinced that they possess privileged access to supernatural truth. These claims arise across traditions that cannot all be simultaneously correct in their substantive theological assertions.

The prior question is therefore not simply:

Is this revelation genuine?

It is:

What process generates entire populations of claims like these, and what determines their content, distribution, survival, and historical development?

This essay approaches religion as a complex social and historical system. Its central proposal is that religious traditions exhibit systematic spatial, temporal, genealogical, institutional, conceptual, and network dependence. Their forms are shaped by identifiable mechanisms operating at several levels: individual cognition and experience; social learning and interpersonal transmission; institutions and authority structures; political power and demographic change; linguistic and philosophical environments; migration, conquest, and trade; and contact with neighboring traditions. These processes interact dynamically, producing feedback, path dependence, cultural selection, and historical transformation. The argument is not that identifying social causes automatically proves religious claims false. That would be a genetic fallacy. Nor is the argument that any similarity between religions establishes direct borrowing. The more important question is comparative and explanatory. If ordinary cognitive, social, historical, and cultural mechanisms already generate the broad structure of the religious world we observe, what explanatory work remains for a special-revelation hypothesis to perform?

Let \(M\) represent a social-historical generative model of religion and \(H_{R}\) a substantive hypothesis of special revelation. The relevant comparison is not merely whether revelation is compatible with the historical record. Almost any sufficiently flexible theology can be made compatible with cultural mediation, doctrinal development, literary borrowing, geographic inequality, and historical contingency. The stronger question is whether adding revelation improves the explanation:

\[ M \quad \text{versus} \quad M+H_{R} \]

If the social-historical model already predicts much of the observed structure, then the revelatory component must contribute some additional explanatory or predictive information. Otherwise revelation risks becoming an auxiliary metaphysical layer placed over a causal system that is already doing the explanatory work. This issue becomes especially important when the content of religion itself displays historical dependence. Sacred narratives resemble earlier literary forms. Theological problems emerge within inherited intellectual traditions. Conceptions of God change. Accounts of angels, demons, resurrection, judgment, and the end of history develop over time. The philosophical categories later used to define God's nature arise within identifiable intellectual environments. The historical question is therefore not merely why religious communities change around an unchanging revelation, but why purported knowledge of supernatural reality appears to change along with the cultural systems through which it is expressed.

The five historical case studies examined below explore this problem at progressively deeper levels. The birth traditions of Sargon and Moses concern the cultural ecology of sacred narrative. Mesopotamian suffering literature and the Book of Job concern the historical development of theological problem-spaces. The development of YHWH within ancient Israelite religion concerns the changing concept of the deity itself. Iranian religion and Jewish apocalypticism concern the historical construction of the invisible world and the end of history. Finally, Hellenistic Judaism and Christianity concern the philosophical categories through which later Christianity came to describe the metaphysical nature of God.

Taken individually, none of these cases proves that revelation is impossible. That is not their purpose. Their significance is cumulative. Together they allow us to ask whether religion is better understood as a collection of exceptional supernatural disclosures that happen to be deeply embedded in ordinary cultural history, or as the output of a multilevel social system capable of generating, selecting, preserving, and transforming claims of revelation from within. The question that will guide the analysis is therefore not whether theology can be made compatible with the historical evidence. It is more demanding:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What explains the total observable religious system, and what explanatory contribution remains for revelation once its ordinary causal structure is taken into account?} } \]

II. What Must Be Explained: The Broad Evidential Structure of Religion

Before proposing a causal model of religion, it is useful to describe the structure of the phenomenon itself. The relevant evidence is not exhausted by the existence of particular doctrines or scriptures. Religious systems exhibit recurrent patterns across geography, history, institutions, cultural networks, and populations. These patterns form the empirical target that any adequate theory of religion must explain.

At a glance: the evidential structure

Observable pattern What must be explained Main mechanism developed later
Spatial clustering Why religion tracks geography, language, and community Social transmission and network structure
Intergenerational continuity Why children disproportionately inherit local religion Vertical transmission and socialization
Historical change Why doctrines and supernatural concepts vary through time Innovation, selection, and path dependence
Genealogical continuity Why later traditions retain identifiable ancestry Cultural inheritance
Network sensitivity Why contact produces borrowing, hybridization, or polemical differentiation Diffusion and boundary formation
Institutional filtering Why some texts, revelations, and doctrines survive while others disappear Selection and preservation infrastructure
Conceptual dependence Why experiences and revelations are interpreted through local categories Conceptual ecology
Adaptive reinterpretation Why traditions can remain continuous while changing substantially Feedback, reinterpretation, and institutional resilience

Spatial and Intergenerational Dependence

One of the most obvious features is spatial dependence. Religious belief is not distributed randomly across the human population. It clusters strongly by geography, family, language, ethnicity, and surrounding community. A person born into a predominantly Muslim society is much more likely to become Muslim than a person born into a predominantly Hindu, Buddhist, Christian, or secular society. The same applies at finer scales. Particular Christian denominations, forms of Islam, schools of Buddhism, and local religious movements often display clear regional concentrations. This can be represented schematically as:

\[ P(B \mid G_1) \neq P(B \mid G_2) \]

where \(B\) is a religious belief or affiliation and \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) are different geographic or social environments. The point is not merely that people influence one another. It is that religious belief exhibits a pronounced dependence on the local informational environment. What an individual regards as the obvious, sacred, ancient, or divinely revealed description of reality is strongly associated with which tradition is already dominant around that individual. Closely related is intergenerational dependence. Religious identity and belief are frequently inherited through families and communities. Children ordinarily encounter one religious framework long before they encounter its rivals in comparable depth. Sacred stories, rituals, categories of supernatural beings, moral norms, and interpretive habits are acquired through socialization. The resulting causal pathway is familiar:

Causal pattern: parental/community belief → childhood socialization → religious belief

This does not imply that individuals never convert or critically revise inherited beliefs. They clearly do. But conversion itself takes place against a prior background of culturally acquired concepts, and population-level patterns continue to show strong dependence on inherited social environments.

Temporal and Genealogical Dependence

Religion also exhibits profound temporal dependence. The religious concepts available to people at one historical moment differ from those available at another. Ancient Israelite religious literature does not present exactly the same supernatural world as Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic literature. Early Christian writings do not yet articulate the full metaphysical vocabulary of later Christian theology. Concepts such as Satan, resurrection, divine simplicity, purgatory, the Trinity, angelic hierarchies, and detailed eschatological systems all possess histories. In general:

\[ P(B \mid t_1) \neq P(B \mid t_2) \]

even within what later generations may describe as a single continuous tradition. This matters because many religious beliefs purport to describe realities that are themselves independent of history. If angels, demons, resurrection, divine attributes, or postmortem judgment are objective features of supernatural reality, then the historical development of knowledge concerning them requires explanation. Why do particular concepts become available at particular times? Why do earlier stages of a tradition conceptualize the same supposed reality differently? A third major feature is genealogical dependence. Religions have histories of descent. Later systems inherit texts, practices, categories, and institutions from earlier ones. Christianity cannot be understood apart from Second Temple Judaism; Second Temple Judaism cannot be understood apart from earlier Israelite religion and ancient Near Eastern culture. Later Christian theology inherits not only Jewish scriptures but also earlier Christian controversies, Greek philosophical categories, Roman institutions, and centuries of interpretive precedent.

This relationship can be represented as:

\[ R_{t+1} = f(R_t,\ldots) \]

where a later religious state is partly generated from an earlier one. The significance of this is that religious systems display path dependence. Once a text becomes canonical, a ritual becomes institutionalized, or a theological category becomes authoritative, later development takes place inside a constrained conceptual space. Historical accidents can therefore exert durable downstream effects. The canonization of one text rather than another can alter centuries of theology. A translation choice can shape later interpretation. A successful institution can preserve one doctrinal lineage while rival lineages disappear. Early contingent outcomes become structural constraints on later religious development.

Network and Institutional Dependence

Another important feature is network dependence. Religious systems do not evolve in isolation. They exist inside networks of contact created by trade, conquest, migration, imperial administration, intermarriage, translation, missionary activity, and intellectual exchange. We can imagine communities as nodes in a network:

\[ V=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\} \]

with edges:

\[ E=\{e_{ij}\} \]

representing channels of interaction between them. Where contact is strong, opportunities for cultural transmission increase. Religious concepts, literary motifs, ritual practices, philosophical categories, and institutional forms can move along the same social pathways as other forms of information. Thus we would generally expect:

\[ P(\text{transmission}_{ij}) \uparrow \quad \text{as} \quad w_{ij}\uparrow \]

where \(w_{ij}\) represents the strength of interaction between two populations. This implies a further observable pattern: clustering. Religions and religious ideas should often display greater similarity among historically connected populations than among populations that lack such connections. However, network contact does not produce convergence alone. Neighboring traditions can also define themselves against one another. Contact may therefore generate: borrowing, hybridization, polemical rejection, or boundary intensification. A religious community may adopt a neighboring idea, reinterpret it, reject it explicitly, or emphasize an opposing doctrine precisely because the neighboring idea has become salient. This is why influence should not be conceptualized as simple copying. Cultural interaction changes the space of possible religious responses. Religion also exhibits institutional dependence. Beliefs do not survive merely because individuals entertain them. They are preserved through social structures: temples, churches, monasteries, schools, priesthoods, councils, scribal communities, universities, families, and states. These institutions determine, in part, which texts are copied, which interpretations are taught, which rituals become standardized, and which claims are declared orthodox or heretical. A useful abstraction is:

\[ \text{religious innovation} \rightarrow \text{institutional selection} \rightarrow \begin{cases} \text{extinction}\\ \text{marginal survival}\\ \text{sectarian persistence}\\ \text{orthodoxy}\\ \text{canonization} \end{cases} \]

This is especially important because the traditions we observe today are survivorship samples. The historical record contains only a fraction of the religious innovations that were once proposed. Many prophets were forgotten. Many sects disappeared. Many texts were not preserved. Many revelations never became canonical. The religious traditions that survive are therefore the outputs of long processes of selection.

Conceptual Dependence and Revelation Claims

There is also conceptual dependence. Religious experiences are not interpreted in a conceptual vacuum. A dream, vision, auditory experience, overwhelming sense of presence, or mystical state does not intrinsically identify its own metaphysical cause. Interpretation depends on prior expectations and available categories. The same broad class of extraordinary experience may be interpreted as:

  • contact with an ancestor;
  • possession by a spirit;
  • communication from an angel;
  • encounter with a saint;
  • revelation from God;
  • manifestation of a bodhisattva;
  • demonic attack;
  • mystical union.

This suggests:

Causal pattern: experience + conceptual environment → religious interpretation

The content attributed to extraordinary experiences therefore tends to reflect the symbolic and theological resources already available to the experiencer and community. This becomes particularly important when considering claims of revelation. Human societies repeatedly generate individuals who sincerely report contact with supernatural agents, divine speech, visionary journeys, miracles, prophetic insight, or privileged access to transcendent truth. These claims are not confined to one tradition. They arise across Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Hindu traditions, Buddhism, indigenous religions, new religious movements, and countless smaller historical communities. Moreover, the resulting claims often contradict one another. One prophet reports that God is triune. Another tradition denies that God could possibly be triune. One revelation identifies Jesus as divine. Another explicitly denies his divinity. One tradition teaches reincarnation. Another teaches a single earthly life followed by judgment. One claims exclusive divine authorization for a particular scripture; another makes the same kind of claim for a different scripture. This recurrent production of incompatible revelatory claims is itself an empirical phenomenon. The relevant question is therefore not only whether a particular claimant was sincere or whether one particular revelation might be true. A broader explanatory theory must account for the population-level production of such claims:

\[ \mathcal{R} = \{R_1,R_2,R_3,\ldots,R_n\} \]

where members of \(\mathcal{R}\) may be mutually incompatible. This leads to a central systems-level question:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What process generates entire populations of revelatory claims, supernatural ontologies, sacred texts, and communities that regard them as authoritative?} } \]

Adaptive Reinterpretation and the Explanatory Target

Finally, religious systems exhibit adaptive reinterpretation. When political, intellectual, or empirical circumstances change, traditions often reinterpret inherited material rather than simply abandoning it. Military defeat can produce new theologies of judgment and restoration. Exile can transform national religion into portable textual religion. Foreign rule can intensify apocalyptic expectation. Philosophical criticism can encourage more abstract conceptions of deity. Scientific developments can produce reinterpretations of creation narratives. Moral change can lead communities to reread older legal or ethical material. These processes show that religious traditions are not static repositories of propositions. They are adaptive systems capable of maintaining continuity while substantially altering the interpretation of inherited content.

Taken together, these regularities form a striking pattern. Religion displays: spatial dependence + temporal dependence + genealogical dependence + network dependence + institutional dependence + conceptual dependence + path dependence. The key point is not that every religious belief can be predicted perfectly from these variables. Complex social systems contain innovation, randomness, feedback, and individual agency. The point is that religious phenomena display systematic structure along precisely the dimensions that social and cultural processes are known to generate. This gives us a more demanding explanatory target than any isolated revelation claim. An adequate theory must explain why religious beliefs cluster where they do, why they change when they do, why related traditions resemble one another, why contact produces both borrowing and differentiation, why certain claims become canonical while others disappear, why supernatural concepts track conceptual environments, and why populations repeatedly generate incompatible revelations.

The next step is therefore to construct a causal model capable of producing these patterns rather than merely describing them.

Part IIA Generative Model of Religion

III. A Hierarchical Causal Model of Religious Systems

The regularities described in the previous section suggest that religion should be approached not as a collection of isolated beliefs, but as a multilevel causal system. Religious traditions are generated, transmitted, stabilized, and transformed through interactions among individuals, communities, institutions, political structures, and cultural environments. No single level is sufficient by itself. Individual experiences occur within conceptual worlds; institutions select among individual claims; states and empires restructure institutions; and inherited traditions constrain what kinds of innovations are intelligible in the first place. A useful starting point is to distinguish three broad levels of analysis: micro, meso, and macro. These levels should not be understood as independent layers. They are dynamically coupled. Effects at one level can propagate upward or downward, and feedback across levels can stabilize or transform the entire system.

Micro Level: Experience, Cognition, and Interpretation

At the micro level, the relevant processes are those occurring in individual cognition and experience. These include perception, memory, dreams, visions, emotional salience, agency attribution, testimony, conformity, prestige bias, intuitive moral reasoning, charismatic authority, and individual acts of interpretation or innovation. Religious experience is especially important because it is often treated as if it arrived already carrying a theological interpretation. But an experience and its interpretation are not identical. A person may undergo an unusual dream, auditory event, powerful sense of presence, trance, mystical experience, or overwhelming emotional state. What that experience is taken to mean depends partly on prior expectations and available concepts. Schematically:

Causal pattern: experience + prior expectations + conceptual repertoire → religious interpretation

A person raised within a Christian framework may interpret an experience through categories such as angel, demon, saint, Holy Spirit, or divine calling. A person embedded in a different religious environment may interpret a phenomenologically similar event through categories such as ancestor, spirit, deity, bodhisattva, possession, or karmic revelation. The relevant point is not that all such experiences are reducible to one psychological cause. The point is that their religious meaning is mediated by culturally available interpretive resources. The same applies to doctrinal innovation. Individuals rarely construct theological systems from nothing. They inherit languages, scriptures, metaphors, ritual practices, institutional expectations, and conceptual distinctions. Innovation therefore usually has the form of recombination:

\[ \text{new religious claim} = f( \text{inherited concepts}, \text{experience}, \text{social context}, \text{individual innovation} ) \]

The individual is thus neither a passive transmitter nor a completely unconstrained originator. Religious creativity takes place within a structured conceptual space.

Meso Level: Institutions and Selection

At the meso level, communities and institutions perform a different function. They determine which individual claims are amplified, preserved, modified, rejected, or forgotten. Relevant structures include families, congregations, monasteries, churches, temples, priesthoods, scribal schools, theological academies, councils, sects, denominations, and networks of teachers and students. These institutions act as selection environments. An individual may produce a novel interpretation, revelation, ritual, or doctrinal claim, but only some innovations acquire durable social form. A useful abstraction is:

\[ \text{religious innovation} \rightarrow \text{social and institutional selection} \rightarrow \begin{cases} \text{extinction}\\ \text{marginal persistence}\\ \text{sect formation}\\ \text{institutionalization}\\ \text{canonization} \end{cases} \]

This distinction between production and selection is crucial. Religious systems may generate large numbers of prophetic claims, interpretations, visionary experiences, and textual traditions. The fact that only a small fraction survive does not mean that the surviving ones were uniquely generated. It may mean that they were uniquely selected, preserved, or institutionally reinforced. Canon formation is an obvious example. A text becomes culturally consequential not merely because it was written, but because communities copy it, teach it, interpret it, liturgically use it, and eventually treat it as authoritative. The causal history of a canonical text therefore includes both its production and the institutional processes that determine its survival. The meso level also generates normative boundaries. Communities distinguish orthodoxy from heresy, legitimate prophecy from false prophecy, authentic scripture from apocrypha, proper ritual from deviance, and authorized teachers from outsiders. Once these distinctions are institutionalized, they constrain future development. A community's later state therefore depends partly on earlier institutional decisions:

\[ R_{t+1} = f(R_t,I_t,\ldots) \]

where \(I_t\) represents the institutional structure at time \(t\). This creates path dependence. Once one doctrinal tradition becomes authoritative, future debates occur within a narrower range of acceptable possibilities. What began as a contingent historical victory can later appear timeless or inevitable because the alternatives have disappeared from common awareness.

Macro Level: Politics, Empire, and Demography

At the macro level, the relevant variables are large-scale historical and social structures. These include states, empires, war, conquest, migration, diaspora, demographic change, trade, literacy, communication technologies, economic organization, legal regimes, and political patronage. Macro-level events can radically reshape religious systems. Conquest may destroy temples or displace priesthoods. Exile may weaken territorially localized religious practices while increasing the importance of portable texts. Empire may connect previously separated religious communities. Trade may create routes for the movement of myths, rituals, and philosophical concepts. State patronage may transform a marginal sect into an institutional majority. Changes in literacy or communication technology may alter who can access religious texts and who controls interpretation. These are not peripheral effects. They alter the structural conditions within which religious beliefs are reproduced. A simplified relation might be written as:

Causal pattern: macro historical change → institutional restructuring → changes in transmission and doctrine

The important point is that macro-level processes can change both the content and the distribution of religion without requiring changes in the truth value of any supernatural proposition. A religious system can therefore shift dramatically because the environment through which it reproduces itself has changed.

Conceptual Ecology

Running across all three levels is what can be called a conceptual ecology. Let \(C_t\) represent the set of concepts, distinctions, metaphors, philosophical categories, scripts, and supernatural types available within a population at time \(t\). This variable is indispensable because religious thought is not generated in a conceptual vacuum. At any historical moment, communities possess a limited but evolving repertoire of ways to describe reality. The contents of \(C_t\) might include:

  • categories of divine beings;
  • ideas about the soul;
  • metaphysical distinctions;
  • models of causation;
  • inherited myths;
  • scriptural interpretations;
  • moral concepts;
  • theories of kingship;
  • cosmologies;
  • philosophical vocabularies.

Conceptual ecology constrains both individual experience and institutional theology. At the micro level:

\[ C_t \rightarrow \text{interpretation of experience} \]

At the meso level:

\[ C_t \rightarrow \text{available doctrinal formulations} \]

At the macro level, historical contact can alter \(C_t\) itself. For example:

\[ \text{cultural contact} \rightarrow C_t' \rightarrow \text{new theological possibilities} \]

This will become especially important in the later case studies. The interaction between Israelite religion and Mesopotamian literary culture, Jewish apocalypticism and Iranian religious concepts, or Christianity and Greek philosophy can be understood partly as changes in conceptual ecology.

Putting these components together, we can represent religious development schematically as:

\[ R_{t+1} = F( R_t, C_t, I_t, P_t, N_t, E_t, D_t, \epsilon_t ) \]

where:

  • \(R_t\) is the inherited religious state;
  • \(C_t\) is the conceptual ecology;
  • \(I_t\) is the institutional structure;
  • \(P_t\) is the political and macro-historical environment;
  • \(N_t\) is the social and cultural network;
  • \(E_t\) represents individual experiences and innovations;
  • \(D_t\) represents demographic conditions;
  • \(\epsilon_t\) represents contingency, stochastic variation, and events not captured by the simplified model.

This is not intended as a finished quantitative theory. It is a structural causal schema: a way of identifying the major classes of variables that plausibly generate religious change. The model is also recursive. The output of one period becomes part of the input to the next:

\[ R_t \rightarrow R_{t+1} \rightarrow R_{t+2} \]

but each transition takes place under new institutional, political, demographic, and conceptual conditions. Thus religious history is neither pure continuity nor unconstrained invention. It is better understood as structured transformation. The same framework also allows causation to run in multiple directions. Religion can be affected by politics, but religion can also affect politics. Institutions shape beliefs, but beliefs can motivate institutions. Revelatory claims can generate communities, while communities can retrospectively authenticate revelatory claims. So the system contains feedback:

Causal pattern: belief ↔︎ institution ↔︎ identity ↔︎ transmission

This is why simple linear explanations are often inadequate. A doctrine may survive because an institution preserves it; the institution may survive because the doctrine legitimates it; both may persist because the community derives identity from their mutual reinforcement. The explanatory power of this framework lies partly in the fact that its mechanisms are not unique to religion. Social learning, prestige effects, institutional selection, network diffusion, path dependence, political patronage, conceptual inheritance, and feedback are observed throughout human culture. That gives the model a significant methodological advantage: its core mechanisms are independently motivated. They are not introduced solely to explain religious history.

The central question is therefore whether a combination of these mechanisms can generate the patterns described in the previous section: spatial clustering, historical development, genealogical continuity, network-sensitive borrowing and differentiation, institutional selection, recurrent revelation claims, and conceptual change in supernatural belief. If so, religion can be approached as a generative social system rather than merely as a sequence of isolated theological claims. The next step is to make one component of this model more explicit: the role of networks. Religious communities do not simply inherit ideas vertically from the past. They also exchange, transform, and resist ideas horizontally through contact with neighboring traditions. That network structure helps explain why religious similarities and differences cluster in the historical patterns that they do.

IV. Religion as a Networked Cultural Ecology

Religious traditions do not develop in isolation. They are embedded in networks of contact through which people, texts, practices, symbols, institutions, and ideas move. Trade routes connect populations. Empires incorporate different ethnic and religious groups into common political systems. Conquest relocates peoples and elites. Migration produces diasporas. Translation communities transmit texts across linguistic boundaries. Missionary movements establish new channels of exchange. Philosophical schools, courts, temples, monasteries, and urban centers create nodes in which otherwise separate intellectual traditions interact. This makes network theory a natural way to describe one important dimension of religious development.

Nodes, Edges, and Historical Contact

Let a religious or cultural population be represented by a node:

\[ V=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\} \]

and let an edge between two nodes, \(e_{ij}\), represent some channel of interaction between populations \(v_i\) and \(v_j\). The existence of an edge may indicate trade, migration, political incorporation, intermarriage, bilingualism, intellectual exchange, shared institutions, missionary activity, or textual transmission. The strength of the relationship can be represented by an edge weight: \(w_{ij}\). In a simplified model, stronger and more frequent contact increases opportunities for cultural transmission:

\[ P(\text{transmission}_{ij}) \uparrow \quad \text{as} \quad w_{ij}\uparrow. \]

This does not imply that every idea spreads whenever contact occurs. It means that the probability space changes. Two communities with sustained linguistic, political, or commercial interaction possess more pathways through which religious information can travel than two communities with little or no contact. This produces a straightforward expectation: religious similarity should often exhibit network clustering. If neighboring or historically connected populations repeatedly exchange cultural information, then their religious systems should, on average, share more features than those of populations lacking such contact. The relevant similarity may concern stories, ritual forms, divine names, cosmological concepts, legal traditions, philosophical categories, institutional structures, or modes of religious authority. Schematically:

Causal pattern: greater network proximity → greater opportunity for cultural correlation

The word correlation is important. Similarity alone does not establish the direction of influence, nor does every resemblance require direct borrowing. Populations can inherit material from a common ancestor, develop similar responses to similar environments, or encounter the same third-party tradition. Network analysis therefore does not reduce historical explanation to finding a single source for each religious idea. It instead asks how patterns of resemblance and divergence relate to structures of contact.

Transmission as Transformation

This is especially useful for understanding syncretism. Syncretism is often discussed as though one religion simply imports a foreign doctrine intact. But cultural transmission is rarely so mechanical. Religious systems possess preexisting texts, institutions, social boundaries, concepts, and internal theological constraints. Incoming material is therefore filtered through the receiving system. For two interacting traditions, we might represent the process as:

\[ R_j(t+1) = F( R_j(t), R_i(t), w_{ij}, C_j(t), I_j(t), P_j(t), \ldots ) \]

where \(R_j(t)\) is the receiving tradition, \(R_i(t)\) is a neighboring tradition, \(w_{ij}\) represents contact strength, \(C_j(t)\) is the receiving culture's conceptual ecology, \(I_j(t)\) its institutional structure, and \(P_j(t)\) its political environment. The important implication is that the output is not expected to be a duplicate of the source. Instead:

Causal pattern: transmission + local constraints → transformation

A foreign concept may be reinterpreted through indigenous categories. A story may be retained while its theological meaning is reversed. A deity may be identified with a local deity, subordinated to another deity, or stripped of divine status entirely. A philosophical distinction may enter a tradition but acquire a new doctrinal purpose. A ritual may survive while its original symbolism is forgotten. This means that the absence of exact identity between two traditions is not evidence against influence. Transformation is itself what a cultural model predicts. The Sargon and Moses comparison examined later will provide a useful example. Even if a Sargonic or broader exposed-child tradition influenced the Moses narrative, the resulting story is not a Mesopotamian story with Israelite names substituted into it. Its ideological function changes. The future universal king becomes the future opponent of imperial kingship. Cultural dependence and theological originality can therefore coexist.

The same principle applies at much deeper levels. Iranian ideas about cosmic struggle need not have entered Judaism in their original dualistic form. Greek philosophical concepts need not have entered Christianity unchanged. What matters is whether contact alters the receiving tradition's conceptual space. We can represent this as:

\[ C_j(t+1) = C_j(t) + \Delta C_{ij}, \]

where \(\Delta C_{ij}\) represents concepts made available through contact with another cultural system. Once introduced, those concepts can participate in later combinations that would previously have been unavailable. Network interaction therefore changes not merely what a population believes, but what it becomes possible for that population to think. This creates historical option value. A community that acquires new conceptual resources can formulate distinctions, reinterpret older texts, or generate theological syntheses that earlier members of the same tradition could not have formulated in the same way. The Hellenistic transformation of Jewish and Christian thought is an especially important later example. Greek linguistic and philosophical environments did not merely transmit foreign propositions into Christianity. They expanded the conceptual repertoire through which Jewish scriptures and Christian claims could be interpreted. Categories such as Logos, substance, essence, incorporeality, intelligible reality, and metaphysical simplicity became part of an increasingly sophisticated theological vocabulary.

Contact, Competition, and Boundary Formation

A network model also helps explain why cultural contact does not always produce convergence. Religious systems are not merely information-processing networks; they are also systems of social identity. Communities often define themselves in relation to neighboring groups. The presence of a competing tradition can therefore increase the salience of doctrinal boundaries. Thus:

\[ \text{contact} \rightarrow \begin{cases} \text{borrowing}\\ \text{hybridization}\\ \text{reinterpretation}\\ \text{polemical rejection}\\ \text{boundary intensification} \end{cases} \]

This is a crucial addition to any model of religious influence. Suppose two traditions come into sustained contact. Some beliefs may converge because concepts are exchanged. Other beliefs may diverge precisely because each group emphasizes whatever most clearly distinguishes it from the other. Religious competition can therefore produce both similarity and anti-similarity. A community may explicitly reject a neighboring doctrine only after that doctrine becomes culturally salient enough to require rejection. Polemic itself can therefore be evidence of contact. This means that influence can occur negatively. A tradition can be changed by another tradition without adopting its beliefs. For example:

Causal pattern: foreign concept → local rejection → sharpened indigenous doctrine

In such a case, the neighboring tradition becomes part of the causal explanation for why a doctrine acquired its later form even though the foreign doctrine itself was rejected. This possibility will matter when considering texts such as Isaiah 45. Whether or not the passage is directly responding to Iranian dualism, its historical setting illustrates a general principle: cultural interaction can generate theological clarification through opposition as well as borrowing. Network structures can also produce clusters within larger religious traditions. Christianity provides an obvious example. Christian communities do not vary randomly. Shared language, political jurisdiction, missionary history, institutional lineage, migration, and geographic proximity help explain why particular theological and liturgical traditions cluster. One can imagine a network in which nodes correspond to Christian communities and edges represent:

  • episcopal relationships;
  • correspondence;
  • shared liturgical language;
  • political administration;
  • missionary descent;
  • monastic networks;
  • theological education;
  • migration.

Doctrinal disputes then diffuse through these networks rather than through an abstract universal “Christianity.” This helps explain why a single revelatory source does not generate a single uniform religious outcome. Even if one granted a common revelation at the beginning, its later trajectory would still depend upon network structure:

Causal pattern: shared source + different network positions + different institutions + different conceptual ecologies → divergent religious outcomes

The same logic applies to Judaism, Islam, Buddhism, Hindu traditions, and new religious movements.

Centrality and Diffusion Infrastructure

Network position can also affect which innovations become influential. A theological idea arising in an isolated community may disappear even if it is intellectually sophisticated. A similar idea arising in a major political, commercial, or educational center may spread widely because the node possesses greater connectivity. Thus cultural success depends not only on the intrinsic content of an idea but on the topology through which it propagates. This introduces concepts analogous to centrality. A highly connected node can disproportionately influence surrounding communities:

Causal pattern: high network centrality → high potential cultural reach

Historically, cities such as Babylon, Alexandria, Antioch, Rome, Constantinople, and later major Islamic and Christian intellectual centers mattered partly because they were not simply geographic locations. They were dense nodes connecting populations, texts, political authority, languages, and educational institutions. Religious diffusion should therefore be expected to follow infrastructure. Ideas travel where people and texts can travel. This gives the social model another potentially testable implication: religious diffusion ~ communication network structure. If a doctrine spreads largely through ordinary cultural mechanisms, its historical distribution should bear traces of routes of communication, political boundaries, migration patterns, and institutional affiliation. Conversely, a revelation process operating independently of cultural transmission might be expected, at least under some formulations, to weaken those dependencies. This does not mean that revelation would necessarily produce identical beliefs everywhere. A sophisticated theology could propose that God intentionally works through historical networks. But once again the explanatory question shifts: if the distribution of religious information follows ordinary social networks in the same way as other cultural information, what distinctive empirical contribution is being made by the revelatory variable?

Horizontal and Vertical Transmission

Network theory also clarifies an important relationship between horizontal and vertical transmission. Vertical transmission occurs across generations:

\[ R_t \rightarrow R_{t+1}. \]

Horizontal transmission occurs between contemporaneous communities:

\[ R_i(t) \leftrightarrow R_j(t). \]

Real religious history combines both. A tradition at time \(t+1\) inherits material from its own past while simultaneously incorporating or resisting information from neighboring systems:

\[ R_i(t+1) = F( R_i(t), R_j(t), R_k(t), \ldots ). \]

This produces a cultural genealogy that is not a simple branching tree. It is better represented as a network with both descent and reticulation. Traditions split, merge, borrow, reform, hybridize, and reconnect. Christianity descends from Judaism but also develops through Greek and Roman cultural environments. Later Christian traditions inherit earlier Christianity while interacting with new philosophical, political, and religious systems. The same networked structure recurs across religious history. This is why metaphors of pure religious essences are often misleading. There is rarely a timeless, uncontaminated religious system moving through history while merely acquiring superficial external decoration. Historical traditions are constituted partly through the interactions that transform them. The network model therefore makes a more general prediction:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Religious resemblance and divergence should be structured by historical connectivity.} } \]

This claim is broader than any single argument about borrowing. It predicts that religious development should display clustering, diffusion, hybridization, local transformation, and polemical differentiation in ways that track actual channels of human contact. The historical case studies later in this essay can therefore be understood not merely as isolated examples of foreign influence but as instances of a more general process. Mesopotamian, Israelite, Iranian, Jewish, Greek, and Christian traditions occupied changing positions within large cultural networks. Their interaction altered the conceptual resources available to subsequent communities. Religious systems, on this view, behave as components of a larger cultural ecology. They compete, exchange information, adapt to one another, occupy institutional niches, and alter the environment in which other traditions develop.

The next section adds another layer to this picture. Network transmission explains how information moves between communities, but it does not by itself explain why some beliefs become stable, why some institutions persist, or how religious systems reinforce their own reproduction. For that we need to examine feedback loops, selection, emergence, and path dependence.

V. Feedback, Selection, Emergence, and Path Dependence

Network structure helps explain how religious ideas move between communities, but transmission alone does not explain why some religious systems become stable, why particular doctrines survive while others disappear, or why historical accidents can shape theological development centuries later. To explain those features, the causal model must include feedback loops, selection processes, emergence, and path dependence. These mechanisms are especially important because religious traditions are not static repositories of inherited information. They are self-reinforcing systems. Beliefs generate institutions; institutions preserve beliefs. Rituals intensify group identity; stronger group identity increases ritual participation. Sacred texts legitimate authorities; authorities determine which texts are treated as sacred. A revelation claim can generate a community, while the later existence of that community becomes evidence, within the tradition, for the authority of the original revelation.

The causal structure is therefore frequently recursive. A simple example is:

Causal pattern: belief → institution → education → belief transmission

Once an institution develops around a doctrine, it can reproduce the doctrine more reliably than unaffiliated individuals could. It can create schools, train specialists, establish liturgies, copy texts, discipline dissent, and socialize children into a shared conceptual framework. The doctrine therefore becomes increasingly stable not merely because individuals continue to find it persuasive, but because a social infrastructure exists to preserve it. This creates a positive feedback loop:

\[ B_t \rightarrow I_t \rightarrow B_{t+1} \rightarrow I_{t+1}. \]

The stronger the institution becomes, the more effectively it can reproduce the belief system that legitimates the institution. And the more widely the belief system is accepted, the more authority the institution may acquire. Religious systems can therefore become self-reinforcing. This does not mean that institutions consciously manipulate believers or that adherents are insincere. Feedback does not require deception. Individuals can sincerely believe doctrines, sincerely establish institutions to preserve them, and sincerely interpret the institutional durability of the tradition as evidence of its truth. The resulting causal loop exists regardless of anyone's intentions.

Feedback and Self-Reinforcement

A similar process can occur with ritual. Suppose participation in a ritual increases social cohesion:

Causal pattern: ritual participation → group cohesion

Greater cohesion may then increase commitment to the ritual:

Causal pattern: group cohesion → ritual participation

Together:

Causal pattern: ritual ↔︎ cohesion

Over time, the ritual becomes increasingly central to group identity. Its persistence may then be interpreted theologically as evidence that it was divinely instituted, even though its social durability can also be explained through the reinforcing relationship between ritual and group cohesion. The same general structure applies to sacred texts. A community treats a text as authoritative. Because it is authoritative, the community copies it, teaches it, translates it, cites it, constructs institutions around it, and preserves it during periods of disruption. Because it survives and becomes culturally central, later generations encounter it as an ancient and apparently uniquely enduring source of wisdom. Thus:

Causal pattern: authority → preservation → cultural centrality → perceived authority

Again, the loop does not establish that the text is false. It explains why longevity and cultural influence cannot automatically be treated as independent evidence of divine origin when the tradition itself contains mechanisms that generate longevity and influence. This point generalizes to the survival of religions. A successful religious system may contain doctrines and practices that increase the probability of its own reproduction. These can include:

  • strong norms of intergenerational instruction;
  • missionary obligations;
  • communal ritual;
  • costly commitment signals;
  • sanctions against apostasy;
  • prestige for religious specialists;
  • institutions for textual preservation;
  • theological explanations for persecution;
  • mechanisms for resolving internal disputes;
  • promises or threats concerning the afterlife.

Such features may create what can be described as cultural fitness. This term does not imply biological fitness in a strict evolutionary sense, nor does it imply that successful doctrines are false. It simply refers to the fact that some religious configurations reproduce themselves more effectively than others. A rough relation might be:

\[ P(R_{t+1}\mid R_t) = f( \text{transmission efficiency}, \text{institutional stability}, \text{social incentives}, \text{demographic reproduction}, \text{network reach}, \ldots ). \]

Religious persistence is therefore partly a problem of cultural reproduction.

Selection and Survivorship

This leads naturally to selection. At any historical moment, far more religious ideas may be generated than survive. Individuals produce interpretations, prophetic claims, visions, rituals, sectarian innovations, theological syntheses, and new texts. Most disappear. The historical record we inherit is therefore filtered. We observe that surviving religious forms are not identical to all religious forms ever generated. Instead:

Causal pattern: production → selection → historical survival

Selection can occur through many mechanisms. An innovation may spread because it is emotionally compelling. Another may spread because it is supported by political authority. Another may persist because it is easier to teach. Another may survive because it becomes associated with a powerful institution. Another may be preserved because it provides a useful identity marker against neighboring groups. Another may disappear because its adherents are geographically isolated, politically defeated, demographically absorbed, or simply unlucky in textual preservation. This introduces a major interpretive danger: survivorship bias. Later generations often encounter successful traditions without seeing the much larger population of failed competitors from which they emerged. The resulting historical picture can create an illusion of inevitability. A doctrine that eventually becomes orthodox can appear to have been the natural or obvious interpretation from the beginning. But during the period in which it emerged, it may have competed with multiple alternatives. Similarly, a canonical collection can appear uniquely coherent after alternative texts and communities have disappeared. The relevant causal question is not merely:

Why did this doctrine exist?

It is also:

Why did this doctrine survive while others did not?

This distinction is essential when evaluating revelation claims. Suppose a religious tradition points to the survival and influence of a particular revelation as evidence of divine preservation. A social model asks whether survival can instead be explained by identifiable selection mechanisms:

Causal pattern: institutional authority + political support + demographic success + textual reproduction + network centrality → survival

If these mechanisms are sufficient, then survival itself contributes less evidential weight to the supernatural hypothesis. Selection also interacts with retrospective interpretation. Once a tradition has survived, later members often reconstruct its history from the standpoint of the successful outcome. Earlier ambiguities are interpreted as anticipations of later orthodoxy. Defeated alternatives are described as deviations. Contingent institutional victories may be narrated as providential necessity. The causal sequence may look like:

Causal pattern: contingent historical outcome → institutional dominance → retrospective theological interpretation

The theology then represents the outcome as if it were the inevitable expression of an underlying divine plan. This does not show that providence is false. But it does identify a natural mechanism by which historical contingency can later be experienced as providential inevitability.

Emergence

These processes contribute to emergence. An emergent property is a large-scale feature of a system that arises from interactions among lower-level components but is not reducible to the intentions of any single participant. Religious orthodoxy is a good example. No single early participant may intend to create the final doctrinal system that later defines a tradition. Individual theologians respond to local controversies. Bishops negotiate institutional disputes. Communities preserve different texts. Councils settle particular conflicts. Political authorities intervene for their own reasons. Later generations reinterpret earlier decisions. Over time, these interactions can generate something that appears at the macro level as: orthodoxy. Similarly, phenomena such as:

  • canon;
  • denomination;
  • religious identity;
  • priestly class;
  • doctrinal tradition;
  • sacred geography;
  • liturgical calendar;

can emerge from many interacting local processes. This is important because complex outcomes need not require centralized design. A religious system may exhibit order without having been planned as a whole. The emergence of order can be represented schematically as:

\[ \{\text{many local interactions}\} \rightarrow \text{stable macro-level structure}. \]

The same principle is familiar outside religion. Languages possess grammatical order even though no single speaker designed the language. Markets exhibit large-scale structure through decentralized activity. Scientific disciplines develop institutions and paradigms through the interaction of many researchers. Cultural traditions acquire stable conventions without central authorship. Religion can be studied similarly. This does not mean that intentional actors are unimportant. Prophets, rulers, theologians, reformers, and institutions can have enormous causal influence. The point is that the final system generally exceeds the intentions of any one actor. Christianity, for example, cannot plausibly be treated as the deliberate construction of one individual. Its later form emerged from interactions among Jesus movements, Jewish interpretive traditions, Pauline communities, Greek-speaking churches, Roman political structures, theological disputes, councils, monastic networks, missionary activity, and centuries of philosophical reflection.

The macro-level system is an emergent historical product.

Path Dependence and Historical Lock-In

Emergence is closely related to path dependence. Path dependence occurs when earlier states of a system constrain its later possibilities. Once a system moves down one historical path, alternative trajectories may become increasingly difficult to recover. Let the state of a religious system at time \(t\) be: \(R_t\). Then the next state is not generated independently:

\[ R_{t+1} = F(R_t,\ldots). \]

The presence of \(R_t\) inside the function matters. If an early community canonizes text \(A\) rather than text \(B\), subsequent theology develops around \(A\). If one doctrinal vocabulary becomes authoritative, later debates inherit its distinctions. If a particular institutional hierarchy becomes dominant, later reforms must respond to that structure. If one translation becomes liturgically central, later readers inherit its wording. Thus:

Causal pattern: small early difference → different later state space

The effect can compound over time. Suppose:

\[ R_0 \rightarrow R_1 \rightarrow R_2 \rightarrow R_3. \]

A contingent feature introduced at \(R_1\) may shape all subsequent states. By \(R_3\), that feature may appear deeply intrinsic to the tradition even though its origin was historically contingent. This creates what might be called historical lock-in. Once institutions, texts, and practices accumulate around a particular outcome, reversing it becomes increasingly costly. For example:

Causal pattern: doctrine → liturgical incorporation → institutional reinforcement → educational reproduction → identity formation

At the end of this sequence, changing the doctrine threatens much more than an abstract proposition. It threatens institutions, rituals, identities, and social relationships. The system therefore acquires inertia. Path dependence also helps explain why related traditions can diverge dramatically. Two communities may begin from similar initial conditions but experience different political environments, linguistic transitions, institutional decisions, or network contacts. Then:

\[ R_A(t_0) \approx R_B(t_0) \]

but after different historical sequences:

\[ R_A(t_n) \not\approx R_B(t_n). \]

The divergence need not reflect access to different supernatural realities. It may arise because the systems traveled through different historical trajectories. This is especially relevant when examining Christian denominational development, Jewish sectarian history, or the divergence of religious traditions after migration and political separation. Path dependence also means that contemporary theological systems cannot always be understood merely by examining their current doctrines. Their structure may encode centuries of earlier conflicts. A distinction that now appears metaphysically fundamental may originally have emerged as a solution to a specific historical controversy. A ritual that now appears timeless may preserve the residue of an earlier social environment. A doctrine may persist partly because other commitments have been built around it. Religion therefore contains historical memory in structural form. This idea can be expressed as:

\[ R_t = F( R_{t-1}, R_{t-2}, \ldots, R_0, \text{current conditions} ). \]

The present state contains traces of earlier states. These mechanisms—feedback, selection, emergence, and path dependence—also help explain why religious traditions can appear both stable and adaptable. Feedback generates continuity. Selection filters variation. Path dependence preserves historical structure. But innovation, network contact, and changing environments introduce new variation. The system therefore occupies an intermediate position between rigidity and constant reinvention. We might summarize: religious evolution = variation + selection + inheritance + feedback + environmental interaction. This formulation should not be interpreted as claiming that cultural evolution is identical to biological evolution. The mechanisms differ in important ways. Cultural traits can be transmitted horizontally, intentionally modified, consciously rejected, and acquired from multiple sources. Nevertheless, the evolutionary analogy is useful because it highlights cumulative change under conditions of differential transmission and retention.

The Expanded Generative Model

The overall causal model developed so far can now be expanded:

\[ R_{t+1} = F( R_t, C_t, I_t, P_t, N_t, E_t, D_t, S_t, \Phi_t, \epsilon_t ) \]

where \(S_t\) represents selection pressures and \(\Phi_t\) represents feedback structures. At this stage, the model can account in principle for several otherwise puzzling features of religious history:

  • why religions remain geographically clustered;
  • why traditions inherit earlier conceptual structures;
  • why contact produces both borrowing and opposition;
  • why some revelations become canonical while others disappear;
  • why early institutional decisions have long-term consequences;
  • why doctrine can become increasingly elaborate;
  • why traditions maintain continuity while changing substantially;
  • and why successful historical outcomes can later appear inevitable or providential.

Most importantly, these mechanisms provide a framework for interpreting the historical case studies that follow. The relevant question will not be whether one can find an isolated similarity between two texts or doctrines. It will be whether the observed transformations behave in the manner predicted by a networked, path-dependent cultural system. The first case begins at the level of sacred narrative. The birth legend of Sargon and the biblical infancy story of Moses provide a useful test of how inherited literary motifs can be transmitted, transformed, and given new ideological functions within a different religious system.

VI. Case Study One: Sargon and Moses — Narrative Forms in a Cultural Ecology

The first case concerns one of the most frequently discussed literary parallels between the Hebrew Bible and the wider ancient Near East: the birth of Moses in Exodus 2 and the Mesopotamian Birth Legend of Sargon of Akkad. The comparison is useful precisely because it illustrates both the strength and the limitations of arguments from literary resemblance. The evidence does not require the conclusion that the author of Exodus directly copied a particular Sargon tablet. What it does show is that biblical sacred narrative participates in a recognizable ancient Near Eastern literary environment in which extraordinary figures could be represented through inherited narrative motifs.

Historical Setting and Chronology

Sargon of Akkad was a historical Mesopotamian ruler who reigned in the third millennium BCE, conventionally around 2334–2279 BCE. The literary text generally called the Birth Legend of Sargon, however, is much later than Sargon himself. The principal manuscript, British Museum K.3401, comes from the Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh and is written in Neo-Assyrian script. The British Museum classifies it simply as a Neo-Assyrian fragment of a legend concerning Sargon. This chronological distinction is important. The existence of a historical Sargon in the twenty-fourth or twenty-third century BCE does not establish that the surviving birth narrative was composed in his lifetime. Joan Goodnick Westenholz's standard edition places the Birth Legend within the much broader corpus of later traditions concerning the kings of Akkad, while other Sargonic legends are demonstrably attested in substantially earlier Old Assyrian contexts. Westenholz has argued that at least some Sargonic literature reflects written improvisation based on older oral traditions. The precise age of the particular Birth Legend therefore remains a historical problem rather than something that can simply be inferred from the age of its protagonist.

Some scholars have argued for a much later composition of the surviving form. Thomas Römer, for example, maintains that the Birth Legend was written in the late eighth-century BCE environment of Sargon II and points to Neo-Assyrian orthography and idiom; on this reconstruction, an author of Exodus could have adapted Neo-Assyrian royal imagery as a kind of counter-history. This is a substantive scholarly proposal, not an uncontested datum, and the broader Sargonic tradition clearly predates the surviving Neo-Assyrian manuscript.

The Narrative Parallel

The legend itself presents Sargon speaking autobiographically. His mother is a priestess; his father is unknown to him. His mother conceives him and gives birth secretly. She places the infant in a basket made of reeds, seals it with bitumen, and entrusts it to the river. The Euphrates carries the child to Aqqi, a drawer of water, who rescues him, adopts him, and raises him. Sargon becomes a gardener, receives the favor of the goddess Ishtar, and eventually becomes king. A University of Chicago treatment of ancient Near Eastern kingship interprets the narrative precisely in terms of the discrepancy between Sargon's humble beginnings and his eventual royal status: his extraordinary rise becomes evidence of divine election. The basic narrative movement is therefore:

Causal pattern: obscure or problematic birth → exposure → water ordeal → rescue and adoption → divine favor → extraordinary destiny

Exodus 2 contains an unusually similar concentration of motifs. Pharaoh has ordered Hebrew male infants killed. Moses' mother initially hides her son, but when she can no longer conceal him she places him in a papyrus container, coats it with bitumen and pitch, and places it among the reeds beside the Nile. His sister watches from a distance. Pharaoh's daughter discovers him, recognizes that he is a Hebrew child, and adopts him. Through the intervention of Moses' sister, his biological mother is then hired to nurse him.

Similarities, Differences, and Ideological Inversion

The similarities are sufficiently specific to have generated scholarly comparison for more than a century. Römer, for example, notes that both infants are exposed by their mothers, placed in baskets associated with a river, found and adopted, and connected in different ways with priestly and royal status. Cambridge's methodological literature on Exodus likewise treats Brian Lewis's The Sargon Legend and Brevard Childs's study of Moses' birth as standard points of reference for the comparison. The parallel is stronger than a generic observation that both heroes had unusual childhoods. Particularly notable is the conjunction of the infant, a vegetal container, bituminous waterproofing, water exposure, rescue, adoption, and later extraordinary status. Yet the differences are equally important.

Sargon's father is unknown; Moses is explicitly situated within a Levite family. Sargon's mother relinquishes him in secret; Moses' mother acts under an external death threat created by Pharaoh. Sargon is carried by the Euphrates to a humble water-drawer; Moses is placed among Nile reeds and found by Pharaoh's own daughter. Sargon's biological family disappears from the narrative; Moses' sister remains nearby, and Moses' own mother is brought back into the story as his nurse. Sargon grows into a conquering king. Moses never becomes king at all. Indeed, the political function of the two stories may move in opposite directions. The Sargon legend helps explain how a person without an ordinary royal pedigree could nevertheless possess legitimate kingship. His extraordinary survival and subsequent favor from Ishtar retrospectively authenticate his rule. The story converts obscure origin into divine election.

Moses' story places a similarly extraordinary child inside a profoundly different ideological framework. The infant endangered by imperial power is rescued by a member of the imperial household and eventually becomes the agent through whom that empire is confronted. Pharaoh's own daughter participates in preserving the person who will later undermine Pharaoh's policy and lead Israel out of Egyptian domination. The motif can therefore be transformed:

Causal pattern: exposed child + Mesopotamian royal ideology → legitimation of king

while in Exodus:

Causal pattern: exposed child + Israelite liberation narrative → opponent of king

This is precisely why “borrowing” is too weak a conceptual category. Cultural transmission frequently involves appropriation, inversion, and recombination rather than replication.

Competing Explanations

Several hypotheses can account for the resemblance. The similarities could arise partly from a widespread exposed-child or heroic-foundling motif found in numerous ancient cultures. The biblical and Mesopotamian stories could both participate in a broader stock of narrative conventions without the author of Exodus knowing this particular Sargon composition. An Israelite or Judean writer could have encountered Sargonic traditions indirectly through oral circulation or scribal culture. Or Exodus 2 could reflect more specific literary knowledge of a Sargon tradition. Some scholars, including Römer, regard direct literary dependence as plausible; others emphasize the broader heroic-foundling pattern and the significant differences between the narratives.

What the Case Shows

The causal model developed in this essay does not require us to choose the strongest version. That is methodologically important. Suppose the historical evidence eventually made direct dependence on the surviving Sargon Birth Legend unlikely. The larger argument would remain largely untouched. The observation requiring explanation is that the Moses story is intelligible within an existing ancient Near Eastern narrative ecology. The relevant causal relationship can therefore be stated more conservatively:

Causal pattern: available narrative repertoire + Israelite social and theological context + authorial innovation → Moses birth narrative

This formulation is stronger historically because it does not require an undocumented act of textual copying. It asks instead whether the narrative exhibits the kind of structured dependence expected when storytellers operate inside inherited cultural systems. The social model predicts exactly this kind of outcome. Authors do not normally invent narrative forms ex nihilo. They inherit motifs, genres, symbols, and expectations from earlier and neighboring traditions. They then rearrange them to address new problems and express new identities.

The result can be simultaneously dependent and original, a distinction that matters because discussions of religious influence are often trapped between two false alternatives. Either the biblical account must be wholly unique and therefore independent, or similarity must imply crude plagiarism. Neither follows. Cultural production ordinarily works through: inheritance + selection + recombination + transformation + innovation. The Moses story is therefore useful not because it proves that biblical authors “stole” a pagan myth, but because its literary form behaves exactly as we expect cultural products to behave. The network framework developed earlier makes this particularly intelligible. Israel and Judah were not culturally isolated societies. They existed within political and literary networks connecting Egypt, the Levant, Assyria, Babylonia, and later Persia. Under Neo-Assyrian domination especially, Judean elites encountered imperial institutions, ideology, language, and royal propaganda. A narrative associated with one of the most famous kings in Mesopotamian historical memory therefore belonged to a cultural environment in which transmission was entirely possible even if the exact path cannot be reconstructed.

This also explains why differences do not count straightforwardly against influence. A receiving tradition does not preserve incoming material in chemically pure form. It filters that material through its own narrative needs. Thus:

\[ \text{foreign or inherited motif} \xrightarrow{\text{local cultural selection}} \text{transformed motif}. \]

Moses' Levite identity, Pharaoh's decree, the actions of the women, the Egyptian princess, and the later liberation narrative all integrate the exposed-child motif into specifically Israelite concerns. The resulting story does more than tell readers that Moses survived infancy. It explains his simultaneous relationship to Israel and Egypt, anticipates the larger conflict with Pharaoh, and portrays the future deliverer as surviving the very system designed to eliminate Hebrew male children. A social-historical model therefore does not predict that sacred literature will merely duplicate surrounding mythology. It predicts that inherited cultural material will be adapted to new ideological environments.

Implications for Revelation

This observation also has an important implication for claims of revelatory uniqueness. A theological response could reasonably say that divine revelation need not create entirely unprecedented literary forms. A deity could communicate through the narrative conventions familiar to a historical audience. Nothing about the Sargon parallel logically excludes such a possibility. But that response changes the evidential question. If sacred narrative uses the same processes of inheritance, adaptation, and literary recombination that generate ordinary cultural narrative, then the literary form itself no longer provides strong evidence of a different causal origin.

Under the social-cultural model, the resemblance is expected:

\[ P( \text{culturally embedded sacred narrative} \mid M ) \text{ is high}. \]

A revelation model can accommodate the same fact, but only by allowing revelation to operate through those cultural processes:

\[ R + M \rightarrow \text{culturally embedded sacred narrative}. \]

At this stage, therefore, the Sargon-Moses comparison does not discriminate strongly between a theology of culturally mediated revelation and a purely social account. What it does establish is something more modest but foundational: biblical sacred narrative exhibits no general exemption from the ordinary historical ecology of storytelling. That conclusion becomes more significant when combined with the later cases. If only narrative form displayed historical dependence, accommodation would be an easy response: perhaps revelation simply uses familiar stories. But the next case moves inward from literary form to theological reasoning itself. Mesopotamian literature had been wrestling with undeserved suffering, hidden sin, divine justice, and the apparent failure of moral retribution many centuries before the Book of Job took its surviving form. Job is not merely a biblical story resembling another story. It participates in an older intellectual problem-space.

The causal question therefore deepens from the question, Where do sacred narrative forms come from?, to:

\[ \boxed{ \text{How do the theological problems addressed by revelation themselves arise and develop historically?} } \]

VII. Case Study Two: Mesopotamian Suffering Literature and Job — The Genealogy of Theological Problems

The comparison between Mesopotamian suffering literature and the Book of Job moves the argument to a deeper level. The Sargon-Moses case concerns the cultural inheritance of narrative form. Job concerns something more conceptually important: the historical development of a theological problem itself.

A Mesopotamian Problem-Space

The question of unjust or unexplained suffering did not first appear in Israelite literature. Mesopotamian scribes had been reflecting on divine anger, hidden guilt, undeserved affliction, the apparent prosperity of the wicked, the failure of conventional piety, and the limits of human knowledge for many centuries before the Book of Job reached its surviving form. These reflections appear in several different compositions rather than in a single direct prototype of Job. That distinction is important. It is misleading to speak as though there were one “Babylonian Job” from which the biblical book was copied. What the evidence reveals instead is an extended intellectual tradition within which related problems were repeatedly reformulated. The relevant corpus includes the Sumerian composition conventionally called A Man and His God, the Old Babylonian Akkadian Dialogue Between a Man and His God, the later Akkadian poem Ludlul bēl nēmeqi, and the Babylonian Theodicy. These texts differ substantially in genre, argument, historical setting, and theological conclusion, but together they demonstrate that the relationship among piety, suffering, guilt, and divine justice constituted a longstanding Mesopotamian problem-space. The Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature classifies A Man and His God among Sumerian “pious sufferer” compositions, while the subsequent scholarly tradition has long compared it with Job without treating the two as identical works.

Hidden Guilt and Human Ignorance

The Old Babylonian Dialogue Between a Man and His God, preserved on Louvre tablet AO 4462, is especially useful because of its antiquity. It belongs to the later Old Babylonian period, around the seventeenth century BCE. The sufferer complains bitterly about his condition and searches for some offense that could explain the withdrawal of divine favor. The central problem is not yet quite the one posed by Job. The speaker does not straightforwardly insist upon absolute innocence. Rather, he cannot identify the sin that would justify what has happened to him. Its predicament can be represented as:

Causal pattern: suffering → presumed divine displeasure → search for unknown offense

That is already an important conceptual development. The traditional moral-retributive model remains largely intact, but it encounters an epistemic problem: if suffering is punishment, what happens when the sufferer cannot discover the crime? The theological tension therefore arises from a mismatch between two beliefs: the gods govern human fortunes justly, yet the sufferer cannot identify conduct warranting his condition. The resulting literature attempts to preserve both propositions by relocating the problem into human ignorance. The sufferer may have sinned without knowing it. The deity's judgment may exceed human understanding. The divine-human relationship may be restored through continued piety even when the precise cause of suffering remains obscure. This is not yet the radical challenge posed by Job, but it establishes an important earlier stage in the conceptual genealogy.

Ludlul: The Crisis of Divine Legibility

A more elaborate development appears in Ludlul bēl nēmeqi, whose title is usually translated “I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom.” The poem recounts the collapse and restoration of a socially prominent man, Šubši-mešrê-Šakkan. He loses social standing, health, divine favor, and the ability to obtain effective ritual or divinatory relief. Eventually Marduk restores him. Alan Lenzi emphasizes that the poem concerns divinely imposed suffering and divinely initiated restoration, and that it reflects the concerns of Mesopotamian scholarly and exorcistic culture. The work is sometimes called the “Babylonian Job,” but that label can be misleading. Its structure differs substantially from Job. It is not built around a sustained argumentative contest between multiple interlocutors. Nor does it finally overturn the theological system within which the sufferer operates. Marduk remains both terrifyingly inscrutable and ultimately worthy of praise.

What Ludlul does, however, is intensify the epistemic crisis. The sufferer has attempted the expected forms of piety. Ritual experts fail to diagnose or remedy his condition. His experience undermines confidence that human beings can reliably infer divine judgment from outward circumstances. Recent scholarship emphasizes that the poem explores precisely the inscrutability of divinely caused misfortune, including suffering that may occur without any clear discernible cause. A characteristic conceptual move can be expressed as: human moral judgment ⇏ divine moral judgment. What appears good to human beings may not appear good to the gods. What appears like undeserved suffering may have causes inaccessible to human understanding. This preserves divine authority while weakening confidence in the human capacity to read divine justice from events. That solution should sound familiar because a related move appears repeatedly in later theology: when observed suffering does not conform to the expected moral structure, the gap can be attributed to the superior and inaccessible perspective of the deity.

The Babylonian Theodicy: Debate Becomes Explicit

The Babylonian Theodicy moves the discussion into an even more recognizable form. Probably composed around the end of the second millennium or beginning of the first millennium BCE, it is structured as a sophisticated dialogue between a sufferer and a friend. The sufferer points to his own distress, unrewarded devotion, social injustice, and the prosperity of impious people. The friend defends continued devotion to the gods and argues that divine wisdom exceeds human comprehension. Oshima describes the work as a discourse on the meaning and benefit of divine worship in which one speaker presents the prosperity of the godless and his own suffering as evidence against the practical value of piety, while the other attempts to defend the traditional religious order.

Formally, this is much closer to Job than the earlier pious-sufferer texts. Carol Newsom identifies the Babylonian Theodicy and the Book of Job as examples of a distinctive “wisdom dialogue” genre: sophisticated literary debates that explore inexplicable suffering or the meaningfulness of life through contrasting speakers rather than merely presenting a single lament. The underlying intellectual structure is now:

Causal pattern: traditional doctrine of divine justice ↔︎ empirical counterevidence from suffering and wicked prosperity

That is a more advanced theological problem than the simple assumption that every sufferer must have unknowingly offended a deity. The sufferer's argument increasingly draws attention to observable reality. The pious suffer. The wicked prosper. Social injustice occurs. Religious devotion does not produce a transparent distribution of rewards. The friend responds by defending a religious interpretation in which the gods remain worthy of worship even when their purposes cannot be understood. This is not identical to the argument in Job, but the resemblance at the level of intellectual structure is substantial.

Job Radicalizes the Problem

The Book of Job then radicalizes this inherited problem. The book's prose frame makes the crucial move before the poetic debate even begins: Job is explicitly introduced as righteous and blameless. His suffering is not presented to the reader as punishment for hidden wrongdoing. The reader knows that the friends' central explanation is false. This changes the logical structure. In the earlier hidden-sin model:

Causal pattern: suffering → probably some undiscovered offense

In Job:

Causal pattern: suffering + explicit righteousness → failure of simple retributive explanation

This is why Job is more than another lament about divine inscrutability. The friends repeatedly attempt to defend a conventional moral structure. Their reasoning is recognizable: God is just Job suffers severely therefore: Job must deserve severe suffering. Job refuses the conclusion. Because he regards his own innocence as known, he presses against the system itself. He demands that God identify his offense. He describes a world in which the wicked can flourish and the innocent can be crushed. The problem is no longer merely ignorance about which sin produced suffering. The proportional connection between moral conduct and worldly fortune has become the object of critique. Modern scholarship frequently identifies this as one of the book's defining features. The Oxford Research Encyclopedia describes Job as perhaps the biblical tradition's most forceful presentation of the problem of a genuinely righteous sufferer, placing the book in tension with more straightforward retributive accounts elsewhere in biblical wisdom and legal traditions.

The book is also relatively late compared with the older Mesopotamian materials. Its exact date remains debated, but most contemporary scholarship places it broadly in the postexilic period. Carol Newsom's Oxford bibliography notes that most scholars consider an early postexilic setting likely, while Edward Greenstein argues specifically from the language of the work for a Persian-period context. Chronologically, then, the relevant sequence is substantial:

Intellectual trajectory: older Mesopotamian pious-sufferer traditions → increasingly sophisticated reflection on unexplained suffering → dialogical critique of conventional piety → Job's radical interrogation of retributive theology.

Genealogy Without Simple Copying

This should not be understood as a proven line of direct textual descent. There is no surviving manuscript trail demonstrating that the author of Job sat with Dialogue Between a Man and His God, Ludlul, and the Babylonian Theodicy arranged before him and synthesized them. Nor is such a hypothesis necessary. The historically significant fact is broader: by the time Job was composed, the problem it addresses belonged to a centuries-old Near Eastern intellectual ecology. The causal claim can therefore remain conservative:

Causal pattern: inherited Near Eastern problem-space + Israelite/Judean wisdom tradition + authorial criticism and innovation → Job

The point is not that Job lacks originality. On the contrary, its originality is part of what the social model predicts. Intellectual traditions do not merely repeat inherited answers. They generate new problems by exposing tensions in earlier solutions. Later thinkers can radicalize, reverse, combine, or reject inherited positions. Thus:

Causal pattern: tradition + criticism + conceptual innovation → greater sophistication

This process is familiar from philosophy, law, science, and political thought. A difficult problem can remain active for centuries because earlier solutions are unsatisfactory. Successive thinkers inherit not merely answers but unresolved tensions. The resulting intellectual tradition can become increasingly sophisticated without any supernatural information entering the system.

What the Case Shows

That observation matters for arguments that appeal to the profundity of Job as evidence of revelation. The book is unquestionably profound. But profundity is not itself evidence of supernatural provenance if we can identify a historically continuous process capable of generating increasingly sophisticated reflection. One would not infer that Greek philosophy required supernatural revelation merely because Plato's treatment of a philosophical problem was more sophisticated than that of his predecessors. Nor would the existence of sophisticated later jurisprudence require a supernatural source because earlier legal systems were less conceptually developed. The relevant explanatory mechanism is cumulative intellectual development. Job can therefore be simultaneously:

  • innovative;
  • intellectually exceptional;
  • literarily sophisticated;
  • culturally inherited.

There is no contradiction between these descriptions. In fact, the historical relationship becomes more interesting precisely because Job does not merely reproduce the older Mesopotamian answers. The Babylonian sufferer often seeks restoration of the relationship with the deity. Ludlul ultimately resolves suffering through Marduk's merciful intervention and the restoration of the afflicted man. The Babylonian Theodicy stages the debate more explicitly but still ends within a framework of continued dependence upon the gods. Job pushes further. Even after God appears, the book does not provide the reader with a straightforward moral explanation for Job's suffering. The divine speeches expand the frame of discussion to the structure of creation and the limits of human comprehension, but God never tells Job that his friends were correct. Indeed, the epilogue explicitly rebukes the friends.

This produces a striking historical development. The problem can move from Why am I suffering? to What unknown offense did I commit?, then to Can human beings understand divine justice?, and eventually to Is the conventional connection between righteousness and fortune adequate at all? The significance of this sequence is not that every text occupies one perfectly discrete stage. The traditions overlap, and different theological positions coexist. The point is that the intellectual landscape itself develops. Religious traditions inherit questions. They inherit failed answers. They inherit conceptual tensions. They inherit forms of argument. And later authors operate inside that inherited problem-space. This is a deeper form of cultural dependence than the reuse of a literary motif. In the Sargon case, one might respond that revelation simply adopted a familiar storytelling form. In the Job case, the culturally inherited element includes the very structure of theological reflection:

Causal pattern: piety ↔︎ suffering ↔︎ justice ↔︎ human epistemic limitation

The social model predicts this naturally. Intellectual communities preserve problems and arguments across generations. Scribal traditions maintain texts. Political and cultural contact transmit ideas. Later authors criticize earlier frameworks. Over time, traditions develop increasingly complex responses. Under such a model:

\[ P( \text{historically layered theological sophistication} \mid M ) \]

is not surprising. A revelatory model can again accommodate the same process. One may say that God progressively reveals deeper truths by allowing human authors to participate in an existing intellectual conversation. But as in the previous case, this shifts rather than resolves the explanatory problem. If the theological insight develops through the same mechanisms that generate philosophy and other human intellectual traditions, then what feature of the development identifies an additional revelatory cause? The question becomes increasingly difficult because the cultural dependence is no longer restricted to narrative packaging. The theological reasoning itself has an intelligible genealogy. This case therefore adds a second layer to the cumulative argument:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Sacred narratives have cultural histories, and sacred theological problems have intellectual histories.} } \]

The next case moves deeper still. The question will no longer concern how a tradition tells stories or reasons about suffering. It will concern the object at the center of the tradition itself:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What happens when the concept of God also exhibits historical development?} } \]

VIII. Case Study Three: The Historical Development of YHWH — The Deity as a Changing Concept

The previous two cases concerned the historical development of sacred narrative and theological reasoning. The present case moves to a more fundamental level. The question is no longer merely how people tell stories about God or how they reason about divine justice. It is how the deity itself is conceptualized. Modern scholarship on ancient Israelite religion has made it increasingly difficult to treat the God of later Jewish and Christian theology as a concept that appears fully formed at the beginning of Israel's history. Instead, the biblical and archaeological evidence points toward a much more complicated development in which YHWH emerges within a broader West Semitic religious world, absorbs characteristics associated with other deities, becomes increasingly identified as Israel's exclusive national god, and is eventually described in forms of universal monotheistic rhetoric that differ substantially from earlier representations.

Methodological Caution: No Simple Linear Evolution

The exact reconstruction remains disputed. There is no single uncontested evolutionary sequence from “polytheism” to “monotheism,” and terms such as polytheism, monolatry, and monotheism can themselves impose modern classificatory schemes on ancient religious practice. Nevertheless, a broad historical conclusion is difficult to avoid: Israelite conceptions of divinity changed. Theodore Lewis's major synthesis of ancient Israelite religion explicitly treats questions concerning the relationship between El and YHWH, the historical origins of YHWH, divine iconography, and the multiple ways in which YHWH was characterized—as warrior, family deity, king, judge, and holy one—as historical problems requiring reconstruction from both biblical and extra-biblical evidence. Mark S. Smith similarly situates early Israelite religion within the larger West Semitic religious environment known especially from Ugaritic literature. Ugaritic texts describe an organized divine world containing El, Baal, Athirat/Asherah, and a larger divine family or council. Smith argues that Israelite literature preserves related structures and that the relationship between El and YHWH was historically complex rather than conceptually fixed from the beginning.

This matters because later Jewish and Christian readers commonly encounter biblical terms such as El, Elohim, El Elyon, and El Shaddai simply as titles or names of the same singular God. Historically, however, some of these terms belong to an older West Semitic divine vocabulary. The emergence of the biblical God therefore appears to involve processes of identification, assimilation, and theological consolidation. One schematic way to represent the process is:

Causal pattern: multiple inherited divine traditions + Yahwistic worship → increasing identification and consolidation

Smith argues specifically that characteristics and titles associated with El became assimilated to YHWH. The resulting deity could therefore inherit features once distributed across a larger divine system. This is more complex than saying that “the Israelites replaced El with YHWH.” In many biblical texts the distinction has disappeared entirely. The earlier divine vocabulary has been incorporated into the identity of YHWH. That process itself illustrates an important feature of cultural systems: they can absorb prior religious structures while retrospectively presenting the resulting synthesis as a unified tradition.

YHWH, El, and the Divine Council

The divine council provides another important example. Ugaritic literature depicts El presiding over an assembly or family of gods. Biblical literature preserves strikingly similar imagery. Psalm 82, for example, depicts God standing in a divine assembly and judging other elohim. Deuteronomy 32, especially in its older textual witnesses from the Dead Sea Scrolls and Septuagintal tradition, appears to describe the nations as apportioned according to divine beings, with YHWH receiving Israel as his portion. Scholars disagree over the precise historical reconstruction of these passages, but the larger connection between Israelite divine-council imagery and broader West Semitic traditions is well established.

This produces an important historical contrast. A later theological ontology might be represented as: God ≫ angels ≫ humans, with only one being occupying the category of deity. But an earlier religious structure may have looked conceptually closer to:

Causal pattern: high deity → divine council → national or subordinate divine beings

Later monotheistic interpretation can preserve the personnel while changing their ontological status. Beings once describable as gods can become angels, heavenly hosts, or subordinate celestial beings. Thus historical continuity can coexist with ontological reinterpretation.

Archaeology and the Diversity of Yahwism

The archaeological and epigraphic evidence complicates the picture further. Eighth-century BCE inscriptions from Kuntillet ʿAjrud invoke “Yahweh of Samaria” or “Yahweh of Teman” together with “his asherah.” A related inscription from Khirbet el-Qom also invokes YHWH and “his asherah.” The interpretation remains debated: asherah may refer directly to the goddess Asherah, to a cultic object associated with her, or to a symbolic manifestation of YHWH's cultic presence. What is difficult to deny is that these inscriptions reveal forms of Yahwistic religion less cleanly aligned with the later biblical presentation of exclusive YHWH worship. This distinction between religion as historically practiced and religion as retrospectively represented in authoritative texts is essential. Biblical texts often condemn Asherah, Baal worship, high places, images, and other forms of cultic practice precisely because those practices existed among Israelites and Judeans. Polemic against a practice is evidence that the religious field was contested. The historical picture therefore looks less like:

Causal pattern: pure monotheism → occasional corruption

and more like:

Causal pattern: religious plurality + competition + centralization + scribal selection → increasingly exclusive Yahwism

This does not mean that every Israelite worshipper held the same beliefs or that one neat theological stage replaced another. Different social groups, geographic regions, and institutions may have supported different religious configurations simultaneously. Indeed, this heterogeneity is exactly what a systems model predicts. At any time:

\[ R_t \neq \text{one uniform belief state}. \]

It is better represented as a distribution:

\[ R_t = \{r_1,r_2,r_3,\ldots,r_n\}, \]

containing elite theology, household practice, local cults, royal ideology, prophetic criticism, and competing interpretations. Later textual and institutional selection can reduce that visible diversity.

From Exclusive Yahwism to Universal Sovereignty

A particularly important development is the emergence of increasingly explicit monotheistic rhetoric. Smith argues that biblical monotheism should not simply be imagined as a discrete philosophical discovery replacing an earlier stage. He treats it partly as a rhetoric of exclusive identity that becomes especially intelligible against the political crises of the late monarchic and exilic periods. As Judah's political world collapsed under imperial domination, YHWH's sovereignty could be reconceived on a correspondingly universal scale: the loss of national political power did not imply the defeat of Israel's deity, because the foreign empires themselves could be interpreted as instruments operating under YHWH's authority. The theological transformation is profound. An earlier national framework might be represented as:

Causal pattern: Israel ↔︎ YHWH

within a world containing other peoples and their gods. A later universalizing framework becomes:

Causal pattern: YHWH → all nations + all history + all creation

The political catastrophe does not destroy the religious system. It becomes input into theological transformation. This is precisely the kind of adaptive reinterpretation discussed earlier:

Causal pattern: political crisis + inherited theology → expanded concept of divine sovereignty

Under a systems model, defeat can therefore generate theological innovation rather than straightforward religious abandonment.

Divine Embodiment and Representation

The conceptual development of YHWH concerns not only divine status but also divine embodiment. This is where Francesca Stavrakopoulou's God: An Anatomy becomes particularly relevant. Her central thesis is that the ancient biblical deity should be understood against the embodied male gods of the surrounding ancient Southwest Asian world. She emphasizes the extensive bodily language applied to YHWH: face, eyes, ears, nose, mouth, arms, hands, legs, feet, breath, movement, bodily radiance, appetite, and physical presence. She argues strongly that later theological assumptions about an incorporeal God can obscure how bodily many ancient representations originally were. This is a provocative version of a broader scholarly observation. The Hebrew Bible does indeed contain pervasive anthropomorphic language. YHWH walks, descends, smells sacrifices, stretches out his hand, sits enthroned, shows his back to Moses, hides his face, rides upon clouds, and appears in radiant bodily forms. The important scholarly disagreement concerns how these descriptions should be interpreted. Stavrakopoulou tends toward a strongly corporeal reconstruction. Critics such as R. W. L. Moberly argue that she sometimes minimizes metaphor, literary idiom, and the possibility that ancient texts could use bodily language symbolically without committing to the kind of literal divine anatomy she reconstructs. For the present argument, it is unnecessary to resolve that dispute in Stavrakopoulou's favor.

Theodore Lewis provides a more plural picture. He emphasizes that Israelite traditions contain abundant anthropomorphic representations of YHWH alongside traditions emphasizing fire, divine radiance or kābôd, the divine Name, aniconism, and invisibility. These different representations coexist within the textual tradition rather than forming one simple linear replacement sequence. This complexity is theoretically significant. One might expect a simplistic evolutionary model to predict:

Causal pattern: bodily God → non-bodily God

with the earlier representation disappearing. But cultural systems rarely develop so cleanly. A more realistic model predicts:

Causal pattern: older representations + new interpretations → layered conceptual system

Older language can survive after its original conceptual context has changed. Thus later readers can inherit descriptions of God's hands, face, throne, anger, movement, and bodily glory while embedding those descriptions within increasingly abstract theological models. This is the same kind of historical layering visible in other cultural domains. Language preserves archaic forms. Legal systems preserve old categories under new interpretations. Political institutions retain names and rituals after their functions change. Religious traditions can do the same.

What the Case Shows

The significant point, therefore, is not that scholarship has established one uncontested transformation from a literal humanoid deity into a philosophical abstraction. It is that the representation of the deity is historically variable. YHWH can be represented as:

  • warrior;
  • storm-associated divine fighter;
  • king;
  • judge;
  • family deity;
  • enthroned ruler;
  • embodied presence;
  • radiance;
  • fire;
  • invisible deity;
  • universal creator.

Different traditions emphasize different combinations of these traits. Lewis's synthesis makes precisely this plurality of characterization central to reconstructing ancient Israelite concepts of divinity. The variable undergoing historical change is therefore no longer merely the story about God. It is:

\[ \boxed{\text{the conceptual representation of what God is}}. \]

This produces a more difficult problem for revelatory models. If the existence and attributes of God are objective features of reality, one might initially expect revelation to provide information about those attributes that possesses at least some independence from historically contingent conceptual environments. Instead, the representation of YHWH appears deeply embedded in those environments. Early Israelite religious language participates in West Semitic divine structures. Divine-council imagery resembles neighboring systems. Local Yahwistic practice incorporates religious elements later biblical texts reject. Political crisis correlates with the expansion of claims concerning YHWH's universal sovereignty. Anthropomorphic representations coexist with increasingly abstract representations. The deity concept develops as the cultural system develops.

Under the social-historical model, this is expected. We can represent the process schematically:

\[ G_{t+1} = F( G_t, C_t, P_t, I_t, N_t, \text{scribal interpretation}, \ldots ), \]

where \(G_t\) represents the socially represented concept of God at time \(t\). Political change can alter the problem theology must solve. Contact with neighboring religions changes the conceptual ecology. Institutional competition selects some forms of worship over others. Scribal traditions preserve certain representations and polemicize against others. Later interpreters reconcile older texts with newer theological commitments. The resulting deity concept is historically cumulative.

Implications for Revelation

A theological response remains available. One may argue that God progressively revealed himself through these developments, gradually correcting earlier misconceptions while accommodating revelation to the intellectual limitations of particular historical communities. That possibility will require serious consideration later in this essay. But notice what the response concedes. It concedes that the historically observable representation of God is generated through cultural inheritance, political history, conceptual change, institutional selection, and reinterpretation. The revelatory component must therefore be inferred within or behind a historical process whose visible structure is already substantially explained by ordinary mechanisms. The question becomes:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What feature of this development identifies progressive revelation rather than ordinary conceptual evolution?} } \]

That question is harder than the one raised by the Sargon narrative. If only literary forms were culturally inherited, a defender of revelation could say that God simply uses familiar stories. If theological problems were historically inherited, one could say that God gradually leads human reasoning toward deeper answers. But if the identity and properties attributed to the revealer himself change within the same historically intelligible processes, the accommodation required becomes more extensive. The distinction between messenger and message begins to break down. The conceptual form of the supposed source of revelation is itself historically mediated.

This yields the third stage of the cumulative argument: sacred narrative has a cultural genealogy, theological reasoning has an intellectual genealogy, and now:

\[ \boxed{\text{the concept of the deity has a historical genealogy}.} \]

The next case pushes the problem outward from God to the larger supernatural world. During the Second Temple period, Jewish literature increasingly develops resurrection, cosmic conflict, named angelic powers, eschatological judgment, supernatural opponents, and a predetermined end of history. Several of these developments have striking Iranian parallels and emerge after prolonged Jewish contact with Persian imperial culture. The question therefore becomes deeper still:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What happens when purported knowledge of the invisible structure of reality itself also develops historically?} } \]

IX. Case Study Four: Iranian Religion and Jewish Apocalypticism — The Historical Development of Supernatural Ontology

The previous case concerned the historical development of the deity concept itself. The present case widens the frame to the larger supernatural world: angels, demons, cosmic evil, resurrection, judgment, the structure of history, and the final destiny of creation. This is one of the most consequential—and most methodologically difficult—examples in the entire argument. The comparison between Zoroastrianism and later Judaism has long attracted scholars because a cluster of ideas becomes increasingly prominent in Jewish literature during and after centuries of Iranian political and cultural contact: bodily resurrection, final judgment, cosmic conflict between good and evil, increasingly elaborate supernatural agents, the division of history into stages moving toward a final crisis, and the ultimate transformation of the world. Zoroastrian religion contains strikingly similar structures.

At the same time, this is exactly the sort of case in which careless claims of influence can outrun the evidence. Many of the fullest surviving descriptions of Zoroastrian eschatology are preserved in Middle Persian texts written or compiled centuries after the relevant Jewish literature. Shaul Shaked identifies this as the central methodological problem: Zoroastrian influence on Jewish eschatology is quite plausible, but the most complete Iranian sources are later than the earliest Jewish apocalyptic writings. The argument must therefore distinguish between three claims: Iranian and Jewish eschatologies display important parallels, Jewish communities had prolonged historical contact with Iranian culture, and the stronger claim:

\[ \text{specific Jewish doctrine }X\text{ was directly borrowed from specific Iranian doctrine }Y. \]

The first two claims are much easier to support than the third. This distinction is important because the larger systems argument does not require a simplistic one-to-one borrowing model. What matters is whether Jewish supernatural concepts developed within a cultural environment in which Iranian eschatological and dualistic ideas were historically available and whether the resulting changes exhibit the kinds of contact-sensitive transformation predicted by the causal model developed earlier.

Historical Contact and the Chronological Problem

The historical opportunity for interaction is undeniable. Cyrus the Great conquered Babylon in 539 BCE, after which Judah became the Persian province of Yehud within the Achaemenid Empire. Persian rule continued until Alexander's conquests in the late fourth century BCE. Jewish communities also remained in Mesopotamia and continued living under Iranian political systems long afterward. The relevant historical relationship therefore was not a brief encounter. It involved: centuries of political incorporation + diaspora contact + administrative interaction + scribal and cultural exchange. Recent scholarship increasingly treats Persian imperial culture not as a passive background but as an active environment shaping Jewish scribal identity. Mark Leuchter, for example, argues that late Persian-period Jewish writers responded in different ways to Achaemenid imperial mythology and that Persian rule helped structure how Jewish scribes reformulated their own traditions. The network conditions for conceptual exchange were therefore present.

Earlier Israelite Patterns and Later Apocalypticism

The more difficult question is what happened within Jewish supernatural thought during and after this period. Earlier Israelite literature certainly contains supernatural beings, divine councils, spirits, death, and judgment. It would be mistaken to imagine a primitive Israelite religion with no supernatural cosmology at all. Nor does every later Jewish doctrine lack indigenous antecedents. Nevertheless, the overall structure of earlier biblical religion differs significantly from the more developed apocalyptic worldview visible in works such as Daniel and 1 Enoch. In much earlier biblical literature, the dead descend to Sheol, a shadowy realm that is not primarily organized as a system of moral reward and punishment. Hope for Israel often concerns restoration within historical time: return from exile, defeat of enemies, political restoration, agricultural prosperity, or renewed divine presence.

The figure later known as Satan also begins very differently from the cosmic adversary familiar from later Christianity. In Job, ha-satan functions as an accuser within the divine council. He appears before God, operates with divine permission, and possesses no independent metaphysical sovereignty. The conceptual structure looks roughly like:

Causal pattern: YHWH → heavenly court → the accuser as subordinate agent

This is not yet:

Causal pattern: God ↔︎ cosmic anti-God

Likewise, earlier prophetic hopes generally concern God's intervention in history rather than a fully developed universal resurrection of individuals followed by everlasting postmortem destinies. The situation becomes markedly different in later Jewish apocalyptic literature. The Book of Daniel is especially important. Its final apocalyptic visions, Daniel 7–12, are generally dated to the crisis under Antiochus IV Epiphanes in the second century BCE, with the final form of the book commonly placed around 164 BCE. Daniel 12 contains the clearest statement of individual resurrection in the Hebrew Bible: some who sleep in the dust awaken to everlasting life and others to everlasting disgrace. Daniel's supernatural world is also much more elaborately structured. Michael appears as a heavenly prince associated with Israel. Gabriel functions as an interpreting angel. A “prince of Persia” and a “prince of Greece” correspond to earthly political powers. Human history is therefore represented as having a parallel celestial dimension:

Causal pattern: earthly imperial conflict ↔︎ heavenly supernatural conflict

History itself acquires an apocalyptic structure. Successive empires rise and fall according to a hidden timetable. Oppression intensifies. A final crisis occurs. Divine judgment destroys the oppressive order. The righteous are vindicated. The dead rise. Thus:

Causal pattern: history → predetermined sequence → final crisis → divine intervention → resurrection and judgment

This differs significantly from a model in which history remains an open sequence of covenantal judgment and restoration.

The Iranian Comparison

The Iranian comparison becomes relevant here because Zoroastrian religion possessed an unusually systematic cosmic account of moral history. In its developed form, Zoroastrianism describes reality through a struggle between the forces associated with Ahura Mazda and those associated with Angra Mainyu, later Ahriman. Human moral choice participates in that cosmic conflict. History moves toward a decisive defeat of evil, resurrection of the dead, final judgment, purification, and the restoration or perfection of creation known as frašō.kərəti or Frashokereti. Shaked identifies resurrection, universal judgment, renewed creation, and the destruction or neutralization of evil as central features of Zoroastrian eschatology. The broad architecture can be represented as:

Causal pattern: creation → cosmic moral conflict → historical struggle → final crisis → resurrection → judgment → renewed creation

This architecture is strikingly close to the structure that becomes increasingly important in Jewish apocalypticism and, subsequently, Christianity. Mary Boyce emphasizes that the Zoroastrian tradition conceived the whole of history as part of a cosmic struggle moving toward the overthrow of evil and the final restoration of creation. She also notes Greek testimony associated with Theopompus in the fourth century BCE reporting Iranian beliefs about a final defeat of evil and the resurrection of the dead. That testimony is important because it shows that at least some major eschatological concepts were present in Iranian religion well before the late Middle Persian manuscripts in which they are described most fully. This helps with, though does not eliminate, the chronological problem. We therefore have evidence for an Iranian eschatological complex that includes ancient components, even if the exact historical form of every doctrine cannot be reconstructed securely.

Resurrection and Conceptual Stimulus

Among the strongest candidates for Iranian influence is resurrection. Earlier Israelite traditions contain resources from which resurrection theology could develop internally. National restoration could be described metaphorically through return from death, most famously in Ezekiel's vision of dry bones. Isaiah contains passages that move closer to resurrection language. A creator deity capable of giving life could also provide an indigenous conceptual basis for renewed bodily life. It would therefore be wrong to argue:

\[ \text{Judaism had no resources for resurrection} \therefore \text{resurrection must be Persian}. \]

But the historical conjunction remains significant. A doctrine of individual resurrection becomes explicit in Jewish apocalyptic literature after prolonged Persian contact, while resurrection formed part of Iranian eschatological expectation. Daniel 12 then incorporates resurrection into a larger system involving final crisis, heavenly conflict, and everlasting destinies. The appropriate causal hypothesis is therefore not necessarily direct copying. It may instead involve conceptual stimulus:

Causal pattern: indigenous Jewish restoration traditions + Iranian eschatological concepts + new historical crises → Jewish resurrection theology

This is precisely the kind of hybrid outcome predicted by a network model.

Cosmic Opposition Without Simple Identity

The development of cosmic moral dualism provides another important comparison. Zoroastrian theology assigns extraordinary significance to the opposition between truth/order and the Lie, eventually expressed through the conflict between Ahura Mazda's order and Angra Mainyu's destructive power. Later Jewish texts similarly develop increasingly polarized supernatural structures:

Causal pattern: light ↔︎ darkness

Causal pattern: angels ↔︎ demons

Causal pattern: righteous ↔︎ wicked

Causal pattern: present evil age ↔︎ coming divine age

The Dead Sea Scrolls later provide especially dramatic examples in their language of the Sons of Light and Sons of Darkness. Yet Jewish apocalypticism does not simply become Zoroastrian dualism. This difference is important. In most Jewish formulations: God ≫ evil supernatural powers. The hostile powers remain subordinate creatures within a monotheistic ontology. Thus the receiving system transforms the incoming conceptual possibility:

Causal pattern: Iranian-style cosmic opposition + Jewish monotheistic constraint → subordinate eschatological dualism

If Iranian influence occurred, it therefore took precisely the form predicted earlier: transmission constrained by the receiving system. The comparison between Satan and Ahriman illustrates why simplistic equivalences should be avoided. The Jewish Satan has an indigenous genealogy in the adversarial or prosecutorial figure of earlier biblical literature. It would be historically crude to say that Jews encountered Ahriman and simply renamed him Satan. Instead, the plausible question is whether prolonged exposure to a religious system containing a highly developed cosmic opponent contributed to the transformation of an older subordinate adversary into a more powerful eschatological enemy. That model would look like:

Causal pattern: native adversary tradition + dualistic conceptual environment + apocalyptic development → later cosmic Satan

This formulation preserves both continuity and possible external influence. It also illustrates a broader principle of cultural evolution: new religious structures often arise by refunctionalizing inherited figures rather than creating entirely new figures.

Angelology and Supernatural Administration

The development of angelology provides a similar case. Angelic beings existed long before the Persian period, but Daniel presents a much more differentiated supernatural administration. Named angelic beings perform specialized roles; celestial “princes” correspond to nations; earthly geopolitics has a heavenly analogue. Such a system fits naturally within the hierarchical bureaucratic and cosmological environments of large imperial cultures. Indeed, recent work on Persian-period Judaism emphasizes the broader influence of Persian imperial categories on Jewish thought, including court imagery and angelic messenger concepts. Once again, this does not establish that Jewish angels were copied from Zoroastrian yazatas or Amesha Spentas. The stronger and more defensible point is systemic: supernatural bureaucracy can itself reflect the structures and conceptual resources of historically specific societies.

Apocalyptic History and World Ages

The transformation of history itself may be the most important parallel. Earlier prophetic religion often interprets specific historical events morally:

Causal pattern: sin → judgment → repentance/restoration

Apocalyptic literature increasingly treats world history as a single predetermined sequence:

\[ \text{age}_1 \rightarrow \text{age}_2 \rightarrow \text{age}_3 \rightarrow \text{final oppressive age} \rightarrow \text{divine kingdom}. \]

Daniel 2 and Daniel 7 famously represent successive empires through metals and beasts. Zoroastrian apocalyptic traditions also contain sequences of world ages, sometimes represented through metals. This parallel is suggestive but especially difficult to use straightforwardly. The surviving Iranian metallic-age texts are late, and the direction of influence is uncertain. Boyce argued for an Iranian tradition influencing Daniel, while other scholars have proposed Greek influence on Iranian schemes or Jewish influence on the surviving Iranian form. Encyclopaedia Iranica explicitly notes that the relationship remains debated. This is exactly the sort of case in which the larger causal model is stronger than a claim of direct textual dependence. The important claim is not that Daniel simply copied an Iranian metallic-age scheme. It is that Jewish, Iranian, and Hellenistic communities occupied connected intellectual networks in which universal schemes of empire, historical succession, cosmic conflict, and final resolution circulated and were repeatedly reformulated. Influence may be multidirectional; the network is reticulate rather than linear.

Influence Through Rejection

Iranian influence may also operate through polemical rejection rather than adoption. Isaiah 45 is an especially intriguing example. The text concerns Cyrus explicitly, calling him YHWH's anointed instrument, while simultaneously declaring that YHWH creates both light and darkness and produces both well-being and calamity. Some interpreters have understood this as a possible polemic against Iranian dualistic categories: YHWH does not govern only one side of reality while an opposing supernatural principle governs the other. Whether direct anti-Zoroastrian polemic can be demonstrated is uncertain. But the form of interaction is theoretically important. A neighboring idea can generate doctrinal change by provoking explicit rejection:

Causal pattern: foreign conceptual alternative → polemical response → sharpened local doctrine

Cultural influence therefore need not imply agreement. A religion may become more distinctively itself because interaction with another religion forces it to articulate boundaries that previously remained implicit. This greatly expands the explanatory power of the network model. Similarity can indicate contact. Difference can also sometimes indicate contact. What matters is whether historical connectivity changes the conceptual state space.

What the Case Shows

The cumulative transformation becomes clearer when we compare the earlier and later Jewish systems schematically. An earlier structure might be simplified as: YHWH + divine council + Sheol + historical restoration. A later apocalyptic structure increasingly contains: YHWH + named archangels + hostile supernatural powers + cosmic conflict + predetermined ages + resurrection + final judgment + everlasting destinies. This is not merely the addition of decorative mythology. The ontology has changed. Claims are now being made about:

  • what happens to individuals after death;
  • whether bodies will rise;
  • which supernatural beings govern nations;
  • whether evil possesses organized cosmic agency;
  • whether history follows a predetermined timetable;
  • whether a universal judgment will occur;
  • and what the final condition of creation will be.

These are propositions about purportedly objective supernatural reality. That is why this case matters so much for the larger explanatory argument. If religious traditions merely adapted familiar literary styles, accommodation would be comparatively easy to invoke. But here the changing cultural environment appears correlated with changing beliefs about what actually exists beyond the visible world. Under the social-historical model, this is not surprising:

\[ Q_{t+1} = F( Q_t, C_t, N_t, P_t, \text{historical crisis}, \text{foreign conceptual resources}, \ldots ), \]

where \(Q_t\) represents the community's supernatural ontology. Long-term contact changes the conceptual ecology. Historical crisis increases demand for explanations of injustice. Inherited traditions supply local constraints. Foreign systems supply additional categories. The resulting synthesis produces new supernatural models. This is a normal process of conceptual development. A progressive-revelation model can again absorb the same history. One might argue that God gradually disclosed resurrection, judgment, angelic hierarchy, and eschatological truth, perhaps using Iranian ideas as culturally available vehicles. But the explanatory burden has now increased. The question is no longer merely why God would use a familiar story or allow humans to debate suffering. It becomes:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Why does purported knowledge of objective supernatural reality emerge along historically identifiable channels of cultural contact?} } \]

And more importantly:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What observation allows us to distinguish progressive supernatural disclosure from ordinary conceptual diffusion and transformation?} } \]

The significance of Iranian influence therefore does not depend on proving that every Jewish apocalyptic doctrine originated in Zoroastrianism. The stronger result is structural. Jewish eschatology develops in a world of sustained Iranian contact. Several of its emerging doctrines have important Iranian analogues. The resulting Jewish system does not simply replicate Iranian religion; it transforms possible influences under the constraints of Jewish monotheism, scripture, historical experience, and institutional identity. The trajectory is therefore consistent with the cultural model introduced earlier:

Causal pattern: network contact + local inheritance + historical crisis + conceptual selection → new religious ontology

This gives us the fourth stage of the cumulative argument: sacred narrative has a cultural genealogy, theological reasoning has an intellectual genealogy, the concept of the deity has a historical genealogy, and now:

\[ \boxed{\text{the structure of the invisible world itself has a historical genealogy}.} \]

The final case pushes the analysis one level further. Christianity emerges from Judaism within a heavily Hellenized cultural environment. Its scriptures are transmitted largely in Greek; Jewish thinkers such as Philo had already begun interpreting biblical religion through Greek philosophical categories; early Christian writers developed theology using the conceptual resources of the Greco-Roman world; and later church fathers increasingly articulated the Christian God through Platonist and eventually Neoplatonic metaphysics. The question therefore becomes not merely how beliefs about the supernatural world develop, but how the philosophical categories used to define what God fundamentally is can themselves be historically inherited.

\[ \boxed{ \text{What happens when the metaphysics of revelation also has a cultural genealogy?} } \]

X. Case Study Five: Hellenistic Judaism and Christianity — The Historical Construction of Christian Metaphysics

The final historical case moves from the development of supernatural ontology to the philosophical categories through which the divine itself came to be described. Christianity did not emerge from a culturally isolated Judaism and only later encounter Greek thought as an external influence. It arose within a Jewish world that had already been deeply transformed by centuries of Hellenistic linguistic, literary, and philosophical interaction. The later development of Christian theology continued this process, eventually producing highly sophisticated metaphysical accounts of God using conceptual resources drawn in substantial part from Greek philosophy. This case therefore concerns something deeper than the adoption of particular stories or doctrines. It concerns the conceptual framework through which religious claims themselves became intelligible. The process can be represented schematically as:

Causal pattern: Israelite/Jewish tradition + Hellenistic language and philosophy → Hellenistic Judaism → early Christianity → patristic metaphysics

The sequence is not a simple replacement of “Hebrew thought” by “Greek thought.” Such a model would be historically misleading. Judaism had already become internally diverse, Greek-speaking Jewish communities developed their own forms of scriptural interpretation, and Christian writers repeatedly transformed Greek concepts rather than simply adopting them intact. The important point is structural: the conceptual vocabulary through which later Christians described God was historically mediated.

The Septuagint as Conceptual Mediation

The process begins before Christianity. Beginning in the Hellenistic period, Jewish scriptures were translated into Greek. The collection conventionally called the Septuagint was not produced all at once by a single translation project. Different books were translated or composed over several centuries, beginning with the Greek Pentateuch in Ptolemaic Egypt. The resulting Greek Jewish scriptures became enormously important both within Hellenistic Judaism and, later, within Christianity. The Oxford Handbook of the Septuagint emphasizes that these Greek versions originated within Hellenistic Jewish communities and eventually became the scriptures most familiar to the authors of the New Testament and the authoritative Old Testament of much of early Christianity. Translation is often treated as though it were merely the substitution of one set of words for another:

Causal pattern: Hebrew term → equivalent Greek term

But translation rarely works this way, especially when dealing with religious and philosophical vocabulary. Languages divide conceptual space differently. A translator must choose among words carrying different connotations, philosophical histories, and semantic associations. Once translated, a text becomes available to readers through the conceptual possibilities of the target language. The process is therefore closer to:

Causal pattern: source concept + translator interpretation + target-language semantic field → new textual form

This matters because early Christian authors overwhelmingly encountered Jewish scripture in Greek. David Lincicum describes the Septuagint as a kind of intellectual “encyclopaedia” for New Testament authors, shaping not merely their quotations but many characteristic expressions and theological concepts. Thus the scriptural environment inherited by early Christianity was already mediated through a major cultural and linguistic transformation:

Causal pattern: Hebrew/Aramaic religious traditions → Greek Jewish scriptures → Christian interpretation

The transformation was not necessarily theological distortion. A translation can preserve meaning extraordinarily well while still changing the range of associations available to later readers. But from the standpoint of the causal model developed earlier, the important fact is that language becomes part of the historical path dependence of theology. Once a Greek version becomes authoritative, later theological reasoning can depend on distinctions or associations created or amplified by that version. The famous use of Isaiah 7:14 in Matthew provides one illustration, although it should be handled cautiously. The Hebrew text uses ʿalmâ, a term generally referring to a young woman, while the Septuagint renders it with parthenos, a Greek term often associated with virginity. Matthew then quotes the Greek form in interpreting Jesus' birth. It would be too strong to claim that the Septuagint simply “created” the Christian virgin-birth tradition. The semantics are more complicated, and theological traditions rarely arise from one lexical choice alone. Nevertheless, the example shows how translation can alter the interpretive possibilities inherited by a later religious community.

This is a general principle:

Causal pattern: translation → new interpretive affordances → downstream theological consequences

Once a translation becomes canonical, its effects can persist for centuries. This makes the Septuagint an excellent example of the path dependence described earlier. A contingent linguistic decision can become part of the conceptual infrastructure of later revelation.

Hellenistic Judaism Before Christianity

The influence of Greek thought becomes even clearer in Hellenistic Jewish philosophy. This is crucial because it prevents a simplistic model in which Christianity independently encounters Greek philosophy after emerging from an otherwise non-Hellenized Judaism. Greek-speaking Judaism had already been engaging with Hellenistic intellectual culture for generations. Alexandria was one of the most important centers of this interaction. The city combined a large Jewish population with one of the most sophisticated Greek intellectual environments in the Mediterranean. Recent Oxford scholarship describes Hellenistic Jewish philosophy as emerging precisely within this environment of dense intercultural exchange. Philo of Alexandria, who lived roughly from the late first century BCE into the first century CE, is the most important surviving example.

Philo regarded the Torah as divinely authoritative. He did not understand himself as abandoning Judaism for Greek philosophy. Instead, he interpreted Jewish scripture through a philosophical vocabulary shaped especially by Middle Platonism, while also drawing on Stoic and other traditions. Cambridge's History of Philosophy in Late Antiquity identifies Middle Platonism as the philosophical context that best explains many of Philo's formulations. This makes Philo especially valuable for the present argument. He demonstrates that one can sincerely believe in the divine revelation of Jewish scripture while simultaneously reconceptualizing its meaning through a new philosophical ecology. In Philo we encounter an increasingly transcendent God who exceeds ordinary categories of material existence. Peter Schäfer summarizes Philo's theology as presenting an absolutely transcendent God whose essence cannot be known directly, while divine “powers” and the Logos mediate between the transcendent deity and the created world.

Maren Niehoff similarly emphasizes Philo's deep engagement with Platonic and Pythagorean traditions, including his development of an unusually strong negative theology and a Logos functioning as an intermediary between the transcendent God and the world. The causal synthesis can be represented as:

Causal pattern: Jewish scripture + Platonic metaphysics + Alexandrian intellectual culture → Philonic Jewish theology

Notice what has happened. The biblical deity is no longer being interpreted only through inherited Israelite or Near Eastern categories. Questions now arise such as:

  • How can an utterly transcendent God interact with matter?
  • How can the immutable divine relate to a changing cosmos?
  • What mediates between intelligible and sensible reality?
  • How can anthropomorphic biblical language be reconciled with philosophical transcendence?
  • What is the relationship between divine reason and creation?

These questions are not merely answers added to an unchanged biblical conceptual world. The problem-space itself has changed because Greek metaphysics introduces new distinctions that theology must now negotiate.

Logos and the Conceptual Environment of Early Christianity

The concept of Logos provides a particularly useful example. In Greek intellectual traditions, logos possesses a wide semantic range including word, reason, account, rational principle, or discourse. Stoic traditions could describe the cosmos in terms of rational ordering principles. Philo used Logos within a Jewish exegetical framework to describe an intermediary principle connected with divine reason and creation. Christianity emerges into precisely this conceptual environment. The opening of the Gospel of John famously declares:

“In the beginning was the Logos.”

The Johannine Logos is not simply identical with Philo's Logos. Nor is it legitimate to reduce John's Christology to direct borrowing from Philo. Jewish Wisdom traditions, Genesis creation theology, scriptural traditions, and specifically Christian claims about Jesus all contribute to John's conceptual world. But the relevant point is again ecological rather than genealogically simplistic. By the late first century CE, Jewish thinkers could already articulate the relationship between God and creation through the Greek category of Logos. Oxford scholarship on early Jewish writings explicitly treats Philo as important contextual evidence for understanding aspects of Johannine Christology. Thus:

Causal pattern: Jewish Wisdom traditions + Genesis + Hellenistic Logos discourse + Christian claims about Jesus → Johannine Logos theology

This is a much better model than:

Causal pattern: Philo → John copied Philo

The cultural model predicts recombination. A new religious movement can take inherited Jewish categories, Greek conceptual vocabulary, and claims about Jesus and produce something that is both historically dependent and genuinely novel. This same pattern becomes increasingly visible in second-century Christian thinkers.

Christianity and Greek Philosophy

The interaction between Christianity and Greek philosophy becomes much more explicit among Christian intellectuals such as Justin Martyr, Clement of Alexandria, and Origen. A chronological distinction is important here. The earliest Christian writings should not be described as “Neoplatonic.” Neoplatonism properly emerges in the third century CE, especially with Plotinus. Earlier Christianity instead interacts with a complex Greco-Roman philosophical environment that includes Stoicism, Middle Platonism, Aristotelian traditions, and various forms of Platonizing thought. Cambridge's history of late ancient philosophy distinguishes the Platonist traditions before Plotinus from the later development conventionally called Neoplatonism. This distinction matters because Christian philosophical development occurs in stages. We might represent the trajectory roughly as:

Causal pattern: Hellenistic philosophy → Middle Platonism and Stoicism → early Christian philosophical theology → Neoplatonism → later patristic synthesis

Justin Martyr, for example, explicitly interprets Christ as the divine Logos and attempts to relate Christian revelation to Greek philosophy. The Stanford Encyclopedia's history of Trinitarian doctrines notes that Justin's Logos theology shares important conceptual resources with Philo and Middle Platonist traditions, including hierarchical relations among God, Logos, and Spirit. Clement of Alexandria goes further in treating Greek philosophy as a providential preparation for Christian truth. Scholarship has long regarded Clement as a major figure in the emergence of Christian Platonism. Origen then develops one of the most philosophically sophisticated Christian systems of the early church, integrating scriptural exegesis, Platonic conceptual structures, cosmology, soul theory, and theology. Again, these authors do not simply become Platonists who happen to quote the Bible. They transform Platonism in response to Christian commitments. But the resulting theology is historically unintelligible without Greek philosophical categories. This is the central causal point:

Causal pattern: Christian scripture + Greek philosophical problem-space → new Christian metaphysics

From Biblical Deity to Metaphysical God

The significance becomes clearer when we compare the conceptual vocabulary of much biblical literature with that of later classical Christian theology. Biblical texts frequently describe God through imagery of:

  • location;
  • movement;
  • face;
  • voice;
  • anger;
  • bodily manifestation;
  • throne;
  • descent;
  • ascent;
  • visible glory.

Later philosophical theology increasingly describes God as:

  • incorporeal;
  • immutable;
  • impassible;
  • timeless or outside ordinary temporal succession;
  • simple;
  • non-spatial;
  • metaphysically perfect;
  • the ultimate source of being.

These later concepts are not wholly absent from Jewish tradition, nor are they reducible to Greek philosophy. Biblical and Second Temple traditions already contain strong tendencies toward divine transcendence, invisibility, and incomparability. But the philosophical articulation of these attributes increasingly depends upon conceptual distinctions developed within Greek metaphysics. This creates a striking historical trajectory:

Causal pattern: ancient Near Eastern deity language → increasing transcendence → Hellenistic philosophical mediation → classical theistic metaphysics

The transition should not be imagined as one worldview simply replacing another. Older biblical language remains canonical. Later theologians therefore face a hermeneutical problem:

Causal pattern: anthropomorphic scripture + incorporeal metaphysics → allegorical or analogical reinterpretation

Descriptions that might once have functioned naturally within an embodied divine world are increasingly read metaphorically. God's “hand” becomes divine power. God's “anger” becomes anthropopathic accommodation. God's “movement” does not imply spatial relocation. The scriptural text remains the same while its metaphysical interpretation changes. This is an exceptionally clear example of path dependence combined with conceptual transformation.

Augustine and Neoplatonic Christianity

The process becomes especially explicit in Augustine. Augustine's encounter with what he calls the “books of the Platonists” profoundly affected his philosophical and theological development. The exact texts he read remain debated, but the Neoplatonic contribution to major elements of his thought is well established. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy identifies among the clearly Neoplatonic features of Augustine's mature thought:

  • God's transcendence and immateriality;
  • the superiority of the unchangeable over the changeable;
  • an ontological hierarchy of God, soul, and body;
  • the distinction between intelligible and sensible reality;
  • the non-spatial omnipresence of the intelligible;
  • Platonic forms understood as ideas in the mind of God;
  • inward ascent toward divine truth;
  • and the theory of evil as privation of goodness.

The privation theory of evil provides an especially illuminating example. Augustine had previously been attracted to Manichaean dualism, in which evil possessed a much more substantive metaphysical status. Through his engagement with Neoplatonic thought, he came to reject evil as an independent substance. Instead, evil is understood as the privation of due goodness. Everything that exists is good insofar as it possesses being. Evil is a deficiency, corruption, or absence rather than a competing metaphysical substance. The Stanford Encyclopedia explicitly connects Augustine's mature position to the Neoplatonic theory of privation. This is not a peripheral doctrinal detail. It changes the conceptual structure of the problem of evil. Rather than:

Causal pattern: good substance ↔︎ evil substance

Augustine can preserve monotheistic creation by arguing:

Causal pattern: God creates being → being is good → evil is deficiency rather than created substance

Greek metaphysics has therefore supplied conceptual machinery for resolving a major Christian theological problem. This is precisely what the causal model predicts. A philosophical environment generates concepts. A theologian inherits them. The concepts solve tensions within an existing religious system. The resulting synthesis becomes part of the later tradition. Eventually, later believers may encounter the synthesized doctrine as though it simply expressed what Christianity had always meant. The historical sequence becomes obscured by successful integration.

Cultural Dependence at the Level of Metaphysics

This case therefore pushes the cumulative argument further than the previous four. The issue is not merely that Christianity borrowed Greek terminology. Every religion expressed in a language necessarily uses culturally inherited words. The more significant observation is that Greek philosophical traditions helped provide the problem-space, distinctions, and ontological categories through which later Christian thinkers defined the nature of God. The variables now include: being, substance, essence, incorporeality, immutability, simplicity, intelligible versus sensible reality. These are not merely metaphors decorating an otherwise complete revelation. They form part of the conceptual architecture through which Christian theology answers:

\[ \boxed{\text{What kind of being is God?}} \]

This gives the Hellenistic case particular significance. The historical development can be represented as:

Causal pattern: Jewish revelation claims + Greek linguistic mediation + Platonic and Stoic conceptual resources + Christian theological problems → patristic metaphysics

Once again, a revelatory account can accommodate this. A theologian may say that Greek philosophy providentially supplied concepts needed to articulate truths already implicit in revelation. Justin and Clement themselves sometimes reasoned in roughly this way: Greek philosophy could function as preparation for the fuller truth manifested in Christianity. But the same evidential question returns. If the concepts needed to identify the true metaphysical nature of God arise historically through surrounding philosophical traditions, then:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What distinguishes philosophical maturation guided by revelation from ordinary conceptual development?} } \]

The issue becomes particularly sharp when later theology retrospectively interprets earlier texts through categories unavailable to their original authors. If an ancient Israelite text uses bodily language for YHWH, and a later Christian theologian says that the true God is incorporeal, immutable, non-spatial, and metaphysically simple, the later interpretation may be correct. But its correctness cannot simply be inferred from the antiquity or revelatory status of the earlier text. Something historically significant has occurred between them. The tradition has acquired new conceptual resources. Those resources have changed how the original revelation is understood. The interpretation of revelation has therefore become dependent upon cultural history.

This yields the fifth and deepest stage of the cumulative sequence: sacred narrative has a cultural genealogy, theological reasoning has an intellectual genealogy, the concept of the deity has a historical genealogy, the supernatural world has a historical genealogy, and finally:

\[ \boxed{ \text{the metaphysical categories used to define God have a historical genealogy}. } \]

Taken together, these five cases reveal a progressively deeper form of dependence. What begins with recognizable narrative motifs ends with historically conditioned answers to the question of what God fundamentally is. The significance is not that Greek philosophy, Persian religion, or Mesopotamian literature can individually “explain Christianity.” No single influence can do that, and the cultural model does not predict such a simple derivation. Instead, the cases exhibit the same general generative structure:

\[ R_{t+1} = F( R_t, C_t, N_t, I_t, P_t, E_t, \ldots ). \]

Religious systems inherit prior states, encounter new conceptual environments, selectively absorb or reject external material, reinterpret older commitments, and generate increasingly complex syntheses. The next task is therefore to stop treating the five examples separately and ask what they show collectively. The central question becomes:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Can one generative social-historical model account for the common structure underlying all five cases?} } \]
Part IVFrom Historical Explanation to Model Comparison

XI. From Five Cases to One Generative Model

The five historical cases considered so far differ in content, chronology, and evidential strength. The Sargon-Moses comparison concerns sacred narrative. Mesopotamian suffering literature and Job concern theological problem-spaces. The development of YHWH concerns the concept of deity itself. Iranian-Jewish interaction concerns supernatural ontology and eschatology. Hellenistic Judaism and Christianity concern the philosophical categories through which God and revelation are ultimately interpreted. Taken separately, each case raises a local historical question.

Taken together, they reveal a more general pattern. The recurring structure is not simply “religion A borrowed from religion B.” Nor is it that every religious concept can be traced to one earlier source. The deeper pattern is that religious systems change through historically structured interaction between inherited traditions and changing environments. The same generative logic appears repeatedly:

\[ R_{t+1} = F( R_t, C_t, N_t, I_t, P_t, E_t, D_t, S_t, \Phi_t, \epsilon_t ) \]

where the later religious state depends upon:

  • the inherited religious state \(R_t\);
  • the conceptual ecology \(C_t\);
  • the network environment \(N_t\);
  • institutional structures \(I_t\);
  • political and historical conditions \(P_t\);
  • individual experiences and innovations \(E_t\);
  • demographic conditions \(D_t\);
  • selection pressures \(S_t\);
  • feedback structures \(\Phi_t\);
  • and contingent factors represented by \(\epsilon_t\).

The Five Cases as Projections of One System

The historical cases can now be interpreted as different projections of this same underlying system. In the Sargon-Moses case, the relevant variable is narrative form. A community inherits a repertoire of narrative motifs, political symbols, and heroic conventions. Those materials are then recombined within a new ideological setting:

Causal pattern: inherited narrative repertoire + local theological aims + historical context → new sacred narrative

In the Job case, the relevant variable is not primarily story but intellectual problem-space. A culture inherits questions about suffering, divine justice, guilt, retribution, and human ignorance. Later thinkers modify and criticize earlier solutions:

Causal pattern: inherited theological problem + prior attempted solutions + critical reflection → new theological argument

In the development of YHWH, the changing variable is deeper still: the concept of the deity. Older West Semitic categories, local Yahwistic worship, political centralization, scribal interpretation, cultic competition, and historical crisis contribute to changing representations of divine identity and sovereignty:

Causal pattern: earlier deity concepts + political and cultic transformation + scribal selection → new conception of God

In the Iranian-Jewish case, the changing variable is supernatural ontology. Ideas about resurrection, cosmic opposition, judgment, angelic powers, and the structure of history become increasingly elaborate within a context of prolonged intercultural contact and political crisis:

Causal pattern: indigenous eschatological resources + foreign conceptual inputs + historical crisis → new supernatural model

Finally, in Hellenistic Christianity, the changing variable is metaphysical articulation. Jewish scriptures and Christian claims enter a conceptual environment shaped by Greek language and philosophy. Later theology uses those resources to describe God's being, transcendence, immateriality, simplicity, and relation to the world:

Causal pattern: inherited revelation claims + Greek conceptual resources + Christian theological problems → patristic metaphysics

The cumulative sequence is therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \text{narrative} \rightarrow \text{problem-space} \rightarrow \text{deity concept} \rightarrow \text{supernatural ontology} \rightarrow \text{metaphysical framework}. } \]

This sequence matters because it shows historical dependence at progressively deeper levels. If cultural influence affected only narrative form, a theological interpretation could easily maintain that revelation merely adopts familiar storytelling conventions. If historical dependence also affects theological reasoning, the explanation must expand: perhaps revelation guides an inherited intellectual conversation. If the concept of God itself develops, the account must expand again: perhaps God gradually corrects or deepens humanity's understanding of his own nature. If supernatural ontology develops through identifiable historical interaction, progressive revelation must now encompass claims about angels, demons, resurrection, judgment, and the structure of history. And if the metaphysical categories used to define God arise partly from later philosophical systems, the theological explanation must account for why supposedly objective knowledge of divine nature becomes articulable through conceptual resources generated outside the original revelatory tradition.

The increasing depth of dependence is therefore significant. The cumulative question is no longer:

Why does scripture sometimes resemble neighboring literature?

It becomes:

Why do the form, content, ontology, and metaphysical interpretation of revelation all exhibit historical structure?

This is where the systems model becomes more powerful than a collection of source-critical observations. The five cases are not best understood as five pieces of evidence for five separate acts of borrowing. They are evidence that religious systems display a common causal morphology. A general social-historical model predicts that traditions will exhibit: inheritance, variation, selection, network diffusion, reinterpretation, institutional stabilization, and path-dependent development. Each of the historical cases exhibits some combination of those processes.

Explanatory Compression

This matters methodologically because a theory gains explanatory strength when the same small family of mechanisms explains many superficially different observations. We can call this explanatory compression. Suppose one theory requires a separate special explanation for each phenomenon:

  • one explanation for the Moses narrative;
  • another for Job;
  • another for the development of monotheism;
  • another for apocalypticism;
  • another for Christian metaphysics.

A more unified theory explains all of them as outcomes of recurrent generative processes. Schematically:

\[ \{E_1,E_2,E_3,E_4,E_5\} \]

are explained not by:

\[ \{H_1,H_2,H_3,H_4,H_5\}, \]

but by a common model:

\[ M \rightarrow \{E_1,E_2,E_3,E_4,E_5\}. \]

The advantage is not merely parsimony in the crude sense of preferring fewer entities. It is that the mechanisms invoked by \(M\) are already independently observed elsewhere in human culture. Narrative motifs diffuse. Intellectual traditions inherit problems. Political crisis changes ideology. Institutions select among competing ideas. Languages reshape interpretation. Philosophical vocabularies migrate across cultural boundaries. Social networks influence diffusion. None of these mechanisms was invented specifically to explain religion. This gives the model substantial independent motivation. The five historical cases therefore do not stand alone. They are instances of more general processes that can be observed in other religious and nonreligious domains. The same underlying structure appears in:

  • legal traditions;
  • political ideologies;
  • philosophical schools;
  • literary genres;
  • scientific paradigms;
  • linguistic change;
  • institutional development.

This does not mean religion is identical to any of these phenomena. Religious systems have distinctive contents, practices, experiences, and authority structures. But they appear to participate in the same broader family of cultural processes.

Continuity, Adaptation, and Historical Layering

This is why the social model can potentially explain both religious continuity and religious change. Continuity follows from inheritance, institutional reinforcement, and path dependence. Change follows from innovation, network contact, environmental disruption, reinterpretation, and selection. The same system can therefore generate both stability and transformation. This is important because religious traditions often present themselves simultaneously as ancient and adaptive. They claim continuity with foundational revelation while repeatedly changing their interpretations, institutions, and doctrines. The systems model does not treat this as paradoxical. It predicts it. A successful tradition must often preserve enough continuity to maintain identity while adapting enough to survive changing environments. We can represent this tension as:

\[ \text{religious persistence} = f( \text{continuity}, \text{adaptability} ). \]

Too little continuity and the system loses recognizable identity. Too little adaptability and it may fail under new political, intellectual, or social conditions. This balancing problem helps explain why reinterpretation is so common. A community rarely needs to discard an inherited text when it can reinterpret it. An older doctrine can be preserved by changing its conceptual framework. A ritual can remain while its meaning shifts. An anthropomorphic divine description can survive inside an incorporeal metaphysics. An apocalyptic expectation can be revised after an expected event fails to occur. The ability to reinterpret inherited content is therefore not incidental. It is one of the major adaptive capacities of religious systems. This can be represented as:

Causal pattern: environmental pressure → reinterpretation → preservation of system identity

Such processes generate another important feature: historical layering. A mature religious tradition often contains concepts produced under very different cultural conditions. Older mythic language persists alongside later philosophical abstraction. Ancient ritual assumptions coexist with later ethical reinterpretations. Early divine-council imagery survives inside strict monotheism. Apocalyptic imagery remains even after later metaphysics changes how supernatural entities are understood. The tradition therefore resembles a layered system:

\[ R_t = L_1+L_2+L_3+\cdots+L_n, \]

where each layer reflects a different historical stage but later interpretation integrates them into a single canonical whole. This layered structure is itself predicted by path dependence. Later states do not normally erase all earlier states. They inherit them and reinterpret them. This explains why religious traditions can contain tensions that later theology must harmonize. The tensions are not necessarily failures of the system. They may simply be traces of its developmental history. A historically layered canon will naturally preserve materials produced under different assumptions. The task of later theology then becomes:

Causal pattern: heterogeneous inherited materials → systematic synthesis

Systematic theology can therefore be understood partly as a compression and reconciliation process operating on historically heterogeneous data. This point is especially important for later model comparison. The existence of a sophisticated theological synthesis does not by itself show that the underlying materials originated from a unified supernatural source. A mature system can produce coherence retrospectively. The direction of explanation may be:

Causal pattern: historical diversity → later theological harmonization

rather than:

Causal pattern: original perfect unity → surface historical variation

This distinction will matter when considering progressive revelation and accommodation. The social model can also explain why later members of a tradition experience its development as internally coherent. Path dependence means that each generation interprets new developments through inherited categories. Innovations that survive are gradually integrated into the system. Once integration is complete, the innovation can appear natural or inevitable. Thus:

Causal pattern: innovation → controversy → selection → institutionalization → retrospective continuity

What was once controversial becomes traditional. What was once novel becomes ancient. What was once one interpretation among several becomes “what the religion has always taught.” This process is not unique to religion. It occurs whenever institutions reconstruct their histories from the standpoint of successful outcomes. But in religion, the process can acquire theological significance. Successful doctrinal development may be interpreted as the unfolding of divine guidance. The systems model therefore predicts not only doctrinal development but also retrospective narratives explaining that development. This is crucial because those retrospective narratives should not automatically be treated as independent confirmation of the theological interpretation. A tradition may say:

The doctrine developed because God progressively revealed it.

The systems model asks a prior question:

What social processes generated the doctrine and the later interpretation that its development was providential?

From Religious History to Revelation Itself

This brings the analysis to a central conceptual transition. So far, claims of revelation have largely appeared as objects within the religious system. But theological accounts often treat revelation as an external input:

Causal pattern: God → revelation → religious tradition

The causal model developed here suggests another possibility. Revelatory claims may themselves be generated, interpreted, selected, and stabilized through the same system:

Causal pattern: religious system → revelatory claim

This does not yet establish that no revelation occurred. It changes the burden of explanation. Before treating revelation as the source of the religious system, we must ask whether revelation claims themselves can be understood as endogenous outputs of that system. That question becomes particularly important because human societies do not produce one isolated revelation claim. They produce populations of them. They generate prophetic visions, mystical experiences, divine commands, miracle traditions, sacred books, charismatic founders, and claims of unique supernatural authority across mutually incompatible traditions.

The next section therefore turns from the historical content of religion to revelation itself. The central question is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Should revelation be treated as an external cause of religious systems, or first as one of the recurring phenomena those systems generate?} } \]

XII. Revelation Claims as Endogenous Variables

The previous sections treated religious traditions as multilevel cultural systems whose stories, doctrines, institutions, supernatural ontologies, and metaphysical concepts develop through identifiable historical processes. This raises a further question: where should claims of revelation themselves be located within the causal model? Theological accounts often treat revelation as an exogenous input. Something outside the ordinary social system acts upon it:

\[ G \rightarrow R \rightarrow \text{religious tradition}, \]

where \(G\) represents a divine agent and \(R\) a revelation. On this model, revelation explains why the religion exists. The religious tradition is downstream from supernatural disclosure. But from the standpoint of comparative explanation, revelation claims can also be treated as endogenous variables: recurring outputs produced within religious systems and shaped by the same cognitive, cultural, institutional, and historical mechanisms already described. The alternative structure is:

\[ M \rightarrow R, \]

where \(M\) is the broader social-historical system. This does not assume in advance that every revelatory experience is non-supernatural. It establishes a methodological priority. Before using revelation to explain religion, we should first ask whether the production, content, interpretation, acceptance, and survival of revelation claims can themselves be explained. That question becomes unavoidable because human societies do not produce one isolated revelation claim. They produce entire populations of them. Let:

\[ \mathcal{R} = \{R_1,R_2,R_3,\ldots,R_n\} \]

represent the population of historical claims to supernatural disclosure. These include:

  • prophetic speech;
  • dreams;
  • visions;
  • auditions;
  • angelic encounters;
  • divine commands;
  • mystical experiences;
  • scriptural dictation;
  • miracle claims;
  • charismatic revelations;
  • apocalyptic journeys;
  • claims of unique religious authority.

These phenomena arise across traditions that make mutually incompatible claims about supernatural reality. The explanatory question is therefore not merely:

Why did Moses, Paul, Muhammad, Joseph Smith, or some other individual report revelation?

It is broader:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What process generates populations of revelation claims with culturally patterned content?} } \]

A systems approach suggests that revelatory claims can emerge through interaction among several variables.

Micro-Level Generation and Cultural Interpretation

At the micro level, individuals may undergo unusual or emotionally powerful experiences:

\[ E= \{\text{dream, vision, voice, trance, mystical state, intuition, coincidence}\}. \]

These experiences do not by themselves uniquely determine their theological interpretation. Instead:

\[ E + C_t + B_t \rightarrow R_i, \]

where \(C_t\) is the surrounding conceptual ecology and \(B_t\) represents prior beliefs and expectations. A striking dream interpreted within one religious tradition may become a message from an angel. In another context, it may be understood as ancestral communication, possession, divine incarnation, karmic insight, or contact with a saint. The claim is not that all such experiences are phenomenologically identical. It is that their interpretation is constrained by available cultural categories. Thus the content of revelation is expected to show cultural indexing. A Christian visionary is more likely to report Christ, Mary, saints, angels, or demons than deities from an unrelated tradition. A Muslim revelatory framework is more likely to interpret extraordinary experience through Allah, angels, prophets, jinn, or Qur'anic categories.

A religious environment supplies the symbolic vocabulary through which the experience becomes intelligible. This suggests:

\[ P(R_i\mid C_i) > P(R_i\mid C_j) \]

when the content of revelation \(R_i\) is culturally native to environment \(C_i\) rather than \(C_j\). If such dependence is strong, revelation claims begin to resemble other culturally mediated outputs.

Selection, Validation, and Institutional Survival

The meso level then determines which claims become socially consequential. Most private religious experiences do not create durable religions. A claim becomes historically important only if it passes through processes of recognition and selection:

Causal pattern: experience → claim → audience reception → institutional selection → transmission

At each stage, most candidates disappear. A revelatory claim may fail because no one believes it. Another may remain confined to a small group. Another may generate a sect. A few may become foundational narratives of large traditions. The social career of revelation can therefore be represented as:

\[ R_i \rightarrow \begin{cases} \text{rejection}\\ \text{private memory}\\ \text{local cult}\\ \text{sectarian authority}\\ \text{canonical revelation} \end{cases} \]

The final status of a revelation is therefore not determined solely by the original experience. It depends upon social validation. This matters because later believers often encounter only the successful endpoint. A revelation that survived centuries of institutional selection may appear uniquely powerful or self-authenticating. But its survival may partly reflect:

  • charismatic authority;
  • network centrality;
  • political sponsorship;
  • missionary success;
  • demographic expansion;
  • institutional organization;
  • textual preservation;
  • educational reproduction.

The historical authority of revelation is therefore partly a property of its social trajectory. The system can even become self-reinforcing. Consider:

Causal pattern: revelation claim → community → institution → preservation of revelation

Later:

Causal pattern: institutional continuity → perceived confirmation of revelation

The result is a feedback loop:

\[ R \rightarrow I \rightarrow R^\ast, \]

where \(R^\ast\) is the revelation interpreted through the authority accumulated by the institution it helped generate. The same process can operate with scripture. A text is regarded as revealed. Because it is revealed, it is copied. Because it is copied, it survives. Because it survives, it becomes culturally central. Because it becomes central, later generations encounter its persistence as evidence of divine preservation. Thus:

Causal pattern: claimed revelation → preservation → longevity → retrospective evidential weight

The social model explains why this loop can arise even without assuming supernatural intervention. Again, this does not show that the revelation is false. It shows that some of the evidence later used to support it may be causally downstream from the original belief in its authority.

Historical Conditions and Revelatory Content

The macro level can also influence revelation production. Periods of political collapse, foreign occupation, social marginalization, rapid cultural change, or institutional crisis often create environments in which prophetic and apocalyptic movements become especially salient. A simplified relation might be:

Causal pattern: social crisis + existing eschatological expectations + charismatic interpretation → new revelatory movement

This does not mean crisis mechanically produces prophecy. It means that historical environments alter the probability and form of religious innovation. The content of revelation may also respond to the needs of the community. A defeated people may receive revelations explaining defeat as divine punishment. A persecuted community may receive visions promising imminent reversal. A reform movement may receive revelation condemning existing institutions. A movement experiencing expansion may interpret success as divine confirmation. Thus revelation claims can be adaptive responses within social systems:

Causal pattern: historical condition → religious problem → revelatory interpretation

This helps explain why revelations so often address the immediate conceptual and social concerns of the communities in which they arise.

The Epistemic Consequence

The systems approach therefore changes how revelation is treated epistemically. Instead of beginning with:

\[ R \rightarrow \text{explanation of religion}, \]

we begin with:

\[ \text{religious system} \rightarrow \text{production and selection of }R. \]

Only after that causal analysis do we ask whether a further supernatural variable improves the explanation. This is analogous to a general principle in causal inference: one should not treat an observed variable as an external cause until its own generating process has been considered. Revelation claims are especially important because they are often treated as epistemically privileged. A prophet says that God revealed a proposition. The claim then functions as evidence for the very theological framework required to interpret the claim. This can produce a circular structure:

Causal pattern: religion validates revelation → revelation validates religion

A comparative framework breaks that circle by placing both within the same explanatory system. The question becomes:

Why does this community recognize this experience as revelation while another community recognizes a conflicting experience as revelation?

And then:

What variables predict those differences?

If cultural background, social network, institutional authority, prior theology, and historical circumstance explain much of the variation, revelation begins to look less like an independent informational input and more like an internally generated state. This suggests a useful distinction between phenomenological sincerity and causal independence. A person may sincerely experience what they regard as divine communication. But sincerity does not establish that the content entered the system independently of its cultural and cognitive structure. Thus, sincerity ⇏ exogenous supernatural causation. This is especially important because competing traditions can contain sincere believers reporting incompatible revelations. The systems problem is therefore not solved by pointing to the intensity of conviction. Intensity itself becomes another variable requiring explanation.

The Population-Level Model

The broader religious ecology now looks something like:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{conceptual ecology}\\ &+\text{individual experience}\\ &+\text{social interpretation}\\ &+\text{institutional selection}\\ &+\text{network diffusion}\\ &+\text{historical reinforcement} \\ &\rightarrow \text{stable revelatory tradition}. \end{aligned} \]

If this process can generate the phenomena ordinarily cited as revelation, then the theological hypothesis must do more than reproduce the same observations. It must explain some residual structure that the social model does not. This becomes especially clear when we turn to Christianity itself. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that Christian revelation is granted at the beginning of the analysis. That concession would still leave most of Christian history unexplained. The revelation alone would not explain the canon, doctrinal disputes, Christological diversity, denominational fragmentation, geographic clustering, philosophical development, institutional structures, or the continuing production of incompatible revelatory claims within Christianity. To explain those, one must reintroduce the very social-historical system already developed.

This produces an important explanatory asymmetry. The next section examines it directly:

\[ \boxed{ R \text{ cannot explain the actual religious ecology without } M. } \]

The remaining question is whether the converse is also true:

\[ \boxed{ \text{How much of the religious ecology can } M \text{ explain without } R? } \]

XIII. Christianity Cannot Explain Its Own Religious Ecology

The previous section treated revelation claims as endogenous phenomena that can themselves be generated, interpreted, selected, and stabilized within religious systems. This raises a further explanatory problem for any particular revelation hypothesis: even if one grants the foundational revelation, the revelation alone is not sufficient to explain the later religion. Christianity provides a particularly clear example. Let:

\[ H_C = \text{the hypothesis that Christian revelation is substantially true}. \]

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that one grants some strong version of this hypothesis. Perhaps Jesus genuinely possessed divine authority. Perhaps the resurrection occurred. Perhaps the earliest Christian proclamation was grounded in authentic supernatural disclosure. Even granting all of this would not explain the historical phenomenon called Christianity. Christianity is not reducible to \(H_C\); it is a vast religious ecology containing:

  • competing Christologies;
  • rival apostolic traditions;
  • canonical and noncanonical texts;
  • episcopal institutions;
  • doctrinal councils;
  • monastic movements;
  • philosophical schools;
  • liturgical traditions;
  • schisms;
  • regional churches;
  • missionary networks;
  • Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, Coptic, Syriac, and other traditions;
  • revival movements;
  • charismatic claims;
  • denominations;
  • theological reinterpretations;
  • and continuing disputes about what Christianity itself requires.

The explanandum is therefore not merely Why did Christianity begin? It is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Why does Christianity have the historical structure that it actually has?} } \]

A foundational revelation, even if genuine, explains comparatively little of that structure by itself. To see the problem, consider the diversity of early Christianity. The earliest Christian movement did not immediately generate one universally agreed theological system. Different communities emphasized different aspects of Jesus' identity, Jewish law, eschatology, apostolic authority, ritual practice, and scripture. The surviving New Testament itself preserves meaningful differences in theological emphasis among Paul, the Synoptic Gospels, John, Hebrews, James, Revelation, and other writings. Later centuries intensified rather than eliminated the need for interpretation. Christians disputed:

  • the relationship between Jesus' humanity and divinity;
  • the relation of Father, Son, and Spirit;
  • the nature of salvation;
  • free will and grace;
  • the authority of bishops;
  • the boundaries of scripture;
  • the proper interpretation of the Old Testament;
  • the relation between church and empire;
  • the meaning of sacraments;
  • the nature of Christ's presence in the Eucharist;
  • the role of images;
  • the authority of councils;
  • the nature of justification.

A bare revelation hypothesis does not predict which side of these controversies will prevail. To explain that, we need institutions, social networks, political conditions, linguistic communities, intellectual traditions, and historical contingency. Thus: \(H_C \not\Rightarrow \mathbf{D}_C\), where \(\mathbf{D}_C\) is the observable historical ecology of Christianity. At minimum:

\[ H_C + M \rightarrow \mathbf{D}_C, \]

where \(M\) is the social-historical model developed in the earlier sections. This is an important asymmetry. The revelation hypothesis does not replace the social model. It depends upon it.

Canon Formation

Consider the Christian canon. If Christianity were explained simply by a foundational revelation, one might imagine that the authoritative textual corpus would be a straightforward consequence of that revelation. Historically, however, the process was far more complex. Different Christian communities used different texts. Some writings eventually became universally canonical. Others remained locally authoritative. Some were disputed for centuries. Others disappeared. Still others were condemned. The final canonical structure emerged through processes involving:

  • liturgical use;
  • claims of apostolic connection;
  • theological compatibility;
  • institutional authority;
  • geographic circulation;
  • copying practices;
  • controversy;
  • episcopal judgment;
  • historical survival.

A revelation hypothesis may explain why Christians believed certain texts mattered. It does not by itself explain why this particular set of texts survived and became authoritative. That requires: text production + network diffusion + institutional selection + historical survival. The canon is therefore a product of both religious conviction and social selection. Even if some texts were divinely inspired, one still requires the social model to explain the historical process by which they became the canon.

Doctrinal Development

The same applies to doctrine. Christianity did not begin with the full conceptual vocabulary later used to describe the Trinity, incarnation, divine simplicity, sacramental theology, or Christological union. These doctrines emerged through controversy and conceptual refinement. A theological defender can say that the doctrines were always implicitly contained in revelation. But this does not explain why their articulation took the historical route it did. Why did certain controversies become decisive? Why did particular philosophical categories become authoritative? Why did one terminology prevail over another? Why did different linguistic communities sometimes understand the same doctrine differently? Why did doctrinal settlements require councils, creeds, imperial enforcement, and institutional repetition? These are not questions that the original revelation answers by itself. The historical form of doctrine requires:

Causal pattern: revelatory tradition + philosophical vocabulary + controversy + institutional arbitration → orthodox formulation

Again:

\[ H_C \text{ is insufficient without }M. \]

Geographic and Institutional Divergence

The structure of Christianity also displays strong geographic clustering. Different regions developed distinct ecclesial traditions. Language mattered. Greek-speaking Christianity developed differently from Latin-speaking Christianity. Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, and other Christian traditions developed distinctive liturgical and theological profiles. Political boundaries mattered. Imperial administration mattered. Missionary routes mattered. Later, the Protestant Reformation generated further divergence strongly correlated with political geography, printing, state structures, universities, and local institutional networks. If Christianity were explained primarily by one true revelation, then these differences remain unexplained until the social model is added. The observed pattern is better represented as:

Causal pattern: shared theological inheritance + different network positions + different institutions + different political environments → divergent Christian traditions

This is exactly the pattern expected from the causal system described earlier. The revelation hypothesis alone has very little to say about it.

Competing Revelations Within Christianity

The explanatory problem becomes even sharper when Christianity continues producing new revelation claims internally. Throughout Christian history, individuals and movements have reported:

  • visions of Jesus;
  • Marian apparitions;
  • prophetic messages;
  • angelic encounters;
  • charismatic revelations;
  • apocalyptic predictions;
  • new interpretations supposedly delivered by the Holy Spirit;
  • supernatural confirmations of competing doctrines.

These claims frequently contradict one another. Some become institutionally accepted. Others become marginal. Others generate new denominations. Others are condemned as heretical. Thus even within a religion that already claims divine revelation, the system continues to generate additional revelation claims:

\[ H_C \rightarrow \{R_1,R_2,R_3,\ldots,R_n\}. \]

But \(H_C\) does not tell us which of these later claims should appear historically, where they will arise, or which will survive. Again we require: \(M\). The system's ongoing production of revelation therefore cannot simply be explained by pointing back to the original revelation. It remains a population-level phenomenon.

Christianity as a Religious Ecology

This suggests that Christianity is better represented not as a line descending from one revelation but as a dynamic ecology:

\[ \mathbf{C}_t = F( H_C, I_t, N_t, P_t, C_t, E_t, S_t, \Phi_t, \epsilon_t ). \]

Here the revelation hypothesis, even if retained, is only one variable among many. The actual historical form of Christianity depends on:

  • institutions;
  • network structure;
  • political power;
  • inherited conceptual systems;
  • new experiences;
  • selection pressures;
  • feedback;
  • contingency.

This changes the explanatory comparison. The issue is no longer:

\[ H_C \quad \text{versus} \quad M. \]

The actual competition is:

\[ M \quad \text{versus} \quad M+H_C. \]

The social model is required in both. That is the crucial point.

The Asymmetry Between \(M\) and \(H_{R}\)

A revelation hypothesis cannot explain the full religious system without ordinary causal mechanisms. But the social model may be able to explain a large portion of the system without revelation. Thus:

\[ H_C + M \rightarrow \mathbf{D}_C \]

is necessary for the theological account. The key question becomes:

\[ M \stackrel{?}{\longrightarrow} \mathbf{D}_C. \]

If the answer is largely yes, then the explanatory contribution of \(H_C\) becomes narrower. This is not merely a matter of parsimony. It is a question of marginal explanatory contribution. Let \(\Delta E_R\) represent the additional explanatory gain from adding revelation to the social model. Then the issue becomes:

\[ \Delta E_R = E(M+H_{R})-E(M). \]

If \(\Delta E_R\) is small, then revelation is doing little explanatory work even if it remains metaphysically possible. This is especially significant because \(M\) explains not merely Christianity. The same mechanisms explain:

  • Judaism;
  • Islam;
  • Mormonism;
  • new religious movements;
  • sectarian fragmentation;
  • charismatic prophecy;
  • canon formation;
  • doctrinal change;
  • institutional stabilization;
  • missionary diffusion.

The social model therefore possesses broad cross-tradition generality. By contrast, a particular revelation hypothesis is usually tradition-specific. Christian revelation explains, at most, Christianity. Islamic revelation explains, at most, Islam. Mormon revelation explains, at most, Mormonism. But the social model explains why all of them generate similar classes of phenomena. This produces an asymmetry in scope:

\[ \text{scope}(M) > \text{scope}(H_C). \]

The comparison becomes even more striking when mutually incompatible revelation hypotheses require the same social model. For Christianity:

\[ M+H_C. \]

For Islam:

\[ M+H_I. \]

For Mormonism:

\[ M+H_M. \]

And so on. The variable common to all cases is: \(M\). The revelation variable changes according to the tradition. This suggests that much of the observable religious structure is explained by the shared causal machinery rather than the competing supernatural claims.

The Explanatory Residual

This does not establish that revelation contributes nothing. It defines the residual that revelation must explain. Suppose the social model explains:

  • geographic clustering;
  • institutional formation;
  • canonical selection;
  • doctrinal development;
  • sectarian divergence;
  • philosophical adaptation;
  • revelation production;
  • conversion patterns;
  • cultural inheritance.

Then what remains? A revelation hypothesis must identify some feature: \(E^\ast\) such that:

\[ P(E^\ast \mid M+H_C) \gg P(E^\ast \mid M). \]

Without such an evidential residual, revelation risks becoming explanatorily superfluous. This is why appealing to the mere existence of Christianity is insufficient. The social model already explains why religious movements arise. Appealing to Christianity's longevity is insufficient. Institutions and demographic transmission explain persistence. Appealing to theological sophistication is insufficient. Intellectual traditions generate sophistication. Appealing to internal coherence is insufficient. Historical systems can produce retrospective synthesis. Appealing to religious experience is insufficient. Competing traditions produce analogous experiences. The revelation hypothesis needs something more discriminating.

The result can be stated simply:

\[ \boxed{ H_{R} \text{ cannot explain the religious ecology without } M. } \]

The harder question is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Does } M \text{ need } H_{R} \text{ to explain the religious ecology?} } \]

That is the point at which the argument becomes explicitly comparative. The next section therefore performs the counterfactual test. Instead of asking whether revelation can be made compatible with religious history, we ask what kind of religious world we would expect if no special revelation were occurring at all, and then compare that expectation with the world we actually observe. That comparison provides the bridge from causal description to model evaluation.

XIV. Counterfactual Test: What Would Religion Look Like Without Revelation?

The previous section established an asymmetry. A revelation hypothesis cannot explain the actual historical form of Christianity without the social-historical model, because revelation alone does not determine canon formation, doctrinal development, institutional structure, geographic variation, sectarian fragmentation, philosophical transformation, or the continued production of competing revelation claims. The social model remains necessary. The next question is therefore counterfactual:

What would the religious world look like if no special revelation were occurring at all?

This question is central because explanatory models should not be evaluated merely by whether they can accommodate the observations after the fact. They should also be evaluated by what kinds of observations they lead us to expect. Let:

\[ M = \text{the social-historical generative model} \]

and let:

\[ H_{R} = \text{a substantive hypothesis of special revelation}. \]

The relevant counterfactual worlds are therefore approximately:

\[ W_M = \text{a world in which religion develops through ordinary social-historical mechanisms} \]

and:

\[ W_{M+H_{R}} = \text{a world in which those same mechanisms operate, but genuine special revelation also occurs}. \]

The central evidential question is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{How different should the observable religious systems in }W_M\text{ and }W_{M+H_{R}}\text{ be?} } \]

If the two models lead us to expect substantially different evidence, then religious history can discriminate between them. If they predict almost the same observable world, then historical evidence will provide little reason to prefer the revelatory model.

Expectations Under the Social-Historical Model

Begin with the world in which no special revelation occurs. Suppose religions arise through the mechanisms already identified:

  • individual experience and interpretation;
  • social learning;
  • intergenerational transmission;
  • prestige and conformity;
  • institutional selection;
  • network diffusion;
  • political power;
  • cultural contact;
  • conceptual inheritance;
  • feedback;
  • path dependence;
  • demographic reproduction;
  • philosophical innovation.

What sort of religious world should we expect? First, we should expect geographic clustering. If religious beliefs are transmitted primarily through families, communities, institutions, and local networks, then people living in the same social environment should disproportionately share religious beliefs. Thus:

\[ P(B_i \mid G_i) > P(B_i \mid G_j) \]

when belief \(B_i\) is culturally dominant in environment \(G_i\) rather than \(G_j\). The religious map of the world should therefore reflect migration, conquest, missionary history, imperial boundaries, and local institutions. Second, we should expect intergenerational continuity. Children should disproportionately acquire the religious system of their parents and surrounding community. That produces:

\[ P(R_{\text{child}}=R_{\text{parent}}) \gg P(R_{\text{child}}=\text{random alternative}). \]

Conversion should occur, but it should be superimposed upon a strong inherited baseline. Third, we should expect historical path dependence. Later religions should resemble earlier religions from which they developed. Christianity should look recognizably related to Judaism. Islam should display historically intelligible relationships to Judaism, Christianity, Arabian religion, and late antique monotheism. Later Christian denominations should preserve structures inherited from earlier Christianity. A new religion emerging in nineteenth-century America should contain concepts recognizable within nineteenth-century American religious culture. Thus:

\[ R_{t+1} \not\perp R_t. \]

The later religious state should not be statistically independent of the earlier one. Fourth, we should expect network-sensitive similarity. Traditions in sustained contact should exchange concepts more frequently than historically isolated traditions. The probability of shared religious material should therefore be sensitive to network structure:

\[ P(S_{ij}) = f(w_{ij}), \]

where \(S_{ij}\) represents some degree of conceptual similarity and \(w_{ij}\) the intensity of historical contact. This need not produce simple convergence. High contact can also generate opposition, but either outcome should correlate with interaction. Fifth, we should expect syncretism and recombination. New religious systems should often combine inherited materials from multiple sources rather than appearing completely unprecedented. Thus: new religious state = inheritance + recombination + innovation. Religious creativity should resemble cultural creativity more generally. Sixth, we should expect translation effects. When religions move across languages, theological concepts should change at least partly because languages carve conceptual space differently. Translation should generate interpretive consequences:

Causal pattern: linguistic transition → conceptual shift → theological consequence

Seventh, we should expect institutional selection. The doctrines and texts that survive should not necessarily be those that were originally most common, philosophically strongest, or historically earliest. Survival should depend partly on:

  • institutional backing;
  • copying;
  • political protection;
  • network centrality;
  • educational reproduction;
  • demographic success.

Thus: historical survival ≠ truth-tracking selection by default. Eighth, we should expect competing revelation claims. If human cognition, social crisis, charismatic authority, mystical experience, and interpretive traditions can generate claims of supernatural disclosure, then many such claims should arise. Moreover, because they are generated under different conceptual ecologies, their contents should conflict. Thus:

\[ \mathcal{R} = \{R_1,R_2,\ldots,R_n\} \]

should contain: \(R_i \land R_j\) that cannot both be true in their substantive claims. Ninth, we should expect revelation content to be culturally indexed. Prophets and mystics should generally speak using conceptual resources available to their societies. We should expect:

\[ P(\text{locally familiar supernatural content}\mid M) \]

to be relatively high. Tenth, we should expect doctrinal evolution. Religious systems should not remain perfectly static. New political problems, philosophical criticism, foreign contact, scientific developments, demographic shifts, and moral change should create pressure for reinterpretation. Thus:

\[ R_t \rightarrow R_{t+1} \]

should often involve both continuity and modification. Eleventh, we should expect sect formation and fragmentation. If texts require interpretation and institutions compete for authority, then a sufficiently large religious movement should frequently generate internal disagreement. Thus:

Causal pattern: shared tradition + interpretive ambiguity + distributed authority → sectarian differentiation

Twelfth, we should expect retrospective harmonization. Successful traditions should reinterpret their histories in ways that make contingent developments appear coherent. Thus:

Causal pattern: historical heterogeneity → later systematic theology

Finally, we should expect religious beliefs to respond to the same kinds of large-scale events that influence other cultural systems: war, migration, empire, economic change, technology, philosophy, education. In short, under \(M\) we should expect religion to look deeply social. The striking point is that this hypothetical world looks remarkably similar to the one we observe. The broad empirical pattern described earlier is:

\[ \begin{aligned} E_{\text{obs}} = \{ &\text{geographic clustering},\\ &\text{intergenerational transmission},\\ &\text{genealogical continuity},\\ &\text{network dependence},\\ &\text{syncretism},\\ &\text{translation effects},\\ &\text{institutional selection},\\ &\text{competing revelations},\\ &\text{doctrinal evolution},\\ &\text{sectarian fragmentation},\\ &\text{retrospective harmonization},\\ &\text{historical path dependence} \}. \end{aligned} \]

These are not anomalies for the social model. They are approximately what it predicts. This gives the model a substantial advantage before any individual revelation claim is examined.

What Should We Expect Under Special Revelation?

The revelatory counterfactual requires more care. A weak or highly underspecified hypothesis such as:

\[ R_0 = \text{“some deity sometimes influences religious history”} \]

predicts very little. Almost any observable religious world could be compatible with it. Such a hypothesis is therefore difficult to test precisely because it places few constraints on the data. But major revelatory religions generally make stronger claims than this. They often assert something like:

\[ H_{R}^{\ast} = \text{“God has specially disclosed important truths through this historical tradition.”} \]

Some versions additionally imply that the revelation is:

  • uniquely authoritative;
  • reliably preserved;
  • intended to guide human beings;
  • sufficiently clear for major theological purposes;
  • historically privileged relative to rival traditions;
  • authentically connected to a divine source.

These stronger hypotheses create expectations. The exact expectations will differ by theology, and it would be a mistake to impose one universal model of revelation upon all religious traditions. Nevertheless, the general question remains:

If one tradition possesses unusually successful supernatural disclosure, what empirical signal should distinguish it from traditions generated entirely through ordinary cultural mechanisms?

Several possibilities are available.

Candidate Discriminating Expectations Under Revelation

Reduced dependence on local cultural conditions. A genuine revelatory signal might display some degree of independence from the conceptual environment into which it appears. This need not mean that revelation uses no local language or imagery. Communication necessarily requires some cultural mediation. But one might expect a meaningful informational residue:

\[ I_R = \text{information not strongly predicted by the local cultural environment}. \]

If every substantial feature of the revelation is tightly continuous with existing conceptual resources, then it becomes difficult to identify what part of the content entered from outside the system. The relevant comparison is:

\[ P(I_R\mid M+H_{R}) > P(I_R\mid M). \]

Cross-Cultural Convergence. If a deity intends to disclose the same important supernatural truths to humanity, one possible expectation is convergence across populations that lack ordinary contact. Again, this need not imply identical religions everywhere. But genuine independent access to the same external supernatural reality could potentially generate some common informational structure beyond what shared psychology alone predicts. We might therefore look for: cross-cultural convergence - convergence explained by contact or common cognition. A strong residual could favor revelation. The observed pattern, however, often appears strongly correlated with historical connection.

Stability of Core Supernatural Knowledge. If revelation communicates objective truths about God, the afterlife, supernatural beings, or salvation, we might expect at least some central propositions to remain unusually stable across the history of the revealed tradition. A tradition may still develop, but the foundational informational content should display some identifiable resistance to ordinary conceptual drift. Thus:

\[ P(\text{core stability}\mid M+H_{R}) > P(\text{core stability}\mid M) \]

under some revelation models. The historical cases examined earlier place pressure on this expectation precisely because changes occur at increasingly deep levels: narrative, theological reasoning, deity concept, supernatural ontology, and metaphysics.

Reliable Information Beyond Contemporary Access. Another potentially discriminating feature would be accurate knowledge that historical actors were unlikely to possess through ordinary means. Such evidence would have a structure like:

\[ K^\ast = \text{reliable information inaccessible to the relevant culture}. \]

Then:

\[ P(K^\ast\mid M+H_{R}) \gg P(K^\ast\mid M). \]

This would constitute genuine informational surplus. Claims of this kind are often made apologetically, but they require careful independent evaluation precisely because they could provide stronger evidence than mere compatibility with ordinary cultural history.

Resistance to Ordinary Transmission Biases. If a revelation is intended to communicate essential truths broadly, one might also expect some resistance to the extreme dependence of religious identity on birthplace, family, political history, and network position. The issue is not that true ideas should spread instantly or universally. Rather, the question is whether the distribution of access to purported revelation should be almost entirely explainable by ordinary transmission. If:

\[ P(\text{acceptance of revelation}) \]

is overwhelmingly predicted by family, geography, language, and network position, then the revelatory signal appears weak relative to the social signal.

Successful Novel Predictions. A revelation hypothesis could also distinguish itself through predictions not available from the social model. Suppose a religious claim specifies: \(E^\ast\) before the relevant evidence is available and \(E^\ast\) would be unlikely under \(M\). Then:

\[ P(E^\ast\mid M+H_{R}) \gg P(E^\ast\mid M) \]

could provide strong discriminating evidence. This is methodologically much more valuable than retrospectively finding ways to interpret whatever happened as fulfillment.

The Point Is Not to Demand a Particular Miracle

The counterfactual argument does not require asserting that a true God must reveal religion in one particular way. That would simply replace one overly rigid model with another. The point is more modest and more general. If \(M\) and

\[ M+H_{R} \]

are genuinely different explanatory models, then there should ideally exist some observations for which:

\[ P(E\mid M+H_{R}) \neq P(E\mid M). \]

Otherwise the additional variable has little empirical consequence. If every feature of religious history that looks socially generated is explained by saying that God intentionally chose to work through exactly those social mechanisms, then the two models approach observational equivalence. That is:

\[ P(E\mid M+H_{R}) \approx P(E\mid M) \]

for nearly all observable religious evidence \(E\). At that point the revelatory component may remain metaphysically possible, but religious history itself supplies little basis for identifying it.

Counterfactual Asymmetry

The most important result of the exercise can now be stated. Under the social model, many of the major features of the observed religious world—clustering, inheritance, historical change, syncretism, competing revelation, institutional filtering, and conceptual dependence—are positive expectations. The model does not merely survive these observations; it generates them. By contrast, a revelation model frequently responds to the same observations by showing that they are permitted: God may use cultural forms, reveal progressively, allow doctrinal diversity, work through institutions, permit geographical inequality, use foreign philosophy, or remain epistemically hidden. These are possible responses, but there is an important difference between “the model predicts this” and “the model can be adjusted to allow this.”

That distinction is the heart of the counterfactual test. The question is not:

Could God produce a religious world like this?

Almost certainly, given a sufficiently flexible account of divine intention. The question is:

Would this particular religious world have been especially expected if one tradition were the product of uniquely successful special revelation?

And conversely:

Would this religious world have been surprising if religion were generated predominantly through ordinary social and historical mechanisms?

The answer to the second question appears to be no. The world we observe is very close to the world the social model leads us to expect. This does not yet establish that \(M\) should be preferred overall. Model comparison requires more than one counterfactual intuition. We must compare explanatory scope, independent motivation, parsimony, predictive constraint, robustness, generality, and the role of auxiliary assumptions. But the counterfactual exercise establishes the central evidential tension:

\[ \boxed{ E_{\text{obs}} \text{ looks highly characteristic of }M, \text{ while the distinctive empirical signature of }R\text{ remains unclear.} } \]

The next section makes that comparison explicit. Rather than asking merely whether revelation is logically compatible with the evidence, we can compare the social and revelatory models using ordinary criteria of explanatory adequacy.

XV. Formal Model Comparison

The counterfactual analysis suggests that the broad structure of religious history is highly characteristic of a social-historical generative model. But counterfactual resemblance alone is not enough. A more disciplined comparison requires explicit criteria for evaluating competing explanatory models. Let:

\[ M = \text{the multilevel social-historical model} \]

and let:

\[ H_{R} = \text{a substantive special-revelation hypothesis}. \]

Model-comparison criteria at a glance

Criterion The question it asks
Explanatory scope How much of the total religious ecology does the model explain?
Independent motivation Are the proposed mechanisms supported outside the case they are used to explain?
Parsimony Does added complexity purchase genuine explanatory gain?
Explanatory compression Can a small set of mechanisms explain many different observations?
Predictive constraint What plausible outcomes does the model make less likely?
Counterfactual power What should change if a relevant causal variable changes?
Cross-tradition generality Does the model work across competing religions rather than one tradition alone?
Robustness Does the explanation survive uncertainty in individual historical cases?
Novel prediction Does the model generate expectations beyond the examples used to construct it?
Overfitting control Do auxiliary assumptions increase prediction, or merely preserve compatibility?

Because revelation by itself does not explain the actual historical ecology of a religion, the more realistic theological model is not \(H_{R}\) alone but \(M+H_{R}\). This matters: the comparison is not between a social explanation and a theological explanation that somehow replaces social causation. Both sides require the ordinary historical mechanisms. The question is whether \(H_{R}\) adds enough explanatory value to justify its inclusion. The relevant comparison is therefore:

\[ M \quad\text{versus}\quad M+H_{R}. \]

This changes the evidential burden. The revelation hypothesis is not being asked merely whether it can coexist with the data. It must explain why adding \(H_{R}\) improves our account of the total religious system. A useful way to approach this is through several standard explanatory criteria.

Coverage and Explanatory Economy

Explanatory scope. The first criterion is scope. A theory with broad explanatory scope accounts for many different features of the evidence using a common framework. The social model attempts to explain:

  • geographic clustering;
  • intergenerational transmission;
  • doctrinal change;
  • network diffusion;
  • syncretism;
  • sect formation;
  • canonization;
  • revelation production;
  • institutional persistence;
  • philosophical adaptation;
  • supernatural conceptual change;
  • religious fragmentation;
  • historical convergence and divergence.

Thus:

\[ \text{Scope}(M) = \text{large}. \]

A specific revelation hypothesis is usually much narrower. For example:

\[ H_C = \text{Christian revelation is true} \]

may attempt to explain why Christianity exists or why some core Christian claims are true. But it does not by itself explain:

  • Islam;
  • Mormonism;
  • Hindu revelation traditions;
  • competing Christian visions;
  • Buddhist cosmological claims;
  • new religious movements;
  • or the broader population of religious experiences and institutions.

Thus:

\[ \text{Scope}(H_C) < \text{Scope}(M). \]

When \(H_C\) is combined with \(M\), the expanded model has broad scope—but much of that scope comes from \(M\), not from \(H_C\). The relevant question is therefore marginal:

\[ \Delta \text{Scope}_R = \text{Scope}(M+H_{R})-\text{Scope}(M). \]

If that difference is small, then revelation contributes little additional explanatory reach.

Independent Motivation. The second criterion is independent motivation. A strong explanatory mechanism should ideally have support beyond the specific phenomenon it is introduced to explain. The components of \(M\) meet this criterion unusually well. Social learning is observed throughout human behavior. Network diffusion occurs in language, politics, technology, and cultural fashion. Institutional selection occurs in law, education, science, and governance. Path dependence appears in economics, political institutions, technological standards, and language. Prestige bias, conformity, memory, migration, demographic reproduction, and political patronage are all independently established phenomena. Thus:

\[ P(M) \]

is supported by evidence far outside the study of religion. This matters because the mechanisms are not invented after the fact to save a theory of religion. By contrast, many theological auxiliaries are highly religion-specific. Suppose a defender of revelation introduces:

  • progressive revelation;
  • divine accommodation;
  • hiddenness;
  • epistemic distance;
  • providential cultural mediation;
  • permission of theological error.

Some of these ideas may possess independent theological motivation. But their evidential status is often less secure because they are themselves partly inferred from the same religious history they are then used to explain. A model risks circularity when:

\[ E \rightarrow A \]

and then:

\[ A \rightarrow \text{explanation of }E, \]

where \(A\) is an auxiliary introduced largely because \(E\) was observed. Independent motivation reduces this problem.

Parsimony. The third criterion is parsimony. Parsimony is often misunderstood as the claim that simpler theories are automatically true. That is too crude. The more defensible principle is that unnecessary causal entities and assumptions should not be added when they do not improve explanatory performance. Suppose \(M\) already explains a substantial portion of the data. Adding revelation yields:

\[ M+H_{R}. \]

If \(H_{R}\) does not increase predictive accuracy, explanatory scope, or empirical discrimination, then the enlarged model carries additional ontological or theoretical commitments without corresponding explanatory gain. This is not merely:

“God is complicated, therefore naturalism wins.”

The relevant quantity is something like:

\[ \frac{\text{explanatory gain}}{\text{additional assumptions}}. \]

If the numerator is small and the denominator large, the addition is costly.

Explanatory Compression. A closely related criterion is explanatory compression. A powerful theory explains many diverse observations through a relatively small set of recurring mechanisms. The social model has substantial compression because mechanisms such as: transmission, selection, network diffusion, path dependence, institutional reinforcement apply simultaneously to many different religions and historical contexts. The same mechanism that helps explain Christian denominational clustering can help explain Islamic sectarian clustering. The same model of charismatic revelation can be applied across multiple religious movements. The same network mechanisms explain both literary borrowing and doctrinal diffusion. Thus:

\[ M \rightarrow \{E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_n\}. \]

A tradition-specific revelation hypothesis often lacks this compression because each religion requires a different supernatural explanation:

\[ H_C, H_I, H_M, R_H, \ldots \]

for Christianity, Islam, Mormonism, Hindu traditions, and so forth. Unless one posits all of these as genuine revelations, the theological model must explain why similar religious phenomena appear under both true and false revelatory systems. At that point, the social mechanisms again do most of the explanatory work.

Constraint, Generality, and Robustness

Predictive constraint. Another major criterion is predictive constraint. A strong theory should make some observations more likely and others less likely. If a theory can accommodate virtually any possible outcome, its ability to explain the actual outcome becomes weaker. Suppose a revelation model permits:

  • doctrinal stability;
  • doctrinal change;
  • geographic concentration;
  • universal spread;
  • philosophical continuity;
  • philosophical transformation;
  • clear divine communication;
  • divine hiddenness;
  • theological unity;
  • sectarian fragmentation.

If each possible outcome can be incorporated by adjusting divine intention, then the model places very few constraints on the data. We can express predictive constraint roughly as:

\[ \text{Constraint}(H) \propto \frac{1}{\text{range of outcomes compatible with }H}. \]

This is not a literal quantitative formula, but it captures the principle. A hypothesis that rules out nothing learns little from what actually occurs. The social model does make constraints. For example, if network contact matters, then cultural exchange should correlate with contact. If intergenerational transmission matters, belief should correlate with family and community. If conceptual ecology matters, revelations should disproportionately use local symbolic systems. If institutions matter, canonical survival should correlate with institutional preservation. These are not inevitable outcomes under every imaginable social theory, but they are directional predictions. Thus \(M\) possesses genuine empirical structure.

Counterfactual Power. A strong explanation should also support counterfactual reasoning. It should tell us what would likely change if some relevant causal variable changed. For example: If a population were historically isolated from a neighboring tradition, the social model predicts reduced direct cultural exchange. If a religious movement lacked institutional support, its doctrines should be less likely to stabilize. If a new communication technology increased network connectivity, religious ideas should diffuse differently. If a translation tradition developed along a different linguistic path, later theological interpretation could differ. In general:

\[ M \rightarrow P(E\mid do(X=x)). \]

The notation here is causal rather than merely correlational: the model tells us what changes when some variable is altered. This is important because causal explanation is stronger than mere historical description. A revelation hypothesis can also have counterfactual content, but only if divine intentions are specified clearly enough. If God's purposes are left unconstrained, then counterfactual predictions become difficult:

Would God have revealed differently under another empire?

Would a different language have produced the same doctrines?

Would isolated cultures have received comparable supernatural knowledge?

Unless the theological model specifies answers independently, its counterfactual power remains weak.

Cross-Tradition Generality. Another major advantage of \(M\) is generality. The same model can be applied comparatively across religions. This is crucial because the explanandum is not one tradition in isolation. The world contains many competing religions, each exhibiting:

  • founders;
  • revelations;
  • scriptures;
  • rituals;
  • institutions;
  • schisms;
  • mystical experiences;
  • miracle claims;
  • doctrinal development.

A theory with high generality explains why these classes of phenomena recur. Thus:

\[ M(R_1),M(R_2),\ldots,M(R_n) \]

can be instantiated across many traditions. By contrast, if one religion is uniquely revealed, then the model must explain why the non-revealed religions generate so many analogous phenomena. At minimum: \(M\) is still needed to explain them. This means the comparative advantage of revelation depends on whether the supposedly true religion exhibits a detectable residual difference. Without such a residual, the true and false religions appear to be generated by the same observable machinery.

Robustness. A good explanatory model should also be robust to uncertainty in individual cases. This criterion is particularly important for the historical material discussed earlier. Suppose future scholarship weakens the case for direct dependence between the Sargon legend and Exodus 2. The broader systems model remains intact because it does not depend on that one direct transmission claim. Suppose a specific proposed Zoroastrian influence on Daniel is rejected. The larger evidence for historical development, network interaction, conceptual change, and apocalyptic evolution still remains. Thus:

\(M\) is supported by many partially independent lines of evidence. The model does not collapse if: \(E_i\) is weakened. A brittle argument would require every individual borrowing hypothesis to be correct. A robust argument requires only that the larger pattern of cultural dependence remain. This distinction is important because historical scholarship is necessarily probabilistic and revisionary.

Novel Prediction. A theory gains additional credibility if it predicts patterns not originally introduced merely to fit known examples. The social model generates several such expectations:

  • stronger cultural contact should increase conceptual exchange;
  • religious similarity should cluster along network lines;
  • revelation content should often reflect local conceptual ecologies;
  • doctrinal divergence should correlate with institutional and linguistic separation;
  • canonical stability should correlate with strong preservation institutions;
  • crises should sometimes increase apocalyptic or reformist production;
  • high-contact rival groups may show both borrowing and oppositional differentiation.

These claims can in principle be tested beyond the five historical cases. The model therefore produces a broader research program rather than a closed retrospective narrative.

Complexity and Formal Comparison

Parameter flexibility and overfitting. A final major criterion is flexibility. Models can appear to fit data extremely well simply because they contain enough adjustable parameters. In statistical modeling, an excessively flexible model can overfit: high fit to observed data + poor generalization. Something analogous can occur in theological explanation. Suppose one observes:

\[ E_1 = \text{cultural borrowing}. \]

One adds:

\[ A_1 = \text{divine accommodation}. \]

Then:

\[ E_2 = \text{doctrinal development}. \]

Add:

\[ A_2 = \text{progressive revelation}. \]

Then:

\[ E_3 = \text{religious diversity}. \]

Add:

\[ A_3 = \text{divine hiddenness or epistemic distance}. \]

Then:

\[ E_4 = \text{geographic inequality}. \]

Add:

\[ A_4 = \text{providential use of historical circumstances}. \]

The resulting model becomes:

\[ H_{R}^{\ast} = H_{R}+A_1+A_2+A_3+A_4+\cdots+A_n. \]

Its compatibility with observed history increases. But compatibility alone is not the relevant achievement. If each auxiliary is introduced after observing the problem it solves, and if the enlarged model makes few new predictions, then explanatory fit has been purchased through flexibility. The question becomes:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Does the added complexity increase predictive content, or merely protect the original claim from disconfirmation?} } \]

This does not mean that auxiliary hypotheses are illegitimate. All serious theories use auxiliaries. The social model itself does. The relevant issue is whether auxiliaries are:

  • independently motivated;
  • independently testable;
  • constrained;
  • predictively productive.

An auxiliary that satisfies those criteria can strengthen a theory. An auxiliary introduced solely to preserve compatibility may weaken it.

Bayesian Framing. The same comparison can be expressed probabilistically. We want to compare:

\[ P(M\mid E) \]

and:

\[ P(M+H_{R}\mid E), \]

where \(E\) represents the total evidence. By Bayes' theorem:

\[ P(H\mid E) \propto P(E\mid H)P(H). \]

Thus evidence favors \(M+H_{R}\) over \(M\) when:

\[ \frac{P(E\mid M+H_{R})}{P(E\mid M)} \]

is sufficiently greater than one to overcome any prior complexity or uncertainty associated with the additional revelatory hypothesis. The central problem identified throughout this essay is that much of the religious evidence appears to satisfy:

\[ P(E\mid M) \]

already quite well. Geographic clustering, historical dependence, network diffusion, conceptual evolution, and competing revelations are not surprising under \(M\). If:

\[ P(E\mid M+H_{R}) \approx P(E\mid M), \]

then the evidence contributes little likelihood advantage to revelation. This does not mean:

\[ P(R)=0. \]

It means that this particular evidence does not strongly update us toward \(H_{R}\). For revelation to gain evidential support, we need some: \(E^\ast\) such that:

\[ \frac{P(E^\ast\mid M+H_{R})} {P(E^\ast\mid M)} \gg 1. \]

That is the discriminating evidence sought throughout the argument.

The Marginal Explanatory Contribution of Revelation. We can therefore define the central question more precisely. Let:

\[ \mathcal{E}(H) \]

represent the explanatory performance of model \(H\) across the total evidence. Then the contribution of revelation is:

\[ \Delta_R = \mathcal{E}(M+H_{R})-\mathcal{E}(M). \]

The question is not whether:

\[ \mathcal{E}(M+H_{R})>0. \]

Of course it can explain something if formulated broadly enough. The question is whether: \(\Delta_R\) is large enough to justify the additional commitments introduced by \(H_{R}\). This is the central comparative issue. The historical cases discussed earlier raise doubts because many phenomena that might initially have been attributed to revelation—sacred narrative, profound theology, concepts of God, eschatology, metaphysics—turn out to possess substantial causal histories within \(M\). The explanatory space available uniquely to \(H_{R}\) becomes smaller.

Interim Comparison. The argument so far can therefore be summarized approximately as follows. The social model has several strengths:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &\text{broad scope},\\ &\text{independently motivated mechanisms},\\ &\text{cross-tradition generality},\\ &\text{counterfactual structure},\\ &\text{explanatory compression},\\ &\text{robustness},\\ &\text{testable network and transmission predictions}. \end{aligned} } \]

A revelation model may still possess strengths of its own, especially if it can identify phenomena that the social model predicts poorly. But much of the observed historical pattern cannot by itself serve this function because that pattern is already strongly expected under \(M\). This is why the strongest theological response cannot merely deny cultural mediation. It must instead explain why cultural mediation is exactly what revelation should have looked like. Two concepts become especially important here: accommodation and progressive revelation. These are not the same claim. Accommodation explains why divine communication would take culturally local forms. Progressive revelation explains why theological understanding would change over time. Combined, they provide a sophisticated response to much of the historical evidence discussed so far. The next section therefore considers these models on their strongest terms. The issue will not be whether accommodation or progressive revelation are logically possible. They clearly are. The question is whether they are independently motivated, predictively constrained, and capable of generating explanatory advantages over the social-historical model rather than merely reproducing its observed outputs.

Part VThe Strongest Theological Reply and the Evidential Test

XVI. The Strongest Theological Responses: Accommodation, Progressive Revelation, and Providence

The historical and causal framework developed so far does not imply that every theology must deny cultural mediation. In fact, sophisticated Jewish and Christian thinkers have long recognized that revelation occurs through human language, historical communities, inherited concepts, and limited cognitive capacities. Any serious comparison must therefore engage the strongest theological alternatives rather than a naïve model in which God delivers a fully articulated metaphysical system untouched by history.

The strongest theological responses at a glance

Response What it explains well The central comparative question
Accommodation Why revelation uses local language, genres, imagery, and concepts Where does culturally conditioned form end and revealed content begin?
Progressive revelation Why theological understanding changes over time What distinguishes guided disclosure from ordinary conceptual development?
Accommodation + progression Why revelation can be both culturally embedded and historically developing What would uniquely support, constrain, or falsify the combined model?
Providence through history Why ordinary social causes can mediate divine action What observable difference does divine guidance make beyond those causes?

Three responses are especially important:

  • accommodation;
  • progressive revelation;
  • providential guidance through ordinary history.

These models can also be combined. Together they provide a powerful theological explanation for why revelation might display many of the same historical features described in the preceding sections. The question is not whether these responses are logically coherent—they are—but whether they improve the explanatory model.

Accommodation and Progressive Revelation

Accommodation. The doctrine of accommodation begins from a plausible communicative principle: if a transcendent deity communicates with historically situated human beings, that communication must use concepts those people can understand. Human beings do not possess unlimited conceptual resources. A Bronze or Iron Age population cannot be expected to receive divine communication in the technical vocabulary of fourth-century Trinitarian theology, medieval scholastic metaphysics, or modern physics. Thus:

Causal pattern: divine communication + finite historical recipients → culturally accommodated revelation

On this model, revelation may employ:

  • existing literary genres;
  • familiar cosmologies;
  • anthropomorphic language;
  • local political metaphors;
  • inherited ritual forms;
  • culturally available moral concepts;
  • ordinary linguistic conventions.

This immediately explains why revelation need not appear culturally unprecedented. The Sargon-Moses comparison, for example, becomes much less threatening. A theologian can argue that if God wished to communicate the significance of Moses through familiar ancient Near Eastern narrative forms, there is nothing surprising about using a recognizable heroic-birth pattern. Similarly, biblical descriptions of God with hands, face, anger, movement, or throne imagery could be understood as accommodations to the conceptual world of ancient audiences rather than literal metaphysical descriptions. The accommodation model therefore predicts some cultural dependence. Schematically:

\[ R + C_t \rightarrow R(C_t), \]

where \(R\) is divine content and \(C_t\) is the conceptual environment through which it is expressed. This is an important improvement over a simplistic revelation model because it explicitly expects historical mediation. But accommodation introduces a difficult distinction:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Which features belong to the revelation, and which belong to the accommodation?} } \]

If ancient cosmology is accommodation, perhaps the theological core is independent of it. If anthropomorphic descriptions are accommodation, perhaps divine incorporeality lies behind them. If familiar literary motifs are accommodation, perhaps only the theological message matters. This strategy works most naturally when one can distinguish form from content. Suppose:

\[ R(C_t) = \text{unchanging content expressed through changing cultural form}. \]

Then historical variation in form does not imply variation in revealed truth. The difficulty arises when the historically variable elements appear to include substantive theological content. Consider the cases discussed earlier. In the Sargon-Moses case, accommodation can plausibly concern literary form. But in Job, the issue concerns changing conceptions of divine justice. In the historical development of YHWH, the variable includes the attributes and status of the deity. In Jewish apocalypticism, the changing content includes resurrection, supernatural beings, judgment, and the end of history. In Hellenistic Christianity, the relevant developments concern incorporeality, metaphysical simplicity, Logos theology, and the philosophical articulation of divine being. At this point the accommodation model must explain not merely why the revelation is expressed differently, but why the apparent content of supernatural knowledge changes.

The distinction becomes: cultural form versus revealed content, but historically these may be difficult to separate. Suppose an earlier community believes that the deity is embodied while a later theological system insists that God is incorporeal. Which proposition was revelation? Was bodily language merely metaphor from the beginning? Was the earlier community mistaken? Was the later metaphysical abstraction a clearer revelation? Was both language culturally relative? The more theological content is assigned to accommodation, the smaller the stable revelatory residue becomes. Thus one can imagine:

\[ R(C_t) = R_{\text{core}} + A(C_t), \]

where \(A(C_t)\) represents culturally accommodated material. But as more historically conditioned content is moved into \(A(C_t)\):

\[ |R_{\text{core}}| \downarrow. \]

This creates a potential tradeoff. Accommodation protects revelation from historical variability by narrowing what counts as revelation. Yet the narrower the stable revelatory core becomes, the less historical religious content remains available as evidence for a distinctive supernatural source.

Progressive Revelation. Accommodation and progressive revelation solve different problems. Accommodation explains why a divine message might take different cultural forms. Progressive revelation explains why the theological content itself may develop over time. The model can be stated as:

\[ R_1 \rightarrow R_2 \rightarrow R_3 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow R_n, \]

where each stage reveals more than the previous one. Earlier communities possess genuine but incomplete knowledge. Later revelation clarifies, deepens, corrects, or expands that knowledge. This provides a natural theological response to many historical developments. Perhaps early Israel knew YHWH incompletely. Later prophetic and exilic theology clarified monotheism. Second Temple Judaism received deeper knowledge about resurrection and judgment. Christian revelation disclosed truths about the incarnation and Trinity. Patristic reflection made explicit metaphysical implications previously present only implicitly. Thus: historical theological development = unfolding disclosure. Unlike a static revelation model, progressive revelation does not treat doctrinal development as embarrassing. It expects development. This makes it a much stronger competitor to the social model. But progressive revelation generates a crucial empirical problem:

\[ \boxed{ \text{How do we distinguish progressive revelation from ordinary intellectual evolution?} } \]

Both models predict change. Both can predict increasing sophistication. Both can predict later criticism of earlier conceptions. Both can produce transitions from concrete imagery toward abstraction. Both can produce new theological syntheses. The difference lies in the proposed cause. Under the social model:

\[ R_{t+1} = F( R_t, C_t, N_t, I_t, P_t, E_t, \ldots ). \]

Under progressive revelation:

\[ R_{t+1} = F( R_t, C_t, N_t, I_t, P_t, E_t, G_t, \ldots ), \]

where \(G_t\) represents divine informational input or guidance. The empirical problem is therefore to identify the contribution of: \(G_t\). If the historical trajectory can already be generated from the other variables, then the progressive-revelation model must point to some additional feature that would otherwise be unexpected. For example:

  • knowledge arriving before the relevant cultural resources existed;
  • unusually accurate theological convergence across disconnected communities;
  • reliably novel information unavailable to ordinary historical actors;
  • directional development not explained by social selection or intellectual contact.

Without such a residual, progressive revelation and cultural evolution become observationally similar.

Directionality and the Problem of Retrospective Progress. Progressive revelation also relies on a concept of progress. But what makes later theology more revealed rather than merely later? The historical sequence itself cannot answer that question. Suppose:

\[ B_1 \rightarrow B_2 \rightarrow B_3. \]

One can describe this neutrally as development. To call it progressive revelation adds:

\[ B_3 \text{ is closer to supernatural truth than }B_1. \]

But that judgment generally comes from the standpoint of the later tradition. For example, a Christian reading of biblical history may interpret earlier Israelite religion as progressively preparing for Christian revelation. A later Jewish tradition may interpret the same history differently. Islam may interpret both Judaism and Christianity as partial or corrupted stages preceding Qur'anic correction. A Latter-day Saint framework may describe earlier Christianity as incomplete or apostatized before later restoration. Each tradition can therefore construct:

Causal pattern: earlier stage → partial truth → later fulfillment

This creates a problem of retrospective directionality. A surviving tradition naturally interprets its own present as the culmination of the historical process leading to it. Thus:

Causal pattern: historical survival → retrospective narrative of progress

The danger is that “progressive revelation” becomes a theological redescrip­tion of whichever sequence happened to survive. To function as an explanatory model rather than merely a retrospective interpretation, progressive revelation should ideally specify in advance what kind of progression we ought to expect. Otherwise: whatever happened = the next stage God intended. That formulation possesses very little predictive constraint.

Accommodation and Progressive Revelation Combined. The strongest theological model combines the two. Call it:

\[ H_{R}^{\ast} = H_{R}+A+P, \]

where:

  • \(A\) = cultural accommodation;
  • \(P\) = progressive revelation.

On this account, God does not reveal all truth at once, and each stage of revelation is expressed through the cultural categories available to its recipients. This is a sophisticated model. It predicts both historical development and cultural embeddedness. An ancient Israelite community may therefore receive an accommodated and partial revelation. Later Jewish communities receive further disclosure through different cultural resources. Christianity then develops the revelation further. Greek philosophy may provide concepts through which truths implicit in earlier revelation can finally be articulated precisely. The entire historical sequence can be interpreted as:

Causal pattern: divine guidance + human cultural development → progressively clearer theology

This model can accommodate all five case studies. Sargon-like narrative structures become accommodated literary forms. Mesopotamian theological problem-spaces become contexts in which revelation deepens human understanding. Changing concepts of YHWH become stages of progressive divine self-disclosure. Iranian parallels become conceptual resources through which God introduces eschatological truths. Greek philosophy becomes providential preparation for more precise Christian metaphysics. Nothing in the historical evidence logically rules this out. Indeed, this may be the strongest general theological response available. Precisely for that reason, it should be evaluated carefully rather than dismissed.

Evaluating the Combined Model

The problem of independent motivation. The first question is whether this combined model was independently motivated. There is an important distinction between a theological principle that predicts evidence and one introduced primarily to accommodate evidence. If one independently expects God to communicate gradually through human cultural categories, then historical development provides some degree of confirmation. But if the theory becomes increasingly elaborate only after historical criticism reveals cultural dependence, then the explanatory direction is different. Suppose the historical evidence reveals:

\[ E_1 = \text{literary borrowing}. \]

The theology responds:

\[ A_1 = \text{God accommodates literary forms}. \]

Then evidence reveals:

\[ E_2 = \text{changing concepts of God}. \]

Add:

\[ A_2 = \text{God progressively reveals his nature}. \]

Then:

\[ E_3 = \text{foreign influence on supernatural ontology}. \]

Add:

\[ A_3 = \text{God providentially uses foreign concepts}. \]

Then:

\[ E_4 = \text{Greek philosophical influence}. \]

Add:

\[ A_4 = \text{God prepared Greek philosophy for Christianity}. \]

The resulting theory may fit every observation. But its evidential strength depends partly on whether:

\[ A_1,A_2,A_3,A_4 \]

have reasons for acceptance independent of the problems they solve. Without such reasons, explanatory flexibility begins to resemble overfitting.

Predictive Constraint. The second question concerns constraint. What would the combined accommodation-progressive model make unlikely? Would a completely static theology count against it? Perhaps God could have revealed everything clearly at the beginning. Would radical doctrinal change count against it? Perhaps God reveals progressively. Would foreign conceptual influence count against it? Perhaps God uses surrounding cultures. Would indigenous continuity count against it? Perhaps God works through inherited tradition. Would theological disagreement count against it? Perhaps God permits interpretive freedom. Would strong consensus count in its favor? Perhaps divine guidance produced unity. The danger is clear. If:

\[ \forall E,\quad P(E\mid H_{R}^{\ast})>0 \]

and almost any \(E\) can be assigned a suitable divine rationale, then \(H_{R}^{\ast}\) becomes weakly constrained. The central issue is not logical possibility. It is whether the model meaningfully differentiates among possible historical worlds. A theory with high explanatory power should imply:

\[ P(E_1\mid H) > P(E_2\mid H) \]

for at least some plausible outcomes. Otherwise observing \(E_1\) tells us very little about whether \(H\) is true.

Providence Through Ordinary Causation. A still more comprehensive theological response appeals to providence. Perhaps God ordinarily works through natural and historical causes rather than bypassing them. On this view, the very social mechanisms described throughout this essay may themselves be instruments of divine action. Thus:

\[ M \neq \text{competitor to God}. \]

Instead:

\[ G \rightarrow M \rightarrow \text{religious development}. \]

This is metaphysically coherent. A theist may reasonably argue that distinguishing “natural cause” from “divine cause” creates a false dichotomy. If God sustains the world, then ordinary causal processes can simultaneously have natural explanations and providential significance. The point is important and should be granted. The systems argument does not show that natural causal explanation logically excludes divine causation. But the evidential question changes. If God acts entirely through ordinary mechanisms, and those mechanisms produce exactly the outcomes expected without detectable supernatural intervention, then the historical evidence does not discriminate between \(M\) and

\[ G\rightarrow M. \]

The models become observationally close:

\[ P(E\mid M) \approx P(E\mid G\rightarrow M). \]

At that point the claim of providence may function as a metaphysical interpretation of the causal system rather than an independently supported explanation derived from the religious evidence. That distinction is crucial. A proposition can be metaphysically compatible with all observations while receiving little evidential support from those observations.

Theological Underdetermination. There is another problem. Once providence, accommodation, and progressive revelation are combined, many different theological systems can interpret the same historical process as divinely guided. A Christian can say:

Causal pattern: Judaism → Christian fulfillment

A Muslim can say:

Causal pattern: Judaism → Christian partial development → Qur'anic correction

A restorationist movement can say:

Causal pattern: early revelation → corruption → later restoration

Each can use accommodation and providence to explain why competing traditions arose historically. Thus the same historical evidence may be compatible with multiple mutually exclusive revelatory narratives. If: \(E\) is equally interpretable under:

\[ R_1, R_2, R_3, \ldots,R_n, \]

then: \(E\) has limited power to discriminate among them. This matters because the aim is not merely to show that some providential story can be told. The relevant claim of many religions is much stronger: this particular historical tradition is uniquely revelatory. The historical evidence should therefore be evaluated for its ability to identify that particular tradition.

When Does an Auxiliary Become Ad Hoc? The language of “ad hoc” explanation should be used carefully. Not every auxiliary assumption is ad hoc. All complex theories require auxiliaries. The social model itself includes many:

  • network structure;
  • institutional effects;
  • demographic variation;
  • cognitive mechanisms;
  • historical contingency.

The question is whether an auxiliary possesses independent evidential support and increases the theory's explanatory or predictive power. An auxiliary becomes increasingly ad hoc when it is:

  1. introduced primarily after problematic evidence appears;
  2. weakly supported independently;
  3. tailored closely to the anomaly it solves;
  4. difficult to test separately;
  5. capable of preserving the theory under almost any outcome.

We can represent the theological model as:

\[ H_{R}^{\ast} = H_{R}+A+P+H+V+\cdots, \]

where perhaps:

  • \(A\) = accommodation;
  • \(P\) = progressive revelation;
  • \(H\) = hiddenness;
  • \(V\) = providential cultural mediation.

The problem is not the number of terms by itself. A complicated theory can be excellent if its parts are independently justified. The problem arises if each additional term primarily serves to prevent observed history from counting against \(H_{R}\). In that case: fit ↑ while: constraint ↓ . The theory becomes increasingly able to reproduce the observed data but decreasingly able to tell us what should have happened instead. This is exactly analogous to overfitting in statistical modeling.

Detectability and the Remaining Explanatory Burden

The core discriminating question. The strongest theological model can therefore be stated generously:

God reveals truth gradually, communicates through culturally available forms, allows limited human misunderstanding, works providentially through ordinary historical causes, and guides the tradition toward increasingly adequate understanding.

Nothing in the five historical cases deductively falsifies this model. But this is precisely where the comparative question becomes decisive:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What observations does this model make more probable than the social-historical model alone?} } \]

Consider again the sequence examined earlier:

Causal pattern: Mesopotamian narrative ecology → biblical narrative

Causal pattern: Near Eastern wisdom tradition → Job

Causal pattern: West Semitic deity traditions → developing Yahwism

Causal pattern: Iranian contact → apocalyptic development

Causal pattern: Greek philosophy → Christian metaphysics

Under \(M\), these relationships are expected consequences of historical dependence. Under \(M+H_{R}^{\ast}\), they are interpreted as the means through which God chose to reveal truth. But if both models predict the same visible causal sequence, then the sequence itself cannot strongly discriminate between them. The evidential burden shifts toward finding some additional feature: \(E^\ast\) such that:

\[ P(E^\ast\mid M+H_{R}^{\ast}) \gg P(E^\ast\mid M). \]

Without such evidence, revelation functions primarily as an interpretation imposed on a causal process already explained at the social-historical level.

A Tradeoff Between Compatibility and Detectability. This yields a broader tradeoff. The more rigid the revelation hypothesis, the easier it is to test. For example:

\[ R_{\text{rigid}} = \text{God provides clear, stable, culturally independent revelation}. \]

Such a theory makes strong predictions. Historical dependence can count significantly against it. But a sophisticated theology can increase compatibility:

\[ R_{\text{flexible}} = \text{God reveals progressively through culturally mediated historical processes}. \]

This fits the evidence much better. Yet the price is empirical detectability. As: flexibility ↑ , often: discriminating prediction ↓ . The revelation model increasingly resembles:

\[ M+\text{“God intended this history.”} \]

The historical record may remain perfectly compatible with this interpretation. But compatibility has been purchased by making the supernatural contribution difficult to identify independently of the ordinary causal process. This is the central tension.

Accommodation and Progressive Revelation as Explananda. There is one final reversal worth noting. Accommodation and progressive revelation are themselves theological concepts produced historically. They emerge as interpretive strategies for reconciling inherited revelation with:

  • textual diversity;
  • doctrinal development;
  • changing moral understanding;
  • historical criticism;
  • philosophical change.

They should therefore also be included within the explanandum. The social model can ask:

Under what historical conditions do religious systems develop doctrines of accommodation?

When do traditions reinterpret doctrinal change as progressive revelation?

What institutional pressures make such theories attractive?

This does not refute them. It shows that the theological response itself has a social history. Thus:

Causal pattern: historical challenge → theological adaptation → new doctrine of revelation

Even the theory explaining why revelation looks historical may itself be part of the historical evolution of the religion. That observation further reinforces the need for comparative rather than merely internal theological evaluation.

The result of this section is therefore not that accommodation, progressive revelation, or providence fail logically. On the contrary, they are powerful enough to reconcile revelation with much of the evidence. The problem is more subtle. As these theological models become sufficiently rich to reproduce the historical patterns already generated by \(M\), their empirical distinctiveness declines. The next section develops that distinction directly. The central principle is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{A theory's ability to remain compatible with evidence is not the same as the evidence providing support for the theory.} } \]

XVII. Why Compatibility Is Not Enough

The preceding section identified a central feature of sophisticated theological responses: they can often make revelation compatible with extensive historical dependence. Accommodation can explain culturally local expression. Progressive revelation can explain doctrinal development. Providence can explain why ordinary social and historical mechanisms mediate the process. Hiddenness can explain why supernatural guidance is not more obvious. Human limitation can explain error and disagreement. Taken together, these ideas make revelation difficult to falsify through historical evidence alone. But that does not mean the historical evidence supports revelation. The distinction between compatibility and confirmation is therefore essential. Suppose a body of evidence is represented by \(E\), and suppose the two competing models are \(M\) and

\[ M+H_{R}. \]

If both models are logically compatible with \(E\), then we have established only:

\[ P(E\mid M)>0 \]

and:

\[ P(E\mid M+H_{R})>0. \]

That is a very weak result. The evidential question is comparative:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Is }E\text{ substantially more expected under }M+H_{R}\text{ than under }M? } \]

In probabilistic terms, what matters is the likelihood ratio:

\[ \Lambda = \frac{P(E\mid M+H_{R})} {P(E\mid M)}. \]

If:

\[ \Lambda \gg 1, \]

then the evidence favors the revelatory addition. If:

\[ \Lambda \approx 1, \]

then the evidence does little to discriminate between the models. And if:

\[ \Lambda < 1, \]

then the evidence favors the social-historical model over the revelatory alternative. This framework clarifies why mere theological accommodation is insufficient. A theologian may successfully show:

There is no contradiction between revelation and cultural borrowing.

That establishes possibility. It does not establish:

Cultural borrowing is evidence for revelation.

Likewise:

Progressive revelation can explain doctrinal development.

does not establish:

The observed pattern of doctrinal development is more likely if progressive revelation is true.

This difference is fundamental.

Compatibility, Confirmation, and Retrodiction

Compatibility has very low evidential cost. Almost any sufficiently flexible theory can be made compatible with a large range of observations. Suppose a theory says:

God intended the religious history that actually occurred.

Then whatever happens becomes compatible with the theory. If Christianity spreads rapidly:

Providence favored its expansion.

If Christianity spreads unevenly:

Providence works through historical contingency.

If doctrine remains stable:

God preserved the truth.

If doctrine changes:

God revealed progressively.

If theology resembles surrounding culture:

God accommodated revelation.

If theology departs from surrounding culture:

Revelation transcended its environment.

If believers agree:

The Spirit produced unity.

If believers disagree:

God permits human freedom and interpretive limitation.

Such a system may be internally coherent. But its very flexibility reduces the evidential force of any one outcome. If both \(E\) and \(\neg E\) are readily accommodated, then observing \(E\) tells us little. More formally, if:

\[ P(E\mid H) \approx P(\neg E\mid H), \]

or if the model can be adjusted after observation so that either becomes expected, the actual evidence provides weak confirmation. This is why explanatory evaluation cannot stop at:

\[ E\text{ is compatible with }H. \]

That standard is too permissive.

Prediction Versus Retrodiction. The distinction can also be expressed as the difference between prediction and retrodiction. A model has strong predictive content when it constrains what should happen before the outcome is known. A model has weaker retrodictive content when it can explain many different outcomes after they occur. Consider the difference:

Predictive structure

\[ H \rightarrow \text{high probability of }E. \]

Retrodictive flexibility

\[ E \rightarrow \text{construct an auxiliary explanation making }H\text{ compatible with }E. \]

The second procedure can produce impressive narratives without strong evidential discrimination. This is particularly relevant to religious history because theological interpretation is often retrospective. A community knows which texts survived. It knows which councils prevailed. It knows which political events occurred. It knows which doctrine became orthodox. It then interprets the successful historical sequence as providential. Thus:

Causal pattern: observed history → theology of why God intended observed history

The explanation may be meaningful from within the tradition, but its evidential force depends on whether the same theology would have predicted the history independently. If not, it risks becoming an after-the-fact reconstruction.

The Difference Between “Can Explain” and “Best Explains”. This distinction also helps separate two questions that are often conflated:

Can this model explain the evidence?

and:

Does this model explain the evidence better than its alternatives?

The first asks for adequacy. The second asks for comparative superiority. Many theories can be made adequate. Far fewer are best supported. The relevant comparison is:

\[ \mathcal{E}(M) \]

versus:

\[ \mathcal{E}(M+H_{R}), \]

where \(\mathcal{E}\) represents total explanatory performance. If both models account for the observations, then we must compare them using the criteria developed earlier:

  • scope;
  • independent motivation;
  • parsimony;
  • predictive constraint;
  • counterfactual power;
  • robustness;
  • generality;
  • explanatory compression.

A revelation model does not gain evidential credit merely because it survives historical criticism. Survival is not the same as confirmation.

The Problem of Equal Compatibility Across Rival Revelations. The weakness of compatibility becomes especially clear when multiple incompatible religions can make the same historical evidence fit their own revelatory systems. Suppose:

\[ E = \text{the actual history of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam}. \]

A Christian theology may interpret that history as:

Causal pattern: Israel → Christ → church

An Islamic theology may interpret it as:

Causal pattern: earlier revelation → partial corruption or incompleteness → final Qur'anic correction

A Jewish theology may interpret Christianity and Islam as later religious developments outside the covenantal revelation centered on Israel. All three can appeal to providence. All three can appeal to human misunderstanding. All three can appeal to preservation and corruption selectively. Thus:

\[ P(E\mid H_C)>0, \]
\[ P(E\mid H_I)>0, \]

and:

\[ P(E\mid H_J)>0. \]

Compatibility therefore cannot identify which revelation is true. For that, one needs discriminating evidence: \(E^\ast\) such that:

\[ P(E^\ast\mid H_C) \gg P(E^\ast\mid H_I) \]

or some analogous inequality. Without this, the same historical evidence simply supports several mutually exclusive theological narratives at once. That severely limits its evidential value.

Internal Coherence Is Not External Confirmation. A similar distinction applies to theological coherence. A religious system may possess an internally elegant explanation for its own history. For example:

  • earlier revelation was partial;
  • later revelation completed it;
  • foreign philosophy was providential preparation;
  • apparent contradictions reflect accommodation;
  • doctrinal development reflects maturation.

This may form a coherent narrative. But coherence is not sufficient for external confirmation. A model can be internally coherent while still being only one among several equally coherent interpretations. The relevant question remains:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Why should an observer outside the system prefer this interpretation of the evidence?} } \]

That requires comparative evidence, not merely internal fit.

Flexible Divine Intention and Independent Expectations

The “anything God could do” problem. There is a recurring theological move that takes the form:

God could have chosen to reveal himself this way.

This statement may be true. But it has little evidential force unless paired with:

and we had independent reason to expect that God would reveal himself this way.

The difference is: could ≠ would probably. In Bayesian terms:

\[ P(E\mid H_{R})>0 \]

does not tell us whether:

\[ P(E\mid H_{R}) \]

is high. A deity could presumably reveal truth through:

  • one culture;
  • many cultures;
  • private visions;
  • public miracles;
  • scriptures;
  • philosophical discovery;
  • hidden providence;
  • unmistakable manifestations.

The space of divine possibilities is enormous. This makes divine-intention models difficult to constrain unless the theology specifies what God is likely to do. Without such specification, almost any history can be narrated as compatible with divine choice. That is precisely why the social model possesses an evidential advantage when it makes narrower, mechanism-based predictions.

The Importance of Independent Expectations. A model receives stronger confirmation when it predicts an observation for reasons established before that observation is used as evidence. For example, the social model independently predicts that:

Causal pattern: network contact → greater opportunity for conceptual exchange

This principle is supported outside religion. If religious similarity then tracks network contact, the observation fits an independently motivated expectation. Likewise:

Causal pattern: intergenerational transmission → religious clustering by family

Again, the mechanism is independently established. The same is true for:

  • institutional selection;
  • prestige bias;
  • path dependence;
  • cultural translation;
  • demographic reproduction.

The explanatory chain is therefore:

Causal pattern: independently established mechanism → religious prediction → observed pattern

That structure is evidentially stronger than:

Causal pattern: observed pattern → new theological rationale explaining why God allowed it

The issue is not that theological explanations are automatically false. It is that they gain less confirmation when they are generated primarily in response to the evidence they explain.

Conditionalization on the Full Evidence. Another advantage of the comparative framework is that it prevents selective use of favorable evidence. Religious arguments often focus on isolated features:

  • the profundity of scripture;
  • a fulfilled prophecy;
  • a powerful mystical experience;
  • the rapid growth of a movement;
  • the endurance of a tradition.

But these observations should be evaluated alongside the rest of the data. Let:

\[ E_{\text{total}} = E_1+E_2+\cdots+E_n. \]

The appropriate comparison is not:

\[ P(E_1\mid H_{R}) \]

in isolation. It is closer to:

\[ P(E_{\text{total}}\mid H_{R}) \]

relative to:

\[ P(E_{\text{total}}\mid M). \]

This includes inconvenient evidence:

  • geographic contingency;
  • rival revelations;
  • doctrinal instability;
  • failed prophecies;
  • textual development;
  • conceptual borrowing;
  • theological disagreement;
  • institutional selection;
  • cultural clustering.

A hypothesis should be judged against the joint distribution, not only the observations it explains most comfortably. This is where the vector formulation from the opening becomes important. The explanandum is:

\[ \mathbf{D}, \]

not one chosen coordinate of \(\mathbf{D}\). A theory that explains one component brilliantly but requires substantial auxiliary structure to accommodate the remaining components may perform worse overall than a model with broad, unified coverage.

Likelihood and Explanatory Detectability

Likelihood, not mere possibility. The core probabilistic distinction can be summarized simply. Suppose:

\[ H_1=M \]

and:

\[ H_2=M+H_{R}. \]

The fact that:

\[ P(E\mid H_2)>0 \]

is almost irrelevant if:

\[ P(E\mid H_1) \]

is already high. The evidence supports \(H_2\) over \(H_1\) only insofar as:

\[ \frac{P(E\mid H_2)} {P(E\mid H_1)} >1. \]

And the larger that ratio, the stronger the discrimination. This reframes many religious arguments. The question is not:

Can progressive revelation explain doctrinal development?

It is:

Is the observed pattern of doctrinal development more probable under progressive revelation than under ordinary cultural evolution?

Not:

Can God use Greek philosophy?

But:

Does the historically specific dependence of Christian metaphysics on Greek philosophy provide evidence for divine revelation beyond what cultural transmission already predicts?

Not:

Could God reveal himself differently across cultures?

But:

Does the actual geographic distribution of religious belief look more like intentional revelation or ordinary social inheritance?

These are harder questions because they require comparison rather than possibility.

Compatibility Can Even Reduce Evidential Value. There is a deeper consequence. The more a theological model is modified to ensure compatibility with ordinary historical processes, the less those processes can function as evidence for the theology. Suppose a defender says:

Of course revelation looks exactly like cultural evolution, because God chose to work entirely through cultural evolution.

This removes the apparent conflict. But it also removes the discriminating signal. If:

\[ P(E\mid M+H_{R}) \approx P(E\mid M), \]

then: \(E\) cannot strongly support \(H_{R}\). Thus accommodation can protect the theology from disconfirmation while simultaneously weakening the evidential role of religious history. This creates a tradeoff: historical compatibility ↑ ⇒ historical discriminability ↓ when compatibility is achieved by making divine action mirror ordinary causation. This is one of the most important conclusions of the entire argument. A revelation model may survive the historical evidence precisely by becoming difficult to detect within it.

Explanatory Detectability. We can describe the missing quantity as explanatory detectability. A causal variable is detectably explanatory when including it changes what we expect to observe. Let \(H_{R}\) be the revelatory hypothesis. Then \(H_{R}\) has high explanatory detectability if:

\[ P(E\mid M+H_{R}) \]

differs meaningfully from:

\[ P(E\mid M). \]

But if:

\[ P(E\mid M+H_{R}) \approx P(E\mid M) \]

for the major structures of religious history, then \(H_{R}\) has low detectability in those data. This does not prove that \(H_{R}\) is false. It means the data do not strongly identify it. That distinction is important. A cause can exist without being detectable from a particular dataset. But if the religious history itself is repeatedly cited as evidence for divine revelation, then low detectability becomes an epistemic problem. The evidential burden shifts toward some other class of evidence capable of distinguishing the models.

From Compatibility to Comparative Support. The methodological principle can therefore be stated as:

\[ \boxed{ \text{A theory is not supported merely because it can explain an observation after the fact; the observation must be comparatively more expected under that theory than under relevant alternatives.} } \]

Applied to religion, this means that:

  • accommodation may establish compatibility;
  • progressive revelation may establish compatibility;
  • providence may establish compatibility;
  • hiddenness may establish compatibility.

But none automatically establishes comparative support. The total historical pattern must still be evaluated under competing models. This prepares the next stage of the argument. So far, the essay has emphasized historical examples and explanatory comparison. But a serious causal model should do more than redescribe the past. It should generate further expectations that could, at least in principle, be examined empirically. If the social-historical model is genuinely generative, then it should predict patterns in religious diffusion, conceptual clustering, institutional stability, crisis response, translation effects, and revelation content beyond the five cases already discussed.

The next section therefore turns from retrospective explanation to forward-looking empirical predictions.

XVIII. Empirical Predictions of the Social-Systems Model

A causal model is stronger when it does more than organize known historical evidence. It should generate expectations that extend beyond the cases from which it was initially constructed. If the social-historical model developed here is genuinely explanatory rather than merely retrospective, then it should imply observable regularities in the distribution, transmission, transformation, and stabilization of religious systems. The aim of this section is not to claim that every prediction has already been tested quantitatively or that religion can be reduced to a few simple equations. The relevant systems are too historically complex for that. The point is methodological: the model implies directional expectations that can, at least in principle, be compared with historical, sociological, textual, demographic, and network data.

Let the model again be represented as:

\[ M= F( C, N, I, P, E, D, S, F_b, \epsilon ) \]

where:

  • \(C\) represents conceptual ecology;
  • \(N\) network structure;
  • \(I\) institutions;
  • \(P\) political and historical conditions;
  • \(E\) individual experiences and innovations;
  • \(D\) demographic structure;
  • \(S\) selection pressures;
  • \(F_b\) feedback processes;
  • \(\epsilon\) contingency and unmodeled variation.

If these variables genuinely contribute to religious development, then changing them should produce systematic changes in religious outcomes. The value of the model lies partly in identifying such relationships.

Network and Distribution Predictions

Prediction 1: Religious Similarity Should Track Network Connectivity. If religious concepts diffuse through ordinary social contact, then historically connected populations should, all else being equal, display greater opportunities for religious exchange than isolated populations. Thus:

\[ P(\text{conceptual transmission}_{ij}) \uparrow \quad\text{as}\quad w_{ij}\uparrow, \]

where \(w_{ij}\) represents the strength of historical interaction between populations \(i\) and \(j\). This produces a general expectation:

Causal pattern: network proximity → increased religious correlation

The correlation need not consist of simple doctrinal similarity. As argued earlier, contact can generate both convergence and oppositional differentiation. The broader prediction is that religious change should be sensitive to network structure. If two communities engage in sustained trade, migration, intermarriage, translation, political integration, or intellectual exchange, we should expect more evidence of mutual conceptual influence than between otherwise comparable communities lacking such contact.

Conversely, if a proposed influence is historically plausible only under strong contact, then the absence of any credible transmission network weakens the hypothesis. This turns network theory into a methodological constraint on claims of influence. Instead of arguing merely:

Text A resembles Text B.

we ask:

Was there a historically plausible pathway connecting the relevant communities?

Thus: similarity + network connectivity provides stronger evidence for cultural transmission than similarity alone.

Prediction 2: Religious Clustering Should Exceed What Random Distribution Predicts. If beliefs are transmitted socially, religious affiliation and doctrine should exhibit geographic and institutional clustering. Let \(R_i\) represent the religion or religious configuration of individual \(i\), and let:

\[ N(i) \]

represent that individual's social neighborhood. Then the model predicts:

\[ P(R_i=R_j\mid j\in N(i)) > P(R_i=R_k\mid k\notin N(i)) \]

under many ordinary conditions. In plain language, people connected through family, community, language, and institutions should be more religiously similar than randomly chosen individuals from unrelated social environments. This prediction operates at multiple scales:

  • family;
  • congregation;
  • town;
  • linguistic community;
  • region;
  • nation;
  • diaspora network.

The same logic applies to doctrines within traditions. Particular theological positions should often cluster along:

  • educational networks;
  • denominational institutions;
  • clerical lineages;
  • seminaries;
  • political jurisdictions;
  • missionary histories.

This matters because many religions describe their beliefs as responses to objective supernatural reality. Yet if belief distributions are strongly predicted by social topology, then the social variables are doing substantial causal work. The more accurately religious affiliation can be predicted from family, geography, language, and institutional location, the greater the explanatory burden on models claiming that belief distributions primarily reflect differential access to supernatural truth.

Prediction 3: Cultural Contact Should Produce Both Borrowing and Boundary Formation. The model predicts not only similarity but structured differentiation. Suppose two religious communities enter sustained contact. Then:

\[ \text{contact} \rightarrow \begin{cases} \text{borrowing}\\ \text{hybridization}\\ \text{reinterpretation}\\ \text{polemical rejection}\\ \text{identity differentiation}. \end{cases} \]

Which response occurs should depend on additional variables such as:

  • power relations;
  • perceived threat;
  • institutional flexibility;
  • doctrinal centrality;
  • group identity;
  • political incentives.

This generates a particularly useful prediction: the strongest doctrinal contrasts may sometimes arise between closely interacting groups rather than isolated ones. A neighboring religion can become the relevant “other” against which a community defines itself. Thus:

Causal pattern: high contact + high identity competition → boundary intensification

This helps explain why similarity and difference should not be interpreted independently. A tradition may borrow narrative structures from a neighbor while simultaneously hardening doctrinal differences. A community may adopt a philosophical vocabulary while rejecting the metaphysics originally associated with it. A religion may develop more precise theological definitions precisely because competing interpretations force clarification. The systems model therefore predicts a complex mixture of: convergence + divergence.

Prediction 4: Major Linguistic Transitions Should Generate Theological Effects. If language shapes conceptual availability, then major translation events should leave theological traces. Suppose:

\[ L_1 \rightarrow L_2 \]

is a transition from one linguistic environment to another. Because lexical fields do not map perfectly, the translated religious system should become:

\[ R(L_2) \neq R(L_1) \]

in at least some interpretive dimensions. The expectation is not wholesale theological transformation from every translation. It is that language change creates new interpretive affordances. Thus:

Causal pattern: translation → semantic shift → new possible interpretations

This suggests several empirical questions:

  • Do major doctrinal innovations cluster around periods of translation?
  • Do different linguistic branches of the same religion develop distinct theological vocabularies?
  • Do terms with unusually wide semantic ranges produce later doctrinal disputes?
  • Do authoritative translations create path-dependent interpretive traditions?

The Septuagint provides one historical case, but the model predicts analogous effects elsewhere. This is especially useful because it generates a comparative research program across religions.

Institutional Selection, Crisis, and Path Dependence

Prediction 5: Institutional Centralization Should Increase Doctrinal Standardization. If institutions regulate transmission, more centralized religious systems should generally possess stronger capacities for doctrinal standardization. Let \(I_c\) represent degree of institutional centralization. Then the model predicts, other things being equal:

\[ I_c\uparrow \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{doctrinal variance}\downarrow. \]

The mechanism is straightforward. Centralized institutions can:

  • define official doctrine;
  • standardize liturgy;
  • train clergy;
  • control educational curricula;
  • adjudicate disputes;
  • discipline dissent;
  • preserve authoritative texts.

By contrast, decentralized systems may allow greater local variation. The prediction is probabilistic, not absolute. Highly centralized institutions can experience schisms, while decentralized traditions may converge through informal mechanisms. But the general relationship should be observable. This also produces a counterfactual:

Causal pattern: same revelation + different institutional structure → different degree of doctrinal variation

If this occurs repeatedly, it provides evidence that institutional form contributes causally to theological outcomes.

Prediction 6: Canonical Survival Should Correlate with Preservation Infrastructure. If texts survive partly because communities preserve them, then canonical or quasi-canonical survival should correlate with the strength of preservation mechanisms. Thus:

\[ P(\text{text survives}) = f( \text{copying frequency}, \text{liturgical use}, \text{institutional support}, \text{network distribution}, \text{material preservation} ). \]

Texts embedded in widely distributed institutions should generally possess higher survival probabilities than texts confined to small, isolated communities. This generates an important methodological implication: survival ⇏ supernatural preservation. Before treating textual survival as evidence of providence, one should examine the ordinary causal infrastructure that produced it. The same applies to doctrines. A doctrine repeatedly taught in schools, creeds, sermons, and rituals has a higher probability of survival than one lacking such reinforcement.

Prediction 7: Social and Political Crises Should Alter Religious Innovation Rates and Content. If religion responds adaptively to historical conditions, periods of severe disruption should often produce distinctive kinds of religious innovation. Potential triggers include:

  • war;
  • conquest;
  • exile;
  • persecution;
  • economic collapse;
  • rapid modernization;
  • institutional breakdown.

A generic model might predict:

\[ \text{crisis intensity}\uparrow \rightarrow P(\text{religious innovation})\uparrow \]

under some social conditions. The content may also shift. For example:

Causal pattern: uncontrollable political crisis → increased appeal of cosmic or apocalyptic explanation

A defeated community may reinterpret political loss as:

  • punishment;
  • purification;
  • test;
  • temporary triumph of evil;
  • precursor to imminent divine intervention.

The important prediction is not that every crisis produces apocalypticism. It is that religious innovation should be sensitive to the kinds of problems communities face. Thus: religious content ~ historical problem structure. If this relationship is systematic, then theology is functioning partly as an adaptive interpretive system.

Revelation and Conceptual Ecology

Prediction 8: Revelatory Content Should Be Culturally Indexed. The endogenous-revelation model generates one of the clearest expectations. If revelatory experiences are interpreted through local conceptual ecologies, then reported revelations should disproportionately contain concepts already present in the experiencer's culture. Let \(C_i\) represent the conceptual environment of individual \(i\), and let \(R_i\) represent the content of that individual's revelation claim. Then:

\[ P(R_i\subseteq C_i\mid M) \]

should be relatively high. More realistically, revelation may recombine existing elements:

\[ R_i = f(C_i,E_i,\text{innovation}). \]

The content need not be completely conventional. Novelty is expected. But novelty should generally be locally intelligible. This generates empirical expectations:

  • Christian visions should disproportionately contain Christian entities and symbols;
  • Muslim revelations should disproportionately use Islamic concepts;
  • new religious movements should often recombine categories available in their historical environments;
  • revelations should rarely contain complex theological systems wholly unrelated to the experiencer's conceptual world.

A strong and repeated failure of this prediction could count against the social model. For example, widespread independent convergence on highly specific supernatural information across causally isolated populations would be much harder to explain. The model is therefore not closed to potential disconfirmation.

Prediction 9: Theological Innovation Should Often Follow Conceptual Availability. The causal model predicts that religious thinkers are more likely to formulate doctrines once the necessary conceptual distinctions become available. Let \(C_t\) be the conceptual ecology at time \(t\). A doctrine requiring conceptual resources:

\[ \{c_1,c_2,c_3\} \]

should be less likely to emerge before those resources exist. Thus:

\[ P(D\mid c_1,c_2,c_3\in C_t) > P(D\mid c_1,c_2,c_3\notin C_t). \]

This does not mean thinkers cannot innovate radically. It means conceptual innovation is usually built from some prior repertoire. This prediction is particularly relevant to later metaphysical theology. Highly technical doctrines involving categories such as essence, substance, person, nature, immateriality, or simplicity should tend to appear where those distinctions are intellectually available. The historical timing of doctrinal articulation therefore becomes evidence about causal dependence.

Prediction 10: Competing Religious Systems Should Generate Similar Institutional Solutions. If religions face recurring social problems, different traditions should sometimes develop functionally similar institutions even without direct borrowing. Religious systems must solve problems such as:

  • authority;
  • succession;
  • education;
  • dispute resolution;
  • ritual coordination;
  • preservation of texts;
  • membership boundaries.

This may produce convergent structures:

Causal pattern: similar social problem → similar institutional solution

This is important because not every cross-religious similarity should be attributed to historical borrowing. The social model allows two pathways:

Causal pattern: shared ancestry/contact → homologous similarity

and:

Causal pattern: similar functional pressures → convergent similarity

Distinguishing these is an empirical task. This makes the model more sophisticated than a simple diffusion theory.

Prediction 11: Strong Institutions Should Increase Resistance to Disconfirming Events. Religious systems with developed interpretive and institutional resources should be better able to absorb apparent disconfirmation. Suppose:

\[ E_d = \text{event apparently inconsistent with prior expectation}. \]

A weakly institutionalized movement may fragment. A mature religious system may possess mechanisms such as:

  • reinterpretation;
  • allegory;
  • revised chronology;
  • theological distinction;
  • appeal to mystery;
  • institutional authority.

Thus:

\[ P(\text{system persistence}\mid E_d) \uparrow \]

as interpretive and institutional resources increase. This is not necessarily irrational. All sophisticated intellectual systems revise auxiliary assumptions when confronted with anomalies. The prediction is simply that mature religions should become more resilient because they possess more resources for maintaining continuity under changing evidence. This is another feedback process:

Causal pattern: historical longevity → greater interpretive resources → greater resilience → further longevity

Prediction 12: Early Contingencies Should Produce Persistent Downstream Effects. Path dependence implies that early decisions can shape later religious history long after their original context disappears. Suppose an early event: \(X_{t_0}\) changes the state of the system. Then:

\[ R_{t_n} = F(X_{t_0},R_{t_1},\ldots,R_{t_{n-1}}). \]

The effect may persist through:

  • canonical inclusion;
  • institutional precedent;
  • liturgical usage;
  • doctrinal language;
  • legal norms;
  • geographic separation.

This predicts that apparently minor historical contingencies may have large downstream effects. If true, later religious systems should contain traces of earlier events whose original causal importance may no longer be visible to participants. This is exactly the kind of historical residue the systems model expects.

Prediction 13: Rival Traditions Should Display Parallel Meta-Theological Defenses. A further prediction follows from the feedback and adaptation model. Religious traditions under intellectual pressure should independently develop strategies for protecting central commitments. These may include:

  • accommodation;
  • progressive revelation;
  • metaphorical interpretation;
  • appeal to mystery;
  • hiddenness;
  • distinction between essential and nonessential doctrine;
  • reinterpretation of failed expectations.

Thus:

Causal pattern: challenge to core belief + institutional commitment → meta-theological adaptation

If similar defensive structures arise across incompatible traditions, this suggests they may partly reflect recurrent properties of religious systems rather than unique features of one revelation. That does not show that the defenses are false. It makes them additional objects for comparative explanation.

Cross-Tradition Discrimination

Prediction 14: Social Transmission Should Often Outperform Truth-Tracking Variables in Predicting Religious Identity. Perhaps the most significant prediction is comparative. If religious affiliation is driven heavily by cultural transmission, then variables such as:

  • parental religion;
  • local religious majority;
  • language;
  • network membership

should often predict an individual's religious identity better than variables plausibly connected to independently assessing supernatural truth. That is:

\[ P(R_i\mid \text{social variables}) \]

should have substantial predictive power. If so, the observed distribution of belief is more naturally explained as cultural transmission than as populations independently converging upon supernatural truth. This connects directly with the distributional argument developed elsewhere.

Prediction 15: Similar Causal Mechanisms Should Operate Across True and False Religions. Finally, the social model predicts that the observable causal machinery should operate regardless of whether a religion's theological claims happen to be true. Thus:

\[ M(R_{\text{Christian}}) \]

and:

\[ M(R_{\text{Muslim}}) \]

and:

\[ M(R_{\text{Mormon}}) \]

and:

\[ M(R_{\text{other}}) \]

should display comparable processes of:

  • socialization;
  • institutionalization;
  • testimony;
  • revelation production;
  • canon formation;
  • missionary diffusion;
  • doctrinal adaptation.

This is exactly what makes the model comparative. It does not need to decide beforehand which theology is true in order to explain how religious systems behave. If one religion is genuinely revealed, it should therefore be possible to ask:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What observable feature distinguishes the operation of }M \text{ within the true tradition from its operation within false ones?} } \]

Without such a distinction, the causal structure remains largely invariant across truth status.

A Research Program Rather Than a Closed Explanation

These predictions show why the social-historical model is not merely a rhetorical alternative to theology. It generates a research program. One can investigate:

  • religious network topology;
  • geographic clustering;
  • intergenerational transmission;
  • textual diffusion;
  • translation effects;
  • institutional centralization;
  • crisis-driven innovation;
  • revelation content;
  • doctrinal timing;
  • selection and survival;
  • feedback and resilience.

Different disciplines can test different components. History reconstructs transmission networks. Archaeology examines institutional and ritual change. Sociology studies affiliation and group dynamics. Psychology investigates experience and cognition. Anthropology studies ritual and cultural variation. Network science models diffusion and clustering. Textual criticism reconstructs literary development. The system is therefore genuinely interdisciplinary. The model will undoubtedly require refinement. Some mechanisms will prove stronger than others. Certain religious phenomena may resist existing explanations. That is precisely what a useful causal framework should allow. The aim is not to declare in advance that every phenomenon has already been explained. It is to locate the unexplained residual accurately. If the model fails systematically in some domain, that failure becomes informative. What matters is that the theory generates conditions under which it could be improved or rejected. This distinguishes it from a framework capable of absorbing any observation without constraint. The predictions also prepare the way for the final evidential synthesis. The historical cases showed vertical development through time. The distributional patterns showed horizontal dependence across populations. The network model showed lateral interaction among traditions. The causal framework showed feedback across levels. These can now be combined into one total-evidence picture.

The next section therefore asks what happens when we evaluate religion simultaneously along four dimensions:

\[ \boxed{ \text{horizontal} + \text{vertical} + \text{genealogical} + \text{network dependence}. } \]

The force of the argument lies not in any one of these dimensions, but in their joint structure.

XIX. The Total Evidence: Horizontal, Vertical, Genealogical, and Network Dependence

The argument developed so far does not depend on any single historical parallel, any one disputed reconstruction of ancient religion, or any isolated sociological fact. Its force comes from the way several forms of dependence converge. Religious systems are not merely distributed unevenly across space. They are also inherited across generations. They develop through time. They preserve genealogical relationships to earlier traditions. They change through networks of contact. And these forms of dependence interact. The resulting evidential pattern is therefore multidimensional.

The four dimensions of dependence

Dimension Representative observation Main causal implication
Horizontal Religious belief clusters across geography and social location Belief depends strongly on local transmission environments
Vertical Religious states inherit earlier texts, institutions, and concepts Later states depend on earlier states
Genealogical Traditions and doctrines have reconstructable ancestry Religious concepts possess historical lineages
Network Contact changes trajectories through borrowing, transformation, and rejection Genealogy is reticulate rather than a simple tree

We can represent the total structure as:

\[ E_{\text{total}} = E_H + E_V + E_G + E_N + E_I, \]

where:

  • \(E_H\) = horizontal or spatial dependence;
  • \(E_V\) = vertical or temporal dependence;
  • \(E_G\) = genealogical dependence;
  • \(E_N\) = network dependence;
  • \(E_I\) = interaction among these dimensions.

The argument is strongest when these are considered jointly rather than separately.

Horizontal Dependence: Religion Across Space

The first dimension is horizontal. At any given period, religious beliefs are not distributed randomly across the human population. They cluster geographically, linguistically, politically, and socially. The probability that an individual becomes Christian, Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist, Mormon, or religiously unaffiliated is strongly influenced by where that individual is born and which social networks they inhabit. Schematically:

\[ P(R_i\mid G_i,C_i,N_i) \neq P(R_i), \]

where:

  • \(R_i\) is an individual's religious state;
  • \(G_i\) is geographic environment;
  • \(C_i\) is cultural environment;
  • \(N_i\) is social network.

In other words, religion displays strong spatial autocorrelation. Nearby or socially connected populations are often more religiously similar than distant and historically disconnected populations. This is exactly what one expects from cultural transmission. Religions spread along:

  • migration routes;
  • missionary networks;
  • imperial expansion;
  • trade corridors;
  • kinship structures;
  • language communities;
  • political institutions.

The global distribution of religion therefore bears visible traces of historical causation. Christianity is concentrated where Christian institutions historically expanded. Islam is concentrated where Islamic polities, trade networks, migration, and missionary activity established durable communities. Religious boundaries frequently correspond, at least approximately, to historical political and linguistic boundaries. A social transmission model predicts this directly. The significant question for revelation is not whether divine truth can be transmitted socially. Of course it can. The question is why access to purportedly objective supernatural truth appears so strongly conditional on ordinary social location. If a person's probability of accepting a particular revelation can be predicted substantially from birthplace, parents, language, and community, then those variables are doing major explanatory work. Thus horizontal dependence is not merely demographic trivia. It is part of the causal structure of belief.

Vertical Dependence: Religion Through Time

The second dimension is vertical. Religious systems display strong dependence on their own previous states. Let \(R_t\) represent a religious configuration at time \(t\). Then:

\[ R_{t+1} \]

is generally not independent of: \(R_t\). Rather:

\[ P(R_{t+1}\mid R_t) \gg P(R_{t+1}) \]

for many major components of religious systems. Later theology inherits earlier scripture. Later ritual inherits earlier ritual. Later institutions descend from earlier institutions. Later disputes presuppose earlier disputes. Later doctrines are formulated in response to earlier conceptual structures. This dependence creates historical continuity even when substantial change occurs. Christianity does not emerge as an arbitrary religion unrelated to Judaism. Islam does not emerge independently of late antique Jewish, Christian, Arabian, and monotheistic environments. Later Christian traditions do not begin from scratch after each century. They inherit accumulated textual, institutional, and conceptual states. Thus:

\[ R_{t+1} = F(R_t,\ldots). \]

The previous state is itself a causal input. This is what makes religious development path-dependent. The future state of the religion depends not merely on current external pressures but on the particular route by which the system arrived there.

Genealogical Dependence: Traditions Have Ancestry

Horizontal and vertical dependence together generate a third structure: genealogy. Religions have ancestry. Their texts, doctrines, rituals, institutions, and concepts can often be situated within historically reconstructable lines of descent. Christianity has a Jewish genealogy. Rabbinic Judaism and Christianity both develop from earlier Jewish traditions but diverge historically. Protestant churches descend through historically identifiable transformations of Western Christianity. Later restorationist movements emerge within already existing Christian conceptual environments. Religious ideas likewise possess genealogies. A theological concept may descend through:

\[ C_1 \rightarrow C_2 \rightarrow C_3 \rightarrow C_4, \]

with each later form modifying its predecessors. This was the structure of the five historical cases. Sacred narrative had literary ancestry. Theodicy had intellectual ancestry. The deity concept had religious ancestry. Apocalyptic ontology had intercultural ancestry. Metaphysical theology had philosophical ancestry. The cumulative pattern is therefore not merely that religions change. More specifically:

\[ \boxed{ \text{religious concepts possess historically reconstructable lineages}. } \]

This becomes especially important when a doctrine is presented as information about an independently existing supernatural reality. If a claim about God, angels, resurrection, or metaphysical being can be traced through identifiable conceptual predecessors, then the historian acquires a causal explanation for why that belief appeared in that form at that time. Again, genealogy does not logically prove falsehood. Human beings may discover true propositions through historically cumulative processes. Science itself has genealogy. Philosophy has genealogy. The relevant evidential question is therefore more precise:

Does the religious genealogy contain any detectable informational contribution that cannot be adequately explained by the historical lineage itself?

That is the recurring issue of explanatory residual.

Network Dependence: Genealogy Is Not a Tree

The fourth dimension complicates the third. Religious genealogy is rarely a simple branching tree. Traditions interact laterally. They borrow. They compete. They translate. They polemicize. They intermarry. They share institutions and political environments. Thus religious history is better represented by a reticulated network than by a pure phylogenetic tree. Let:

\[ V = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\} \]

represent communities, traditions, institutions, or intellectual centers. Let:

\[ E = \{e_{ij}\} \]

represent historical relationships between them. Each edge may have weight: \(w_{ij}\), representing the strength of interaction. Then:

\[ P(\text{transmission}_{ij}) \uparrow \quad\text{as}\quad w_{ij}\uparrow. \]

The crucial point is that the later state of one tradition may depend not only on its own earlier state but also on neighboring systems:

\[ R_j(t+1) = F( R_j(t), R_i(t), w_{ij}, C_j(t), I_j(t), P_j(t), \ldots ). \]

This network structure explains why religious traditions can simultaneously display continuity and external influence. A tradition can preserve its identity while absorbing categories from a neighbor. It can borrow a literary form while reversing its ideology. It can adopt philosophical vocabulary while rejecting the original philosophy. It can respond to another religion by becoming less similar to it. This last point is especially important. Network dependence can produce either similarity or anti-similarity. A community exposed to a rival may sharpen precisely those doctrines that distinguish it from that rival. Thus cultural influence is not equivalent to imitation. The relevant question is whether contact changes the trajectory.

What the Four Dimensions Jointly Imply

Interaction among the four dimensions. The real explanatory power appears when these dimensions interact. Consider a simplified case. A religious idea originates in one community. It spreads geographically through a network. It becomes institutionalized. It is transmitted vertically to the next generation. Later communities reinterpret it through new conceptual environments. Those reinterpretations then diffuse laterally to neighboring communities. A controversy emerges. One version becomes canonical. The winning form is transmitted to later generations. This process might be represented as:

Causal pattern: innovation → network diffusion → institutional selection → vertical inheritance → reinterpretation → new diffusion

Thus:

\[ E_H, E_V, E_G, E_N \]

are not independent pieces of evidence. They form one interacting causal architecture. This is why the systems model is more powerful than a simple distribution argument. A distributional observation alone might show that religion correlates with birthplace. A historical observation alone might show that doctrine develops. A source-critical observation alone might show literary dependence. A network observation alone might show conceptual transmission. Taken together, however, they reveal the same underlying pattern at multiple scales. The religious system behaves like a historically evolving cultural population.

The Joint Pattern. The broad observable structure can therefore be summarized as:

\[ \mathbf{D} = \{ \text{spatial clustering}, \text{intergenerational inheritance}, \text{historical development}, \text{genealogical continuity}, \text{network sensitivity}, \text{institutional selection}, \text{conceptual adaptation} \}. \]

Under \(M\), these features are mutually reinforcing expectations. A cultural system should cluster spatially because transmission occurs through networks. It should exhibit vertical continuity because social learning is intergenerational. It should display genealogy because later states inherit earlier states. It should show lateral influence because networks connect traditions. It should exhibit institutional filtering because not every innovation survives. It should show path dependence because earlier selections constrain later possibilities. Thus:

\[ P(\mathbf{D}\mid M) \]

should be relatively high. The important question is what corresponding probability should be assigned under a special-revelation model. If the revelatory hypothesis includes unrestricted accommodation, progressive revelation, providential network transmission, and divine use of cultural inheritance, then:

\[ P(\mathbf{D}\mid M+H_{R}) \]

may also be high. But this returns us to the problem identified earlier:

\[ P(\mathbf{D}\mid M+H_{R}) \approx P(\mathbf{D}\mid M). \]

If so, the total historical pattern does not strongly discriminate in favor of revelation.

Cumulative Evidence and Explanatory Convergence. This is why the argument should not rise or fall on the strongest interpretation of any single case. Suppose one weakens the Sargon-Moses comparison. The horizontal distribution of religion remains. Intergenerational inheritance remains. The genealogy of Jewish and Christian thought remains. The historical development of YHWH remains. Hellenistic philosophical mediation remains. Sectarian fragmentation remains. Network-sensitive diffusion remains. Suppose one disputes Iranian influence on one particular Jewish doctrine. The broader dependence structure still remains. Suppose one rejects a strongly corporeal interpretation of early YHWH. Historical variation in divine representation still remains. The argument is therefore cumulative. Its strength lies in convergence:

\[ E_1+E_2+E_3+\cdots+E_n \rightarrow M. \]

Not every \(E_i\) must be maximally strong. The question is whether the ensemble exhibits a pattern better explained by a common generative system. This kind of explanatory convergence is common in historical sciences. No single fossil establishes an entire phylogeny. No single linguistic cognate establishes a language family's complete history. No single archaeological site establishes an empire's full economic system. Instead, multiple partially independent indicators converge on a causal reconstruction. The same methodological logic applies here.

Truth-Tracking, Causal Residuals, and Discrimination

The problem for independent truth-tracking. The total pattern also creates a challenge for any model in which religious belief is expected to track supernatural truth substantially independently of social history. Suppose:

\[ T = \text{objective supernatural truth}. \]

A strong truth-tracking revelation model might lead us to expect:

\[ R_i \approx f(T), \]

where religious belief is significantly determined by access to \(T\). But the observed distribution appears much more like:

\[ R_i = f( T?, G_i, F_i, N_i, C_i, I_i, H_i ), \]

where:

  • \(G_i\) = geography;
  • \(F_i\) = family;
  • \(N_i\) = network;
  • \(C_i\) = conceptual ecology;
  • \(I_i\) = institutions;
  • \(H_i\) = historical inheritance.

The question mark after \(T\) is intentional. The other variables are empirically visible. Their causal effects can often be traced. The independent contribution of supernatural truth is much harder to identify. This is the evidential asymmetry. The social variables leave signatures. The revelatory variable, if present, appears hidden behind them.

A Causal Decomposition. We can state the issue through causal decomposition. Suppose an observed doctrine \(D\) depends on geography, family, networks, conceptual ecology, institutions, historical inheritance, and a putative revelatory contribution \(Z_R\):

\[ D = f( G, F, N, C, I, H, Z_R ). \]

The historical project can estimate, at least qualitatively, how much explanatory work is being done by the observable social-historical variables \(G,F,N,C,I,H\). As those variables explain more of the pattern, the residual contribution attributed to \(Z_R\) becomes narrower. This does not show that \(Z_R=0\); it shows that evidence for \(Z_R\neq0\) must come from some effect not already accounted for by the observed causal variables. This is exactly why the argument is not a genetic fallacy. The conclusion is not:

This belief has a cultural history, therefore it is false.

The conclusion is:

This belief has a cultural history that substantially explains why it arose in this form, so anyone proposing an additional revelatory cause must identify the explanatory work performed by that additional cause.

That is a model-comparison claim.

Genealogy Does Not Equal Falsity. This distinction is worth making explicit because it is easy to misunderstand. All human knowledge has causal origins. A scientific belief may arise through:

  • education;
  • institutional training;
  • testimony;
  • historical inheritance;
  • social networks.

Those facts do not invalidate it. The difference is that scientific claims can often be independently checked against external reality. The historical origin of a claim and its evidential warrant are separable. Thus: genealogy ⇏ falsehood. The relevant question is whether the claim has independent truth-tracking evidence beyond the mechanisms that explain its social production. If a scientific proposition predicts an observation that would have been unlikely without the theory, then it can acquire support regardless of its social genealogy. The same standard can be applied to religious claims. The problem is not that religious beliefs are culturally produced. The problem is whether there is an additional evidential signal indicating that some of those culturally produced beliefs reliably track supernatural reality. That is precisely what the next section must ask.

From Genealogy to Discrimination. The total-evidence argument therefore reaches a natural limit. Historical and social explanation can show that religion is deeply structured by ordinary causal processes. It can show that revelation claims themselves are culturally patterned. It can show that religious doctrines have genealogies. It can show that supernatural ontologies and metaphysical concepts develop historically. But none of this alone entails: no revelation exists. The remaining question is positive rather than merely critical:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What evidence would actually discriminate in favor of revelation?} } \]

A fair comparison must leave that possibility open. If a revelation hypothesis can generate observations that the social model would not readily predict, then those observations could count in its favor. The question is therefore not whether revelation is conceivable. It is what an observable revelatory signal would look like. That is the task of the next section.

XX. What Would Count as Evidence for Revelation?

A fair model comparison should not merely identify weaknesses in a revelation hypothesis. It should also specify what kind of evidence could count in its favor. Otherwise the argument would risk becoming asymmetric in the opposite direction: constructing a social-historical model capable of explaining every observation while refusing in advance to recognize any possible evidence for supernatural disclosure. That would reproduce exactly the methodological problem criticized earlier.

Candidate forms of discriminating evidence

Candidate signal Why it could matter
Information surplus Reliable information not plausibly supplied by the local cultural environment
Risky novel prediction Specific advance claims whose failure would genuinely count against the revelatory hypothesis
Independent convergence Detailed agreement across populations lacking ordinary transmission channels
Reduced cultural indexing Substantive content that is less dependent on local conceptual resources than the social model predicts
Stable supernatural information A clearly identifiable core unusually resistant to ordinary cultural drift
Reliable correction of entrenched error Repeated truth-tracking departures from culturally expected false beliefs
Public anomalous evidence Intersubjectively accessible events strongly tied to a specific revelatory claim

The relevant question is therefore constructive:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What observable evidence would be substantially more expected if special revelation occurred than if religion were generated by }M\text{ alone?} } \]

Let:

\[ H_{R} = \text{a substantive special-revelation hypothesis}, \]

and:

\[ M = \text{the ordinary social-historical model}. \]

Then useful evidence for revelation would have the form: \(E^\ast\) such that:

\[ P(E^\ast\mid M+H_{R}) \gg P(E^\ast\mid M). \]

The larger this difference, the more strongly \(E^\ast\) discriminates between the models. This standard is important because evidence need not be impossible under \(M\) to favor revelation. It need only be sufficiently surprising under \(M\) relative to \(M+H_{R}\). Thus the target is not necessarily:

\[ P(E^\ast\mid M)=0. \]

It is:

\[ \frac{P(E^\ast\mid M+H_{R})} {P(E^\ast\mid M)} \gg1. \]

What kinds of observations might satisfy that condition?

Informational Signatures

Information surplus. One of the clearest possibilities would be information that exceeds what the surrounding cultural-historical environment can plausibly supply. Throughout the previous sections, religious development repeatedly tracked available conceptual resources. Narratives resembled older narratives. Theological problems developed within inherited intellectual traditions. Concepts of deity changed within regional religious ecologies. Supernatural ontologies developed through historical contact. Metaphysical articulation followed the availability of philosophical categories. Under \(M\), this is expected. A revelatory signal would therefore become more visible if a tradition contained substantial, reliable information that could not plausibly be reconstructed from:

  • prior beliefs;
  • neighboring traditions;
  • ordinary inference;
  • political circumstances;
  • literary conventions;
  • existing philosophy;
  • available empirical knowledge.

Call this:

\[ I_{\text{surplus}}. \]

Then strong evidence would satisfy:

\[ P(I_{\text{surplus}}\mid M+H_{R}) \gg P(I_{\text{surplus}}\mid M). \]

The important word is reliable. A religious text containing thousands of statements will inevitably contain occasional striking correspondences, ambiguous anticipations, or phrases that later readers can reinterpret in light of subsequent discoveries. The evidential value comes from specificity, independence, and successful testing. The information should not depend primarily on retrospective reinterpretation.

Novel and Risky Prediction. Closely related would be a genuinely novel prediction. Suppose a purported revelation clearly specifies an event or fact before it becomes knowable through ordinary means. The ideal structure would be:

\[ R_t \rightarrow P^\ast \]

at time \(t\), followed later by:

\[ E_{t+n} \]

confirming \(P^\ast\). The evidential value increases when:

  • the prediction is precise;
  • the timing is independently established;
  • the language is not highly elastic;
  • the outcome is improbable under ordinary background knowledge;
  • failure would clearly have counted against the claim.

This last condition matters. A prediction that can be declared fulfilled under almost any outcome possesses little evidential value. Strong prediction involves risk:

\[ \text{predictive success} \propto \text{degree of prior constraint}. \]

If a revelation repeatedly produced successful risky predictions beyond ordinary human ability, that would create exactly the kind of informational residual sought in this essay.

Cross-Cultural Independent Convergence. Another possible revelatory signature would be unusually specific convergence among populations lacking ordinary channels of transmission. Suppose two historically disconnected populations independently reported the same complex supernatural structure. The evidential importance would depend upon how specific the convergence was. General similarities such as:

  • powerful gods;
  • spirits;
  • ancestor survival;
  • moral norms;
  • sacred places;
  • ritual specialists

would not be surprising under \(M\), because common human cognition and recurring social problems can produce convergence. But suppose independent communities converged on:

\[ \{q_1,q_2,q_3,\ldots,q_n\} \]

where the \(q_i\) form an unusually detailed and coherent set of propositions about otherwise inaccessible supernatural reality. Then:

\[ P(\{q_1,\ldots,q_n\}\mid M) \]

could become quite low. If the same information appeared independently and reliably across disconnected communities, revelation or some other common external source would become more plausible. The evidential signal would be analogous to independent measurement. If several observers independently detect the same external structure using different paths, confidence that the structure is not merely locally constructed increases. In that sense, a revelatory world might contain something resembling cross-cultural replication.

Reduced Cultural Indexing. Another possible signal would be revelation that systematically resists local conceptual constraints. The previous model predicts:

\[ R_i \approx f(C_i,E_i,\ldots), \]

where the content of a revelation is substantially shaped by the individual's conceptual ecology. Strong revelation might instead introduce content with lower-than-expected dependence on \(C_i\). That does not require culturally unintelligible communication. Any message must be comprehensible to its audience. The issue is whether its substantive content displays some independence from the local system. We might represent this as:

\[ R_i = f(C_i) + I^\ast, \]

where \(I^\ast\) is information not plausibly generated by \(C_i\). The stronger and more reliable \(I^\ast\) becomes, the stronger the case for an external informational source. This is what might be called conceptual surplus.

Stability Where Stability Matters. A revelation hypothesis could also gain support if core supernatural information displayed unusual resistance to ordinary cultural drift. The history examined earlier showed change at several levels:

Causal pattern: narrative → theology → deity concept → supernatural ontology → metaphysics

A defender of revelation can explain this through progressive disclosure. But another possible evidential structure would be a clearly identifiable core that remains stable while peripheral cultural forms change. Let:

\[ R_t = C_t+K, \]

where \(C_t\) changes historically but \(K\) remains stable. If \(K\) contained substantive supernatural information and its preservation proved unusually resistant to the ordinary processes that alter religious systems, that could count in favor of revelation. The key issue would be identifying \(K\) independently, rather than defining the stable residue retrospectively as whatever happened to survive. Otherwise the argument becomes circular: what survived = the revealed core because: the revealed core = what survived. A useful revelatory core must be specified by independent theological or historical criteria.

Reliable Correction of Cultural Error. A particularly powerful signal would be systematic correction of culturally entrenched false beliefs before ordinary historical mechanisms would be expected to produce that correction. Suppose a society widely accepts:

\[ B_{\text{false}}. \]

A purported revelation rejects it despite strong cultural pressure. Later independent evidence establishes that the revelation was correct. If this occurred repeatedly, especially across domains unavailable to contemporary investigation, it would be noteworthy. The structure would be:

\[ \text{local culture} \rightarrow B_{\text{false}}, \]

but:

\[ R \rightarrow B_{\text{true}} \]

despite:

\[ P(B_{\text{true}}\mid C_t) \ll1. \]

This would represent a genuine truth-tracking deviation from cultural expectation. A few isolated examples would not suffice because dissent and lucky guessing occur naturally. The evidential strength would come from a systematic pattern.

Transmission, Public Evidence, and Comparative Performance

Resistance to transmission distortions. A revelatory hypothesis could also gain support if its essential information proved unusually robust against known transmission biases. Human communication is vulnerable to:

  • memory distortion;
  • translation;
  • prestige effects;
  • institutional filtering;
  • political pressure;
  • copying errors;
  • sectarian competition.

If a religious message were intended by a deity to communicate important truths, one possible expectation would be that those truths remain unusually recoverable despite these mechanisms. This does not require perfect preservation. But one might expect:

\[ P(\text{recoverable core truth}\mid M+H_{R}) > P(\text{recoverable core truth}\mid M). \]

Again, the precise expectation depends on the theology. A hiddenness-oriented theology may predict less clarity. But such a move changes the likelihood structure and must be evaluated accordingly. The important point is that a theory of revelation should say something about how well its intended message is expected to survive transmission.

Public Rather Than Exclusively Private Evidence. Another possible distinction concerns intersubjective accessibility. Many revelation claims depend heavily on private experiences:

  • visions;
  • auditions;
  • mystical states;
  • inner conviction;
  • personal miracles.

Such experiences may be powerful for the experiencer, but they create difficulties for outsiders because comparable private certainty occurs across incompatible religions. A stronger evidential situation would involve public effects independently accessible to observers with different prior commitments. Let:

\[ O_1,O_2,\ldots,O_n \]

represent observers from different backgrounds. A revelatory event with evidence: \(E^\ast\) that remains robust across:

\[ O_1,O_2,\ldots,O_n \]

would possess stronger intersubjective evidential value than an event whose credibility depends heavily on prior membership in the tradition. The analogy is not that religious evidence must function exactly like laboratory science. The point is simply that evidence becomes more discriminating when its evidential force is less dependent upon already accepting the framework it is supposed to support.

Truth-Tracking Across Rival Traditions. Perhaps the strongest possible test would concern comparative performance. Suppose many traditions generate revelation claims:

\[ R_1,R_2,\ldots,R_n. \]

Most cannot all be substantially correct because some make incompatible claims. If one tradition is genuinely revelation-guided, then it is reasonable to ask whether that tradition shows some systematic advantage in access to truth. Let:

\[ T(R_i) \]

represent the truth-tracking performance of tradition \(i\) on claims independently assessable to some meaningful degree. Then a substantive revelation model might predict:

\[ T(R_{\text{true}}) > T(R_{\text{false}}) \]

in some detectable domain. The relevant advantage need not be perfect moral behavior, political success, or scientific omniscience. But if revelation is supposed to supply genuine information, one might expect some measurable informational consequence. If every tradition exhibits approximately the same mixture of:

  • insight;
  • error;
  • cultural dependence;
  • internal disagreement;
  • revision;
  • reinterpretation;

then the causal contribution of unique revelation becomes harder to identify.

Miracles and Extraordinary Events. Miracle claims are an obvious candidate for discriminating evidence, although their evaluation requires a separate analysis beyond the scope of this essay. In principle, a well-supported event that is extremely improbable under the ordinary causal model could strongly favor a supernatural hypothesis. The relevant form is:

\[ P(E_{\text{miracle}}\mid M) \ll P(E_{\text{miracle}}\mid M+H_{R}). \]

But the evidential work cannot be done merely by labeling an event miraculous. The questions would include:

  • What exactly occurred?
  • How reliable is the evidence?
  • What alternative explanations exist?
  • Is the event genuinely improbable under ordinary causation?
  • Does it specifically support the revelation hypothesis in question rather than merely generic supernaturalism?
  • Do rival traditions possess evidentially comparable miracle claims?

These questions matter because: evidence for anomaly is not automatically: evidence for a particular theology. A miracle would need not only to challenge \(M\) but also to discriminate among competing supernatural explanations.

Moral and Transformative Evidence. Religions often appeal to moral transformation as evidence of divine origin. A revelation may produce:

  • unusual altruism;
  • forgiveness;
  • courage;
  • social reform;
  • personal transformation.

Such evidence should not be dismissed. But once again the comparison must be controlled. Human beings undergo profound transformation through many religious and nonreligious systems. Thus the relevant question is not:

\[ P(\text{transformation}\mid H_{R})>0. \]

It is:

\[ \frac{P(\text{distinctive transformation}\mid M+H_{R})} {P(\text{distinctive transformation}\mid M)} . \]

If one tradition produced reliably exceptional moral outcomes beyond what social organization, group identity, ritual, expectation, and community support could explain, that could provide evidence. But ordinary transformation alone is not strongly discriminating because \(M\) already predicts that powerful social systems can reshape human motivation and behavior.

Standards and Limits

What the argument does not require. It is important not to turn these possibilities into a rigid checklist. A true revelation need not display all of them. Nothing in the argument establishes that God must:

  • reveal scientific information;
  • eliminate all ambiguity;
  • produce universal religious agreement;
  • prevent all cultural influence;
  • bypass human language;
  • perform constant public miracles.

Those would be additional theological premises requiring justification. The point is more modest. If special revelation is to function as an explanation supported by religious history, there must be some detectable feature for which the revelatory hypothesis provides a meaningful comparative advantage. Otherwise, \(H_{R}\) may remain metaphysically possible without being historically evidenced.

What Would Change My Assessment? The position developed here is therefore not:

No possible evidence could support revelation.

It is closer to:

Much of the evidence commonly treated as evidence of revelation is also strongly expected under independently supported social-historical mechanisms, so stronger discrimination is required.

A finding that would materially alter the comparison would look something like:

\[ \boxed{ \text{a reliable, independently established pattern that }M \text{predicts poorly but }M+H_{R}\text{ predicts well}. } \]

Examples could include:

  • repeated highly specific knowledge unavailable to the relevant cultures;
  • robust independent convergence on complex supernatural information;
  • consistently successful risky prophecy;
  • a clear truth-tracking pattern separating one revelatory tradition from rivals;
  • public anomalous events tightly connected to specific revelatory claims;
  • substantive supernatural information resistant to ordinary cultural dependence.

Such evidence would not automatically prove revelation. But it would change the likelihood comparison. That is exactly what evidence is supposed to do.

The Epistemic Standard. The positive evidential standard can therefore be summarized as follows:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Revelation earns evidential support to the extent that adding it changes what we should expect to observe and the distinctive expectations are borne out.} } \]

This avoids two symmetrical mistakes. The first is theological insulation: whatever happens is compatible with revelation. The second is methodological naturalism turned into dogma: nothing could ever count for revelation. Neither provides genuine model comparison. The appropriate approach is conditional and comparative. Ask:

\[ \text{What does }M\text{ predict?} \]

Then:

\[ \text{What additional predictions follow from }R? \]

Then compare both with:

\[ E_{\text{total}}. \]

That framework leaves the supernatural hypothesis genuinely open while demanding that it perform explanatory work. And this brings the argument to its final conclusion.

Conclusion

XXI. Conclusion: Revelation as Explanandum Before Explanation

The central argument of this essay has not been that religious beliefs possess cultural origins and are therefore false. That would be a genetic fallacy. Every human belief has a causal history. Scientific theories, philosophical arguments, political commitments, and mathematical discoveries all arise through historically situated human beings. Genealogy alone does not determine truth. The argument has instead been about comparative explanation. Religion constitutes a large, observable system containing:

\[ \mathbf{D} = ( \mathbf B, \mathbf T, \mathbf X, \mathbf R, \mathbf I, \mathbf C, \mathbf G, \mathbf H, \mathbf Q, \ldots ), \]

including beliefs, texts, experiences, rituals, institutions, communities, geographic distributions, historical developments, revelation claims, and supernatural ontologies. The explanatory task is to ask what process generates this system. The answer developed throughout the essay has been a multilevel social-historical model: \(M\). At the micro level, individuals experience, interpret, remember, innovate, imitate, and testify. At the meso level, communities, priesthoods, churches, scribal schools, councils, sects, and educational institutions select and stabilize particular forms. At the macro level, empires, migrations, wars, demographic change, political patronage, literacy, technology, and trade restructure the environment in which religions develop. Across all three operates the conceptual ecology: \(C_t\), the historically available repertoire of language, metaphysics, mythology, cosmology, scriptural interpretation, and supernatural categories. The resulting religious system is recursive:

\[ R_{t+1} = F( R_t, C_t, I_t, P_t, N_t, E_t, D_t, S_t, \Phi_t, \epsilon_t ). \]

Across time, the system inherits prior states, generates variation, selects among alternatives, diffuses through networks, adapts to changing environments, preserves some forms while losing others, fragments into competing lineages, recombines inherited material, and retrospectively interprets its own history. The model is not speculative machinery invented solely to explain religion; its components are independently visible throughout human social life. That gives it considerable explanatory power.

What the Historical Cases Added

The five historical cases illustrated this model at progressively deeper levels. The comparison between Sargon and Moses showed that sacred narrative participates in a larger literary ecology. This did not require arguing that Exodus simply copied the Sargon legend. The more important result was that sacred narratives can inherit, transform, invert, and recombine existing cultural forms. Thus, narrative form has genealogy. The Mesopotamian suffering traditions and Job moved deeper: the problem of innocent suffering participated in a centuries-long Near Eastern conversation about divine justice, human guilt, inscrutability, and suffering. Job could be both original and historically dependent. Thus, theological problem-space has genealogy.

The historical development of YHWH pushed deeper still. Ancient Israelite concepts of deity developed within a West Semitic religious environment; divine-council imagery, El traditions, diverse Yahwistic practices, increasing exclusivity, anthropomorphic representations, and later theological abstraction all show substantial historical layering. Thus, the concept of God has genealogy. The Iranian-Jewish comparison widened the analysis from God to the larger supernatural order. Resurrection, apocalyptic history, angelic hierarchies, cosmic opposition, judgment, and the final destiny of creation developed within a world of prolonged cultural interaction. Specific borrowing claims remain debated, and simplistic identifications should be avoided, but the wider pattern remains: supernatural ontology has history.

Finally, Hellenistic Judaism and Christianity revealed historical dependence at the level of philosophical articulation itself. Greek language, translation, Middle Platonism, Stoicism, Logos traditions, and eventually Neoplatonic conceptual resources contributed to the intellectual framework within which Christian thinkers described divine transcendence, immateriality, evil, and metaphysical being. Thus, the metaphysics through which revelation is interpreted also has genealogy. The cumulative progression was:

\[ \boxed{ \text{narrative} \rightarrow \text{problem-space} \rightarrow \text{deity concept} \rightarrow \text{supernatural ontology} \rightarrow \text{metaphysics}. } \]

At each stage, the cultural model penetrated more deeply into material that might otherwise have been treated as straightforward evidence of supernatural disclosure.

From Historical Cases to Model Comparison

From historical cases to a general causal pattern. The force of these examples lies not in any one proposed borrowing relationship. The argument survives even if individual cases are weakened. The deeper result is convergence upon one causal morphology: inheritance + network interaction + variation + selection + reinterpretation + institutionalization + path dependence. This pattern does not occur only in ancient Israel. It appears throughout religious history. It explains why religions cluster geographically. It explains why children disproportionately inherit the religions of their parents. It explains why neighboring traditions influence one another. It explains why language affects theology. It explains why political borders and institutional structures affect religious development. It explains why canonization involves selection. It explains why religions fragment into sects. It explains why mature traditions contain historically layered materials. It explains why new revelations continue appearing within traditions that already possess revelation. Most importantly, it does so using mechanisms independently known to operate. This gives the social-historical model considerable explanatory compression:

\[ M \rightarrow \{E_1,E_2,E_3,\ldots,E_n\}. \]

Many apparently distinct religious phenomena can be understood through a comparatively small family of mechanisms.

Revelation Claims Enter the Explanandum. This produces the essay's most important methodological reversal. A revelation claim should not automatically be placed at the beginning of the causal chain:

Causal pattern: God → revelation → religion

Before revelation is allowed to explain the system, revelation claims themselves should be included among the phenomena requiring explanation. Human societies generate:

  • prophets;
  • visions;
  • sacred texts;
  • mystical experiences;
  • miracle reports;
  • divine commands;
  • charismatic founders;
  • claims of final revelation.

And they generate them across incompatible religious systems. The first systems question is therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What generates populations of claims like these?} } \]

The social-historical model provides a substantial answer. Experiences occur. Conceptual ecologies interpret them. Charismatic claims attract followers. Institutions select among them. Communities preserve successful ones. Later generations encounter the survivors as sacred tradition. This creates:

Causal pattern: experience → interpretation → revelatory claim → community → institution → canon → retrospective validation

Revelation therefore begins as part of the explanandum. Only afterward do we ask whether supernatural causation must be added.

The Central Asymmetry. This reframing produces an important asymmetry. A revelation hypothesis cannot explain the actual religious ecology without the social-historical model. Even if Christianity is true, Christianity's history still requires explanations involving:

  • institutions;
  • languages;
  • politics;
  • philosophy;
  • networks;
  • demographic transmission;
  • textual selection;
  • interpretation.

Thus:

\[ M+H_{R} \]

is required. But the social model can explain a great deal without revelation:

\[ M \rightarrow \text{large portions of the observable religious ecology}. \]

The relevant model comparison is therefore not:

\[ M \quad\text{versus}\quad H_{R}. \]

It is:

\[ \boxed{ M \quad\text{versus}\quad M+H_{R}. } \]

That distinction changes everything. The question is no longer whether religion has natural causes rather than supernatural ones. A sophisticated revelation model can include both. The question becomes:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What explanatory contribution does }H_{R}\text{ make once }M\text{ is already included?} } \]

The Counterfactual Result. The counterfactual test sharpened this issue. What would religion look like if no special revelation were occurring? Given the mechanisms in \(M\), we should expect:

  • geographic clustering;
  • inherited affiliation;
  • network diffusion;
  • syncretism;
  • translation effects;
  • doctrinal drift;
  • sectarian fragmentation;
  • competing revelations;
  • institutionally mediated canon formation;
  • culturally indexed supernatural concepts;
  • historical adaptation;
  • retrospective harmonization.

That hypothetical world looks remarkably like the observable one. The result is not:

\[ P(E\mid M)=1. \]

Nor is it:

\[ P(E\mid M+H_{R})=0. \]

The significant claim is comparative:

\[ P(E_{\text{obs}}\mid M) \]

appears substantial. Therefore the existence of:

\[ E_{\text{obs}} \]

does not automatically provide strong evidence for revelation. A revelatory model needs some additional discriminating feature.

The Theological Reply and the Evidential Standard

The strongest theological response. The essay therefore considered accommodation, progressive revelation, and providence in their strongest forms. These responses deserve serious treatment. Accommodation explains why revelation would use local cultural forms. Progressive revelation explains why theological content would unfold historically. Providence allows divine action to operate through ordinary social causes. Together they can make revelation compatible with nearly all the historical evidence discussed. But this success creates another problem. The more completely divine action is identified with the ordinary historical process, the harder it becomes to distinguish \(M\) from

\[ M+H_{R}. \]

If God intentionally works through exactly the same mechanisms and produces exactly the same observable history that would occur under \(M\), then:

\[ P(E\mid M+H_{R}) \approx P(E\mid M). \]

The revelation hypothesis remains possible. But the historical evidence ceases to discriminate strongly in its favor. This produces the tradeoff:

\[ \boxed{ \text{greater compatibility} \longleftrightarrow \text{lower empirical detectability}. } \]

Theology can protect itself from historical disconfirmation by emphasizing accommodation and providence. But the more fully it does so, the less the observed religious history itself can function as evidence for special revelation.

Compatibility Is Not Confirmation. That distinction is central. The fact that \(E\) does not contradict \(H_{R}\) does not establish that \(E\) supports \(H_{R}\). Evidence favors a model comparatively when:

\[ P(E\mid M+H_{R}) > P(E\mid M). \]

The crucial question is therefore not:

Could God have produced this religious history?

Given sufficiently flexible assumptions about divine intention, the answer will almost always be yes. The more informative question is:

Would this religious history have been substantially more expected if this tradition were uniquely guided by special revelation?

And conversely:

Would this religious history have been surprising under ordinary cultural-historical development?

The second question is increasingly difficult to answer affirmatively. The ordinary model predicts a world remarkably like ours.

Why This Is Not a Genetic Fallacy

The argument can now be distinguished clearly from the genetic fallacy. The invalid form would be:

\[ \text{belief }B\text{ has cultural causes} \therefore B\text{ is false}. \]

That conclusion does not follow. The argument here has a different form:

\[ M \text{ explains why }B\text{ arose, spread, and took its observed form}. \]

Then, \(H_{R}\) is proposed as an additional causal explanation.

Therefore ask:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What observable work does }H_{R}\text{ perform beyond }M? } \]

If independent evidence supports \(B\), its cultural genealogy does not defeat it. A scientific theory can have a complete sociological history and still be well supported because it predicts external observations. A mathematical discovery can be culturally transmitted while remaining demonstrably true. The issue for revelation is therefore not genealogy itself. It is whether the tradition supplies independent truth-tracking evidence that cannot be reduced to the process explaining its production.

The Broader Religious Ecology. This becomes particularly important when the explanandum is expanded beyond one tradition. The world contains:

\[ R_1,R_2,\ldots,R_n \]

religious systems making incompatible supernatural claims. Each contains some mixture of:

  • sacred texts;
  • authority structures;
  • miraculous narratives;
  • revelation experiences;
  • doctrinal development;
  • apologetic defenses;
  • traditions of interpretation.

The social model applies across all of them. Thus:

\[ M(R_1), M(R_2),\ldots,M(R_n) \]

possess common causal structure. A special-revelation hypothesis, however, usually singles out one member of that population, \(R_k\). The question then becomes:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What distinguishes }R_k\text{ from the surrounding population in a way predicted by genuine revelation?} } \]

That is the evidential challenge. The true religion need not be culturally independent. It need not be free of history. It need not be philosophically complete from the beginning. But if its truth makes no detectable difference to the evidential distribution, then historical analysis cannot identify it as uniquely revealed.

What Would Make Revelation Evidentially Visible? The preceding section identified several possibilities:

  • genuine information surplus;
  • risky and successful novel predictions;
  • independent cross-cultural convergence;
  • reduced dependence on local conceptual resources;
  • stable core supernatural knowledge;
  • reliable correction of culturally entrenched error;
  • unusual resistance to transmission distortion;
  • public and intersubjectively accessible evidence;
  • superior truth-tracking relative to rival traditions;
  • well-supported extraordinary events tightly linked to specific revelatory claims.

None is demanded as a necessary condition. The broader principle is simply:

\[ \boxed{ H_{R}\text{ should make some observable difference to what we expect.} } \]

Without such a difference, revelation ceases to function as an empirically informative explanatory variable. It becomes a metaphysical interpretation layered over an already sufficient causal history. That interpretation may still be true. But it is not established by the religious history itself.

The Result. The argument therefore stops short of deductively establishing either \(\neg G\) or \(\neg H_{R}\). Bare theism is not the primary target. The claim:

\[ G= \text{“some supernatural reality exists”} \]

predicts too little for the historical argument to address directly. The stronger target is:

\[ H_{R}^{\ast}= \text{“this particular tradition is uniquely and reliably guided by special revelation.”} \]

Such a claim makes contact with history. Once it does, it can be evaluated comparatively. And the historical record presents a challenge. The observable religious world shows pervasive horizontal, vertical, genealogical, network, institutional, and conceptual dependence. Those are exactly the kinds of patterns a cultural system should produce. The ordinary mechanisms possess independent support. They operate across rival religions. They generate counterfactual predictions. They explain both continuity and change. They compress many observations into a common causal framework. The addition of special revelation therefore bears an explanatory burden:

\[ \boxed{ \text{show what remains unexplained without it.} } \]

That is the central thesis.

Revelation as Explanandum Before Explanation

The methodological conclusion can now be stated in its final form:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Revelation should first be treated as an explanandum before it is treated as an explanation.} } \]

We should first ask why human beings produce revelation claims. Why do those claims cluster culturally? Why do their contents resemble local supernatural ontologies? Why do they change historically? Why do institutions select some and reject others? Why do competing religions produce structurally similar claims? Why do later traditions reinterpret earlier revelations through newly available concepts? Only after understanding that generative process should revelation be introduced as an additional causal variable. Then the final question becomes unavoidable:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What does revelation explain that the generative system does not?} } \]

If a compelling answer exists, then the revelation hypothesis deserves corresponding evidential weight. If no such residual can be identified, the social-historical model possesses an important comparative advantage. Not because cultural origins imply falsity. Not because supernatural causes have been ruled out by definition. Not because every religious phenomenon has already been exhaustively explained. But because the world of religion—its geography, history, texts, revelations, gods, supernatural ontologies, metaphysics, institutions, and disagreements—looks strikingly like the output of the ordinary causal processes we independently know generate cultural systems. The question is therefore no longer whether revelation can be made consistent with that fact. It is whether the evidence gives us reason to add revelation to the explanation. That is a harder question. And it is the one that should have been asked from the beginning.
