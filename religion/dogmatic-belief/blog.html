Dogmatic Belief

This essay is about dogmatism as a pattern of belief and behavior: how it can be recognized, how it protects itself from criticism, how it becomes entangled with identity and group dynamics, and how a conversation might proceed once those patterns are visible.

Core claim: Dogmatism is not simply strong conviction. It is a mismatch between confidence and epistemic warrant, maintained by psychological, rhetorical, or social mechanisms that make appropriate recalibration unusually difficult.

Part I

Framing the Problem

Define the phenomenon carefully before trying to diagnose it.

What I Mean by Dogma

The word dogma can refer to strong assent to a proposition about a non-evident matter. In its colloquial sense, it often describes a belief held unquestioningly and with a high degree of conviction, credence, or certainty despite highly questionable grounds—or, in some cases, no articulated grounds at all. The term can refer to a proposition (“the XYZ dogma”) or to a person’s manner of holding a belief (“that person’s adherence to XYZ is dogmatic”). It usually carries a derogatory connotation: rigidity, stifling inflexibility, and sometimes an authoritarian or totalizing force.

What matters here is not merely the strength of a belief. A belief becomes dogmatic, in the sense I am interested in, when it is effectively taken off the table for rational or evidential deliberation. Someone can have a dogmatic attitude toward a single proposition or toward an entire ideology. At the same time, all of us probably have propositions we treat more dogmatically than we would like to admit, so the concept should be used partly as a tool for self-examination rather than merely as a label for opponents.

The label is also easy to abuse. Flat-earthers, for example, sometimes call the scientific community “dogmatic.” I think that is a misuse of the term, but it illustrates the broader problem: calling a person dogmatic cannot substitute for showing how a belief is insulated from criticism or why its level of confidence is poorly matched to its evidential support.

My purpose, therefore, is practical. I want to offer a set of criteria that can help identify patterns of dogmatic belief and, just as importantly, help someone understand what kind of conversation they are actually having. The framework is a checklist of sorts, but not a binary test. Its purpose is to illuminate an interlocutor’s epistemic, rhetorical, psychological, and social constraints so that one can choose a more appropriate strategy for achieving conversational goals.

Why Dogmatism Matters

This essay is particularly concerned with ideologies that are highly contestable, tightly coupled to other ideological systems, and diffused through the public in forms that lose nuance as they spread. In their most vulgar forms, such systems can produce significant harm. The same concern applies to dogmatic ideologies that cascade through societies at the grassroots level through populist movements, as well as beliefs inherited from communities in which doubt and questioning are treated as fundamentally subversive.

It is also concerned with people who truly believe in a cause at the expense of everything else: zealotry that can strip humanity from others in the name of a greater good. And it is concerned with dogmatic systems that prevent individuals from growing, exploring, or finding new solutions because people around them react to questioning with fear, fragility, control, or appeals to “care” that function in practice as pressure to preserve the existing dogma.

A distinction that matters throughout: high confidence is not itself dogmatism. High confidence can be warranted. The diagnostic question is whether confidence remains responsive to the quality of its justification and to relevant counterevidence.

To understand why suspension, doubt, and belief revision matter, it helps to begin with the historical tradition that made resistance to dogmatism a central philosophical practice.

Part II

The Skeptical Counterpoint

Ancient skepticism provides a vocabulary for interrogating certainty without requiring permanent indecision.

Why Skepticism Matters

An ancient school of skepticism known as Pyrrhonism was among the traditions that recommended suspension of judgement as a remedy to dogmatism. The Pyrrhonists were responding to rival philosophies in Ancient Greece that, from the Pyrrhonian point of view, claimed to possess truth about non-evident matters. Those rivals were the “dogmatists.”

those who are ephectic (engaged in suspension of judgment), aporetic (engaged in refutation)[6] or zetetic (engaged in seeking).[7] An ephectic merely suspends judgment on a matter, "balancing perceptions and thoughts against one another,"[8] It is a less aggressive form of skepticism, in that sometimes "suspension of judgment evidently just happens to the sceptic".[9] An aporetic skeptic, in contrast, works more actively towards their goal, engaging in the refutation of arguments in favor of various possible beliefs in order to reach aporia, an impasse, or state of perplexity,[10] which leads to suspension of judgement.[9] Finally, the zetetic claims to be continually searching for the truth but to have thus far been unable to find it, and thus continues to suspend belief while also searching for reason to cease the suspension of belief.

The ancient skeptics’ goal in suspending judgment was to reach a state of ataraxia: a kind of tranquility or unperturbedness, characterized by robust equanimity and freedom from distress and worry. In the Pyrrhonian picture, dogmatic philosophies interfere with ataraxia, so skeptical practice aims to loosen the grip of premature certainty.

Pyrrhonists pursued this through “modes” or “tropes”: epistemological questions and argumentative strategies intended to expose how someone claims to know a non-evident matter, or to show why suspension of judgment may be appropriate. The best-known groups of modes are associated with Sextus Empiricus, Diogenes Laërtius, Aenesidemus, and Agrippa. The lists below preserve those modes because they anticipate many of the modern diagnostic questions developed later in this essay.

What I am borrowing from Pyrrhonism: not a requirement to suspend judgment about everything, but a habit of interrogating the route by which certainty is produced—disagreement, assumptions, circularity, alternative explanations, perspective, and the possibility that our justification does not warrant our confidence.

The Skeptical Toolkit

The Ten Modes

The Ten Modes associated with Aenesidemus emphasize variation in perception, circumstance, relation, custom, and social conditioning.

  1. The mode depending on the variations among animals (I 40–78); Different animals manifest different modes of perception.
  2. The mode depending on the differences among humans (I 79–90); Similar differences are seen among individual men.
  3. The mode depending on the differing constitutions of the sense-organs (I 91–99); For the same man, information perceived with the senses is self-contradictory.
  4. The mode depending on circumstance (I 100–117); The sensory information varies from time to time with physical changes.
  5. The mode depending on positions and intervals and places (I 118–23); The sensory data also varies and differs according to local relations. 
  6. The mode depending on admixtures (I 124–28); Objects are known only indirectly through the medium of air, moisture, etc.
  7. The mode depending on the quantities and preparations of existing things (I 129–34); These objects are in a condition of perpetual change in color, temperature, size and motion .
  8. The mode deriving from relativity (I 135–40); All perceptions are relative and interact one upon another.
  9. The mode depending on frequent or rare encounters (I 141–44); Our impressions become less critical through repetition and custom.
  10. The mode depending on persuasions and customs and laws and belief in myths and dogmatic suppositions (I 145–62). All men are brought up with different beliefs, under different laws and social conditions .

The Five Modes of Agrippa

The Five Modes are especially useful here because they can be restated as compact diagnostic questions about justification.

ModeDiagnostic use
DissentPersistent disagreement among apparently competent people is a reason to examine whether confidence exceeds what the available justification can bear.
Infinite regressAsk what supports the offered proof, then what supports that support. A justification cannot simply defer its burden forever.
RelationExamine how perception, judgment, and description depend on perspective, circumstances, or relations.
AssumptionIdentify premises that are being treated as given even though they remain unsupported or contestable.
CircularityCheck whether the conclusion is being used, directly or indirectly, to support the premises that supposedly establish it.

The Eight Modes

R. J. Hankinson offers another taxonomy consisting of eight modes in The Sceptics. In the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s summary, these modes focus especially on causal explanations and on the skeptic’s ability to put pressure on a favored explanation by raising competing explanations:

"They are modes in accordance with which we bring the Dogmatists to a halt by raising puzzles about their particular causal explanations—we do this because they pride themselves on these especially. (PH I 180)"

These modes suggest lines of attack that the Skeptic could adopt in response to those arguments of the Dogmatists which attempt to discern causes. For instance,

"according to the second [mode], some people often give an explanation in only one way, although there is a rich abundance enabling them to explain the object of investigation in a variety of ways. (PH I 181)"

The idea here is that the Skeptic can apply pressure to the Dogmatist’s attempted explanation by pointing out that there is an equally good alternative explanation. Sextus himself suggests that the Eight Modes are superseded by the Five Modes (PH I 185), and like the Two Modes, Sextus does not employ them explicitly during the course of his writings.

I find these traditions fascinating because, contrary to conventional assumptions about certainty, clinging to a dogmatic ideology can look more insecure than admitting ignorance. Ancient skepticism matters here because it offers a contrast to dogmatism and because its methods correspond to many of the questions developed below.

More importantly, a skeptical attitude can function as a defense against naïve interventionism, unintended consequences, and the human costs that can follow when uncertain propositions are treated as unquestionable truths. Unlike dogmatic commitment, withholding assent is not ordinarily the motive by which people wage war, slaughter innocents, or impose authoritarian systems. Withholding assent is also not the same as refusing to act forever; it is a way of calibrating belief and action to what the available reasons can actually support.

Modern Skepticism and Critical Thinking

Modern uses of the word skeptic are inspired by, rather than direct continuations of, the ancient schools. The modern use generally has little to do with achieving epoché or ataraxia. Instead, skepticism is an attitude, process, mental model, and set of methods for withholding assent when our standards of acceptance have not been satisfied.

A task of the critical thinker is to judge when suspension of judgment is consistent with rational and evidential principles: when to accept a proposition, at least tentatively, and when the burden of proof—including the Burden of production and Burden of persuasion—has not reached a reasonable threshold. The goal is not permanent doubt. It is calibrated assent.

Critical thinking is broader than skepticism alone. It belongs to a larger skill set that includes strategizing, reflection, evaluation, analysis, and the search for a critical path when a domain requires action. It also requires reservation when presented with false dichotomies, creative nuance when pressed with crude simplifications, and, most importantly, self-reflection. Doubt is not something to fear.

Dogma, Assumptions, and Axioms

Before turning to the diagnostic framework, it is useful to contrast dogma with an Axiom. As the Five Modes make clear, reasoning requires assumptions. Hume’s problem of induction; statistical assumptions such as normality of errors or independent, identically distributed observations; computational hardness assumptions in complexity theory; the closed-world assumption in knowledge-representation systems; tacit assumptions in normative decision-making; and assumptions embedded in theoretical frameworks such as transitive preferences and utility maximization in economic models all illustrate the same point: reasoning must begin somewhere.

In mathematical modeling, assumptions can often be made explicit so that we know what must be true for a relationship, inference, or prediction to hold. Dogmatic ideologies frequently work differently. Their adherents may be unable to state the assumptions supporting the ideology because those assumptions have been absorbed rather than examined. Making assumptions explicit is not, by itself, a remedy for dogmatism, but it is an important distinction: an assumption is a starting point in reasoning; dogmatism concerns how that starting point and the conclusions drawn from it are held and defended.

Some ideologues call their systems axiomatic or grounded in first principles as though the label itself protected them from refutation. Axioms, like assumptions, are not proved within the system in which they function, but the comparison has limits. Many axioms used in mathematics are simple rules that define and constrain permissible inference—for example, an equality axiom such as X = X or the probability axiom that probabilities are non-negative. Declaring a complex political or social ideology “axiomatic” is a very different move from specifying the rules of a formal system.

The larger principle is calibration: the more uncertain the assumptions in our reasoning, the less confident we should be in the inferences that depend on them. This is one reason economic theories are sometimes accused of dogmatism. Some models depend on contestable assumptions, yet their conclusions can migrate into policy advice and then into popular ideology as if those premises were established facts. In their vulgarized forms, they can produce zealous adherents of positions such as Market Fundamentalism or Marxism who have little experience interrogating the assumptions of their preferred economic theories.

The historical and conceptual material supplies the method. The next step is to turn it into a practical framework for recognizing when a belief system has become unusually resistant to correction.

Part III

Diagnostic Framework

The original checklist is reorganized into five families so that related mechanisms can be seen together.

How to Use the Framework

This is not an exhaustive list, nor do I claim it is the uniquely correct set of criteria. More importantly, the criteria diagnose patterns of belief and behavior; they should not be treated as a binary test of whether a person simply “is” or “is not” a dogmatist. The mechanisms overlap, occur by degrees, and can appear temporarily in otherwise thoughtful people.

Four interpretive cautions

  • Falsifiability: unfalsifiability is especially concerning when an ideology makes empirical or causal claims while insulating those claims from possible disconfirmation. Not every moral, mathematical, definitional, or metaphysical proposition is supposed to be empirically falsifiable.
  • Narrative: narrative structure is not itself dogmatic. The warning sign is a totalizing, self-sealing narrative in which every event—including apparent counterexamples—is automatically redescribed as confirmation.
  • Social origin: being socially introduced to a belief is not itself dogmatism; virtually everyone inherits large parts of a worldview before being able to defend them. The stronger warning sign is a continued inability or unwillingness to examine inherited beliefs once meaningful challenges arise.
  • Confidence: confidence should track the strength of the justification. The framework is therefore less about asking “How certain are you?” than “What could appropriately move that certainty up or down?”

The five families below distinguish properties of claims from mechanisms of belief maintenance, conversational defense, identity formation, and social enforcement. Some observations could fit more than one family; the point of the taxonomy is not to force perfect boundaries but to make the structure of dogmatism easier to see.

Resistance to revisionCan relevant new information change the belief?Counterexample avoidance, ad hoc rescue, cherry-picking, closed-system behavior, special pleading, refusal of hypotheticals

Five Families of Dogmatic Behavior

A. Epistemic Structure

What kinds of claims does the system make, and how well are those claims specified and supported?

  1. Vague or anti-empirical claims. The ideology or belief is riddled with anti-empirical theories. The theories also happen to be incredibly vaguely formed, using imprecise definitions and unclear language. 
  2. Empirical claims insulated from falsification. The set of beliefs rests on concepts, facts, and hypotheses that are unfalsifiable. In principle, there is no standard or test by which to confirm or deny basic claims. This is a problem because there is then no way to evaluate the system as a whole. 
  3. Pseudo-correlations treated as causal signals. The ideology rests upon pseudo-correlations and consistently produces behaviors in accordance with the alleged signs.
  4. Unverifiable attributions of motive. There is a strong reliance on unverifiable assertions about motive. Ideologies tend to be very teleological, containing pre-loaded explanatory schemes for why an agent decided to act in a situation. If there seems to be a checklist of explanations, but they don't take into consideration case-specific information, you are likely dealing with a belief system of some sort. If the assertions are completely unverifiable, it's probably a dogma. 
  5. Reducing a complex landscape to polarized categories. Unjustifiably reducing nuance and complexity into a polarized characterization of the intellectual landscape. 
  6. A totalizing, self-sealing narrative. The ideology is dependent on a narrative structure. I don't mean this in the loose sense it is currently applied. I mean that there is a beginning, middle and end. A climax, characters, and a resolution. There is a teleology; a directedness to the belief system. All of history can be explained by this telos. Predictions occur due to this telos. Marxism, Abrahamic religions, philosophical romanticism, and even Enlightenment could all fall under this umbrella. Obviously, the degree of rigidness varies from system to system, and person to person. The structure of the narrative also determines the odds of a dogmatic devotion to the core principles.
    • Are there fear mongering analogies?
    • Does the ideology divide people into humans and sub-humans?
    • Is there an urgent sense to act immediately or else everything will be ruined be the group demarcated as evil? 

B. Resistance to Revision

What happens when counterexamples, disconfirming evidence, inconsistencies, or alternative frames appear?

  1. Aversion to counterexamples. There is a strong aversion to counterexamples. If they are not immediately disregarded, there is a process of subsuming them under the ideology with minimal modification to the ideology. If this does not work, they are called "outliers", "fake facts", "contrived by the opposition", or evil.
  2. Disdain for belief revision. Disdain for belief revision in light of conflicting or contrary information. The group has a tendency to reaffirm this behavior, making it seem like it's correct, and in fact, the noble or righteous thing to do. 
    • In the case where counterevidence is overwhelming, ad hoc adjustments might be made to the dogma. 
    • They will cherry-pick data confirming their views and disregard any disconfirming evidence
    • Anything falling under the misrepresentation of evidence falls under this umbrella. 
    • Total evidence must be considered and their prior likelihoods and prior probabilities; if the belief system disregards this stage, that is a clear indication of dogma. If they assign high likelihood to prior probabilities without any justification, this is a very clear indication of dogma.
  3. Closed-system reactions to scrutiny. What happens when beliefs are scrutinized? Is the ideology a closed system? Can new insights change the moral landscape of the belief system? 
    • If you propose a change and face unreasonable backlash, it's probably a dogma you are dealing with. 
    • What are the "trigger words" that evoke negative emotions such as hate, anger, suspicion, or disgust? 
    • The person usually cannot provide reasons for why simple words evoke such associations. 
    • A good sign of dogma is sharp reactions when exposed to the existence of some perceived opposition. 
    • You have reflex reactions such as guilt or anxiety at the thought of questioning certain propositions held by your ideological community.
    • You automatically comply or deny.
  4. Special pleading for one’s own system. The person engages in special pleading when someone points out inconsistencies within their ideological system. They obviously do not give this charity to their opposition or other belief systems, calling themselves "The Exception" (capitalized words purposely). 
  5. Resistance to perspective-changing hypotheticals. The person recoils or finds "unrealistic" hypothetical thought experiments that might give more credence to their enemies, such as the Veil of Ignorance. There is no "just society" except their own. 

C. Argumentative and Rhetorical Defense

What happens in conversation when the belief is challenged?

  1. Deflecting criticism through false-equivalence claims. When flaws in the ideology are pointed out, there is a tendency to attribute false equivalence to the interlocutor. This is a means of avoiding criticism, mainly because the person does not have a well thought out defense.
  2. Distorting or obfuscating opposing positions. The person denies, distorts, or obfuscates any critiques of their own position instead of representing alternatives in an accurate light. They may also apply this lack of charity to entire belief systems or ideologies contrary to their own. They might even introduce irrelevant points that mislead people. Perhaps they are not doing it deliberately; it might be their only mechanism to reduce cognitive dissonance.
    • The person might attribute an exaggerated strawman. For example, if someone makes a moderate claim with qualifications, the person attributes a far more extreme view than expressed. 
    • Anything that can fall under the label "Mischaracterization of Opponents" will land here. 
  3. Overreliance on emotional and authority-based appeals. Overwhelming reliance on emotional appeals to get their point across. 
    • Constant use of loaded language
    • Repeated use of loaded questions to condemn opposition through guilt
    • Has no authentic opinions of their own, argues from perceived authority and dismisses testimony from equally credible experts who hold contrary views. There are many ways someone can misrepresent an authority, anything related to this falls under this criteria. 
  4. Automatic defenses against alternative perspectives. The person has automatic defense mechanisms against other ideas without even knowing why or how the defenses are justified. 
    • One corollary: they can't even state clearly the opposition (something I've alluded to above), and when you try to (and strawman them), you call them a "liar" for disagreeing with your characterization of them.
    • A second corollary: you don't even know anyone from the group who holds opposing views. 
    • A third corollary; the mere possibility of viewing something from an alternative perspective is uncomfortable to you. 
  5. Treating debate as a zero-sum contest. The person views debate as adversarial, something to "win". Their conversational goals are not to build partnerships or a collective pursuit of truth. They do not view listening as a better way to understand their interlocutor. Letting the other person speak feels like a "loss". They struggle to charitably represent their interlocutor's position out of fear that they might be accepting that position. They cannot distinguish between stating someone else's argument versus accepting the argument. 
    • All of this might be amplified by their group membership out of fear of retribution.
    • Their goal is "winning" not "understanding"
    • They view argument as a zero-sum game. 

D. Identity and Intergroup Dynamics

What happens when belief becomes fused with identity, loyalty, enemies, and moral status?

  1. Treating criticism as an attack on the group or belief. Generally, the belief is impervious to criticism by a variety of mechanisms in addition to the ones listed above; but furthermore, criticism is viewed as an attack. Someone is called "Anti - X" when X is scrutinized, equating criticism with oppression.
    • Siege mentality; the idea that your group is constantly under attack, a form of Persecution complex. Sustaining this mentality requires a constant supply of enemies. There is also an implicit desire to sustain this belief.
    • Dissociation from the out-group, taught to fear them perhaps (depending on the internal dynamics of the dogmatic belief system). They are responsible for all of the malevolence in the world. 
    • Hereditary guilt, or guilt by association. Someone is born of group X, or associates with group X, or has any sort of affiliation with group X and respects their right to their existence, is seen to inherit all of the flaws associated with the out group.
  2. Demonizing the out-group. Demonizing the behaviors and individuals of those belonging to the group believed to be hostile to the person's ideology. Writing off the out-group as sick or evil. Reasoning in a circle; "People believe X because they are (sick, evil, twisted etc.) and they are (sick, evil, twisted etc.) because they believe X". 
    • Anything related to the mischaracterization of groups falls under this category. 
  3. Fusing identity with group affiliation. Conflates identity with the group affiliation.
    • Worries about second order punishment if they express an opinion contrary to the monolith of which they belong. This includes group behaviors such as social exclusion and other forms of anti social punishment. 
    • Engages in applying second order punishment to those doubting the established dogma of the group. Says they were "never a true X" (no true Scotsman) when group members diverge. 
    • Ask yourself what the nature of the reward/punishment system is for diverging heterogeneous thought; is it valued? 
    • Is inquisitiveness punished? If doubt comes from inconsistencies, is it dismissed and redirected back to you as your flaw? Are people defensive when you genuinely ask questions? Is doubting shamed?
    • When you meet someone from the out-group who is typically demonized by your in-group, instead of adjusting your belief that "all people from group X are bad", you instead say "they aren't a true X", and grant them exemption while retaining the unquestioning belief in the original proposition that they are all bad. Or you might selectively cherry-pick; any flaws in the person's character or behavior is turned against them (serving as evidence of your dogma) while the goodness is ignored, in order to alleviate your cognitive dissonance. This is a sort of repacking of someone else's behavior, they couldn't have been good, it was evil all along. You frame their actions in such a way that it was bad all along and you either just couldn't see it or they were manipulative (putting them in a no-win situation). Good behavior is evidence they are bad. As a corollary, you avoid situations that could lead to further dissonance (like hanging out with them or discussing controversial topics with them that might result in you changing your mind). In short, you don't adjust to this new information, you deny, select, avoid, and reframe. 
  4. Automatic in-group preference followed by rationalization. When faced with a decision between their in-group and out-group, they automatically select their in-group, and ad hoc rationalize or motivationally justify the decision after the fact, even when someone in the out-group is suffering. Even at the expense of our common humanity.

E. Socialization and Enforcement

How was the belief acquired, and what social mechanisms maintain public or private assent?

  1. Socially inherited or engineered assent. The person was not reasoned into belief; its validity rests on other foundations.
    • Typically a product of generations of belief in XYZ, such that it was taken for granted and reinforced by a community who shares similar beliefs.
    • They may have attended institutions that are dedicated to the belief. 
    • Expected to accept propositions uncritically.
    • Corollary: they have dedicated themselves to a lifelong commitment to a set of propositions. 
    • Their consent was engineered (See the Engineering of Consent)
    • The ideology is the result of a set of compliance techniques
    • The ideology can easily be explained by the Asch Conformity Experiments; they most likely yielded to the majority
    • When speaking with the person and others from the group, there is a Spiral of Silence; individually they do not seem to adhere as dogmatically to the ideology, but when placed in a group setting there is a strong tendency to exhibit and regurgitate the dogma (despite everyone, or the majority not necessarily agreeing with the extreme view). Strong indication that the ideology is very dogmatic. 
Part IV

People and Conversation

Diagnosing the mechanism matters because apparently similar dogmatic behavior can arise from very different motives and constraints.

Not Everyone Who Appears Dogmatic Is the Same

I do not think this framework is exhaustive, and I do not think every person who displays one of these patterns belongs to a single category. A person may look dogmatic because they do not care to assess arguments or consider that they might be wrong—but the same outward behavior can emerge from very different situations. We therefore should not assume the worst. Some possibilities include:

  1. Some people swallow the ideology completely and unquestionably
  2. Some people exploit the ideology for personal gain. They don't even believe it, or find it advantageous to express belief in it. 
  3. Some people are just trying to see the good side of the ideology. They might be aware of some of the dark implications of the ideology, but do not want to engage. It is a sort of wishful thinking. They think that some other authority in the ideology will be able to resolve the problems. 
  4. Some people are in a process of beginning to question the ideology. Perhaps they are vulnerable. Perhaps they are confused and want immediate closure. 
  5. Some have serious doubts about the ideology, but still give it a chance for other motivating factors such as fear of social exclusion, fear of loss of identity, or fear of disappointing their loved ones. They have a hard time rejecting it because they know there are plenty of good people who adhere to the ideology, and want to give it a chance. 
  6. Some people have already rejected the ideology but associate with it for other reasons; such as loyalty to loved ones, financial or monetary constraints, and timing.

This distinction matters because diagnosis should guide strategy. A true believer, an opportunist, a conflicted member who fears exclusion, and someone who has privately rejected a belief but remains socially attached to the group do not present the same conversational problem.

How to Engage a Dogmatic Interlocutor

The practical promise of a diagnostic framework is that it should change what we do next. If the problem is evidential, discuss evidence. If the problem is identity threat, more evidence may simply intensify the defense. If the problem is social punishment, public debate can force a person to perform loyalty even when they privately have doubts. The conversational goal should therefore be chosen in light of the mechanism maintaining the belief.

SituationApproachGoal
The person is genuinely uncertain or beginning to questionHelp them state the uncertainty clearly. Ask what they believe, how strongly they believe it, and what would change their mind.Clarification and calibrated belief revision
Identity is tightly fused with the beliefReduce identity threat before arguing particulars. Separate the person’s worth and group membership from the truth of the disputed proposition.Make examination psychologically possible
They cannot accurately state the opposing viewPause the substantive debate and establish reciprocal charitable representation first.Create a shared object of disagreement
You disagree about standards of evidenceDiscuss the acceptance threshold, burden of production, burden of persuasion, and what each side would count as counterevidence.Resolve the epistemic rules before the conclusion

The person is performing loyalty or exploiting the ideologyRecognize that an evidence-centered argument may not address the actual incentive maintaining the behavior.Identify the real conversational constraint
No shared standards of reasoning can be establishedSet narrower goals: identify one disagreement, clarify consequences, or decide whether continued debate is useful.Avoid mistaking endless conflict for inquiry

A useful sequence of questions

  • What exactly is the proposition under dispute?
  • How confident is each person, and why?
  • What evidence would count against the belief?
  • Can each side state the other’s view charitably?
  • Are the standards of evidence themselves disputed?
  • What identity or social costs would revision impose?
  • Is the goal understanding, persuasion, decision, or merely winning?
  • Would a private conversation reduce performative pressure?

Sometimes the best result is not immediate conversion or agreement. It may be a narrower achievement: clarifying an assumption, identifying a circular argument, recognizing an alternative explanation, lowering unjustified confidence, or making it psychologically safer for someone to admit uncertainty. Those are not conversational failures; they are often the preconditions for genuine belief revision.

Conclusion: Skepticism as a Practice

These are thoughts that have been developing over several years of dealing with dogmatic people from across the ideological spectrum. The information environment is confusing, and many of us have lost friends or strained relationships because of rigid adherence to strange or rapidly spreading beliefs. That has been difficult.

Nevertheless, the response should not be another dogma. It should be a practice of critical thinking: making assumptions visible, matching confidence to evidence, preserving the possibility of revision, representing opponents accurately, recognizing social pressures, and knowing when suspension of judgment is more rational than forced certainty. Doubt is not weakness. Properly used, it is one of the mechanisms by which thinking remains corrigible.
