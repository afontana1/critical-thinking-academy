# I. The Puzzle and the Argument

There is something initially compelling about the idea of psychophysical harmony. Pain tends to accompany bodily damage and motivates avoidance. Pleasure tends to accompany conditions and activities that organisms pursue. Our introspective judgments about our own experiences are, at least much of the time, reasonably accurate: when we judge that we are in pain, seeing red, or experiencing an unpleasant sensation, our phenomenal states generally correspond to those judgments. More broadly, the phenomenal character of our mental lives seems remarkably well coordinated with our behavior, cognition, and physical organization.

The argument from psychophysical harmony asks us to regard this coordination as philosophically surprising. Why, after all, should the phenomenal states associated with our physical and functional organization be the ones that make our behavior rationally appropriate and our introspective judgments true? Why should bodily damage be associated with an unpleasant experience that motivates withdrawal rather than, say, intense pleasure, a visual sensation of blue, an auditory buzzing, or no experience at all? Why should the physical state that disposes someone to report that they are experiencing red be associated with a red experience rather than some entirely unrelated phenomenal state?

The intuitive force of the argument comes from imagining that the psychophysical relations might have been radically different. Suppose that the physical and functional facts were held fixed while the phenomenal states associated with them were permuted. An organism physically identical to us might withdraw from bodily damage while experiencing intense pleasure; it might report seeing red while undergoing an experience phenomenally like hearing a high-pitched tone; or its entire behavioral and cognitive organization might remain unchanged while the associated phenomenology consisted of an undifferentiated buzzing sensation. If such alternative mappings are genuinely possible, then the actual relationship between our physical organization and our phenomenal lives can begin to appear unusually fortunate.

Call the fact to be explained \(H\), for psychophysical harmony. Let \(N\) represent naturalism and \(T\) represent theism. The intended evidential argument can then be expressed, in its most basic form, as a comparative likelihood claim:

$$
P(H\mid T) > P(H\mid N).
$$

If psychophysical harmony would be substantially more expected given theism than given naturalism, then harmony provides evidence favoring theism over naturalism. The argument is therefore not merely that psychophysical harmony is interesting, beneficial, or aesthetically striking. For the Bayesian comparison to have force, the harmony must be improbable, or at least significantly less probable, under naturalism than under theism. Strictly speaking, the comparison should be made relative to relevant background information \(B\):

$$
P(H\mid T,B) > P(H\mid N,B).
$$

The choice of \(B\) matters. Facts about embodiment, evolution, rational agency, the existence of stable physical regularities, and the capacities of the organisms under discussion may already belong to the background against which the likelihoods are assessed. For readability, later formulas will often suppress \(B\), but the relevant probabilities should be understood as conditional on an appropriately specified evidential background. It is precisely here that the argument becomes less straightforward than its intuitive presentation suggests.

The expression *psychophysical harmony* already contains a substantial amount of philosophical machinery. Calling the relevant relations “harmonious” does more than merely report that certain phenomenal, behavioral, and physical states are correlated. It suggests that these states are independently identifiable, that their relationship constitutes a further mapping between distinct domains, and that this mapping can be evaluated as appropriate or inappropriate. The argument then asks us to imagine alternative mappings, treats those alternatives as relevant possibilities, characterizes most of them as disharmonious, and moves from their apparent abundance to a claim about the probability of harmony under naturalism. Finally, this allegedly low naturalistic probability is contrasted with the probability of harmony given the intentions of a divine agent. Each stage introduces a different philosophical question.

What exactly is meant by *harmony*? Are the phenomenal and functional states involved independently individuated in the way required by the argument? Does the fact that we can formally describe or verbally imagine a different psychophysical mapping establish that such a mapping represents a coherent metaphysical possibility? Even if there are enormously many conceivable mappings, why should their relative number determine a probability distribution under naturalism? And if the argument ultimately depends upon a comparison with theism, what probability does bare theism actually assign to psychophysical harmony? Answering the latter question appears to require substantive assumptions about divine motives, preferences, and decision-making that are rarely made explicit.

These questions concern different stages of the argument, but they point toward a common methodological problem. The apparent improbability of psychophysical harmony may depend less upon a single empirical observation than upon a sequence of transitions between different kinds of description. A correlation is redescribed as a harmony; the harmony is represented as a mapping; formally or imaginatively specifiable permutations of that mapping are treated as genuine alternatives; the relative abundance of those alternatives is treated as probabilistically significant; and the resulting “surprise” is then compared with what a divine agent would supposedly be likely to create. Schematically, the progression looks something like this:

$$
\text{psychophysical correlation}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{harmony}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{alternative mappings}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{rarity}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{improbability}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{theistic evidence}.
$$

The central contention of this essay is that none of these transitions is automatic. Psychophysical harmony arguments derive much of their apparent force from compressing semantic, modal, probabilistic, and theological assumptions into an intuitive picture of a “striking” correspondence between mind and world. Once these assumptions are separated, the argument faces several distinct burdens. It must establish that psychophysical harmony is an independently meaningful explanandum rather than a theoretically loaded redescription of familiar psychophysical facts. It must justify the move from formally describable or conceivable permutations to genuine metaphysical alternatives. It must provide a principled probability measure over those alternatives rather than infer probability directly from combinatorial abundance. And it must show that theism, without being supplemented by an ad hoc theory of divine psychology, genuinely makes the observed harmony substantially more probable. The rest of the essay is organized around four inferential warrants:

| Warrant | Transition that requires defense | Central question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conceptual** | correlation → harmony | Has an independently specifiable explanandum been identified? |
| **Modal** | harmony → alternative mappings | Do the proposed permutations represent genuine possibilities rather than formal or verbal variations? |
| **Probabilistic** | alternatives → low \(P(H\mid N,B)\) | What principled measure makes harmony improbable under naturalism? |
| **Comparative** | low naturalistic likelihood → theistic evidence | Why should \(P(H\mid T,B)\) be substantially higher, and why specifically theism? |

The argument may ultimately survive some or all of these challenges. The purpose here is not to assume naturalism, deny consciousness, or insist in advance that psychophysical relations require no explanation. The more modest claim is methodological: before psychophysical harmony can serve as evidence for theism, the argument must establish the explanandum, the relevant possibility space, the probability measure over that space, and the comparative likelihood under theism. Simply describing the psychophysical relations as “harmonious,” “fortunate,” or “surprising” does not yet accomplish any of those tasks.

The critique is therefore primarily **undercutting rather than explanatory**. It need not supply a complete naturalistic theory of consciousness or a deeper explanation of every psychophysical regularity. Its immediate question is whether the evidential bridges required by the design inference have actually been established. A residual explanatory mystery for naturalism would be important, but it is not yet the same thing as a likelihood advantage for theism. The first step, therefore, is to reconstruct the argument as clearly and charitably as possible. Only once its premises and inferential links are made explicit can we determine where the alleged surprise actually enters.

## Reconstructing the Psychophysical Harmony Argument

Before evaluating the argument from psychophysical harmony, it is important to state it in a form that gives it as much initial plausibility as possible. The argument should not be dismissed merely because terms such as *harmony*, *mapping*, or *surprise* will later prove philosophically contentious. Nor should the argument be reduced to the crude claim that consciousness itself is unexplained by naturalism. The distinctive claim is comparative: certain relations among phenomenal states, physical or functional states, behavior, and judgment allegedly have a structure that is much more expected if theism is true than if standard naturalism is true.

Cutter and Crummett characterize psychophysical harmony, roughly, as the fact that phenomenal states are correlated with physical states and with one another in strikingly fortunate ways. They emphasize two principal forms of this phenomenon: **normative harmony** and **semantic harmony**. In the first, phenomenal states are associated with physical and functional states whose behavioral consequences are rationalized or justified by those experiences. In the second, phenomenal states are associated with judgments and reports that those very phenomenal states make true. The basic structure of the argument can therefore be reconstructed as follows.

**P1.** There exist phenomenal states and physical or functional states that stand in systematic relations to one another.

**P2.** Some of these relations exhibit normative and semantic appropriateness rather than mere correlation.

**P3.** These psychophysical relations could, at least apparently, have been radically different while much of the relevant physical or functional organization remained fixed.

**P4.** The vast majority of alternative psychophysical mappings we can envisage would not display the same normative and semantic appropriateness.

**P5.** Standard naturalism gives us little antecedent reason to expect the actual harmonious mapping rather than one of the many allegedly disharmonious alternatives.

Therefore,

$$
P(H\mid N)
$$

is comparatively low, where \(H\) represents psychophysical harmony and \(N\) represents naturalism.

**P6.** Theism provides a reason to expect psychophysical harmony because a rational and benevolent creator would have reason to create conscious agents whose experiences are appropriately coordinated with their behavior, practical reasons, and judgments.

Therefore,

$$
P(H\mid T) > P(H\mid N),
$$

where \(T\) represents theism. **C.** Psychophysical harmony confirms theism relative to naturalism. The argument is thus most naturally understood as a likelihood argument. Its conclusion need not be that theism is probably true simpliciter, nor that harmony proves the existence of God. The more modest claim is that harmony should shift our comparative credence toward theism because the observation is allegedly more expected under theism than under naturalism. In Bayesian terms, the relevant comparison is the likelihood ratio

$$
\frac{P(H\mid T)}{P(H\mid N)}.
$$

If this ratio is substantially greater than one, then \(H\) favors \(T\) over \(N\), holding the relevant background information fixed. The ultimate evidential force of the argument therefore depends upon establishing a sufficiently large difference between these two conditional probabilities.

### Normative Harmony

The clearest examples of normative harmony concern pain and pleasure. Consider a damaging stimulus that produces some physical or functional state \(P_1\). That state causes withdrawal from the damaging stimulus, avoidance of similar stimuli in the future, and other behavior directed toward reducing or preventing the relevant condition. At the same time, suppose the psychophysical relation associates \(P_1\) with an experience of pain:

$$
P_1 \longrightarrow Q_{\text{pain}}.
$$

Pain is not merely correlated with the avoidance behavior. On the standard presentation, pain itself has a normative significance: it is unpleasant or bad, and its presence provides the subject with a reason to eliminate, reduce, or avoid it. The behavioral or functional consequences of \(P_1\) therefore happen to coincide with the behavior that the associated phenomenal state rationalizes. Cutter and Crummett present precisely this alignment as a paradigm case of normative harmony. The same general structure can be represented as:

$$
\text{physical state}
\longrightarrow
\text{avoidance behavior}
$$

while simultaneously:

$$
\text{physical state}
\longrightarrow
\text{pain},
$$

and:

$$
\text{pain}
\longrightarrow
\text{reason for avoidance}.
$$

The alleged harmony consists in the convergence between the causal-functional role of the physical state and the normative role of the associated experience. The argument asks us to notice that this coordination appears contingent. The physical state that produces withdrawal might, it is suggested, have been associated with an experience of intense pleasure instead:

$$
P_1 \longrightarrow Q_{\text{pleasure}}.
$$

The organism would still withdraw from the damaging stimulus, but the phenomenal state associated with that behavior would now seem to rationalize the opposite response. The creature might experience something intrinsically desirable while being physically compelled to flee from its source.

Other alternatives are readily described. The same state might have produced an emotionally neutral visual experience, a ringing sensation, an undifferentiated field of gray, or perhaps no conscious experience at all. Cutter and Crummett use such examples to motivate the thought that, among the many apparently available psychophysical arrangements, the actual one occupies a strikingly fortunate position.

The point extends beyond pain and pleasure. Sensory experience may rationalize perceptual beliefs and behavior; cognitive experiences may fit the judgments and actions with which they are associated; and other phenomenal states may occupy roles that appear normatively appropriate relative to the physical systems accompanying them. The claim is therefore not simply that organisms happen to avoid injury. It is that their phenomenal lives are structured so that the experiences accompanying their behavior often provide reasons for that very behavior.

### Semantic Harmony

The second central category is semantic harmony. Here the relevant correspondence is not primarily between an experience and the behavior it rationalizes, but between an experience and a judgment or report whose truth depends upon that experience. Suppose a physical brain state \(P_2\) disposes a subject to judge or report:

> I am experiencing a smooth black region adjacent to a sharply contrasting white region.

The subject also happens to undergo just such an experience. We might represent the relation as:

$$
P_2
\longrightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{report }R,\\
Q_R,
\end{cases}
$$

where \(Q_R\) is the phenomenal state that makes report \(R\) true. The harmony lies in the fact that the physical state responsible for producing the phenomenal judgment is paired with the phenomenal state that makes the judgment accurate. Cutter and Crummett describe semantic harmony in terms of phenomenal states being correlated with reports and judgments that those very states make true. Again, the argument gains its intuitive force by considering alternatives. The physical state producing the judgment might allegedly have been paired instead with some unrelated experience:

$$
P_2 \longrightarrow Q_{\text{buzzing}}.
$$

The physical system would continue producing the report about a structured black-and-white visual field, while the subject's actual phenomenology would consist of an undifferentiated buzzing sensation. The report would therefore systematically misrepresent the phenomenal state.

One can apparently multiply such cases indefinitely. The physical machinery producing pain reports could have been associated with pleasure. The physical machinery producing red-experience reports could have been associated with auditory phenomenology. A system behaviorally and cognitively identical to us might have been associated with radically impoverished, inverted, or otherwise alien phenomenal life. If these alternatives are genuine possibilities, then our actual condition appears special. The physical processes that produce our phenomenal judgments also tend to produce the very phenomenal states that make those judgments correct.

### From Harmony to a Probability Problem

Normative and semantic harmony therefore supply the initial datum. But the argument requires more than the existence of these correlations. It requires interpreting them against a background of alternative possible psychophysical arrangements. Let \(P\) represent the relevant physical or functional states and \(\Phi\) the relevant phenomenal states. The actual psychophysical relationship may then be represented schematically as a mapping:

$$
M:P\rightarrow\Phi.
$$

The argument invites us to compare the actual mapping \(M\) with alternative mappings:

$$
M_1,M_2,M_3,\ldots,M_n.
$$

Some mappings associate avoidance-producing physical states with pain, perceptual-reporting states with the experiences reported, and so forth. Others associate the same physical states with experiences having radically different phenomenal and normative properties. We may therefore define some subset of the possible mappings as harmonious:

$$
\mathcal{H}
=
\{M_i : M_i \text{ exhibits the relevant normative or semantic harmony}\}.
$$

The intuitive claim is that \(\mathcal{H}\) is extremely small relative to the broader space of candidate mappings:

$$
\mathcal{H}\subsetneq\Omega,
$$

where \(\Omega\) represents the relevant space of psychophysical alternatives. If most members of \(\Omega\) are disharmonious, while the actual world contains a member of \(\mathcal{H}\), then the natural question becomes: why did the world instantiate one of the unusual harmonious mappings?

At this stage the argument turns from a descriptive claim into an explanatory and probabilistic one. Naturalistic processes such as biological evolution may explain why organisms possess physical systems that respond adaptively to environmental conditions. Evolution can explain, for example, why organisms possessing systems that withdraw from damaging stimuli tend to survive and reproduce. But if phenomenal states are independently variable while the physical-functional system is held fixed, then ordinary selection appears unable by itself to determine which phenomenal state accompanies that system. A physically identical organism associated with some alternative phenomenology would, by stipulation, behave identically and receive the same evolutionary benefits.

The proponent of psychophysical harmony therefore claims that the familiar evolutionary explanation leaves the psychophysical mapping itself unexplained. The physical structure may have been selected, but why should the accompanying experience be pain rather than pleasure, buzzing, or nothing at all?

Theism is then introduced as a competing hypothesis. A rational creator, unlike an indifferent naturalistic process, could have reasons to prefer creatures whose conscious states appropriately coordinate with their behavior and whose introspective judgments are largely true. Psychophysical harmony would therefore allegedly be less surprising given purposive creation than given standard naturalism.

In its strongest form, then, the argument does not merely ask why consciousness exists. It asks why the relationship between consciousness and physical organization takes this particular allegedly fortunate form. Its evidential structure depends upon four successive claims: that there is a genuine phenomenon deserving the name *psychophysical harmony*; that relevant alternative mappings are genuinely possible; that harmony occupies a comparatively small or otherwise improbable region of that possibility space under naturalism; and that theism assigns harmony a substantially higher probability.

Those four claims provide the structure for the rest of the discussion. Before asking whether harmony is improbable, however, there is a logically prior question: **what exactly has been identified when the ordinary relations among pain, behavior, judgment, and physiology are redescribed as a single phenomenon called “psychophysical harmony”?**

# II. The Conceptual Warrant: From Correlation to Harmony

The first difficulty arises before any questions about probability, naturalism, or theism enter the picture. The argument begins by describing certain psychophysical relations as instances of *harmony*. But what exactly does that description add to the underlying facts? Consider two ways of describing an ordinary case of pain. The first is comparatively thin:

> Bodily damage is associated with pain, withdrawal, aversion, and subsequent avoidance.

The second recasts the same situation in the language of psychophysical harmony:

> An intrinsically unpleasant phenomenal state is harmoniously mapped onto a physical-functional state that produces behavior rationalized by that phenomenal state.

The second description sounds philosophically more remarkable than the first. Yet it is not merely a stylistic reformulation. It introduces a particular picture of the relation between the physical and phenomenal. On that picture, we begin with two independently identifiable domains: a physical or functional state on one side and a phenomenal state on the other. These are then connected by a further relation—a psychophysical mapping—which can itself be evaluated as fortunate or unfortunate, harmonious or disharmonious. Schematically, the thin description gives us something like:

$$
\text{bodily damage}
\longrightarrow
\text{pain, aversion, withdrawal}.
$$

The harmony description encourages us instead to picture:

$$
\boxed{\text{physical-functional state}}
\overset{M}{\longrightarrow}
\boxed{\text{phenomenal state}},
$$

where the phenomenal state independently possesses certain normative features, the physical state independently possesses certain behavioral consequences, and the fact that these two features line up constitutes an additional striking correspondence. This decomposition is essential to the argument. If pain and its characteristic place in behavior, judgment, practical reasoning, and human life were not sufficiently independent, then it would become much less obvious what the allegedly surprising “match” consists in. A correspondence can be surprising only if the things being compared have first been specified independently enough that their agreement is not guaranteed by the way they were characterized.

Suppose, for example, that I describe two objects independently and later discover that they possess exactly the same complicated sequence of properties. That may constitute an interesting coincidence. But if I first define one object partly by reference to the other, subsequently “discovering” that the two correspond would be considerably less impressive. The question, then, is whether the phenomenal and functional relata in the harmony argument really possess the degree of independent identity that the argument requires.

The weakest form of the objection does not yet depend on Wittgenstein. It is simply an **individuation burden**: the argument must explain how the relevant phenomenal states retain determinate identities while their characteristic relations to judgment, motivation, behavior, and use are radically permuted. A Wittgensteinian challenge then supplies one influential reason to think that this burden is not philosophically trivial.

The later Wittgenstein famously resisted a philosophical picture according to which sensation words acquire their meaning by privately attaching names to intrinsically identifiable inner objects. The private-language discussion is complex and its interpretation remains contested, but one central target is the idea that sensation concepts can be grounded through an entirely private act of identifying and re-identifying an inner item independently of publicly intelligible standards of correct use. The ordinary concept of pain does not function as though the word *pain* were first attached to a metaphysically self-identifying private object and only later discovered to bear contingent relations to crying out, injury, aversion, treatment, consolation, avoidance, and the rest of the practices within which pain-talk has its place.

This point should not be confused with behaviorism. A Wittgensteinian need not claim that pain *is identical to* pain behavior, nor that someone cannot be in pain without displaying its usual manifestations. The point concerns the criteria through which the concept has its use and identity. Sensation language operates within practices in which expressions, circumstances, reactions, training, judgments, and standards of application are interconnected. Wittgenstein's challenge is directed toward the philosophical temptation to strip all of this away while assuming that the sensation concept continues to pick out exactly the same intrinsically individuated object underneath.

That temptation is highly relevant to psychophysical harmony because the argument depends upon precisely this kind of counterfactual separation. We are invited to hold fixed a phenomenal state—*this very pain*—while radically altering its relation to behavior, judgment, motivation, and physical organization. We are asked to imagine, for instance,

$$
P_{\text{withdrawal}}
\longrightarrow
Q_{\text{pleasure}}
$$

instead of

$$
P_{\text{withdrawal}}
\longrightarrow
Q_{\text{pain}}.
$$

But what licenses the claim that the phenomenal item appearing in these counterfactual descriptions retains the identity assigned to it? What makes the state in the imagined world *pleasure* once its role in the subject's life has been radically altered? Conversely, what makes something *pain* after we have detached it from the characteristic network of circumstances, reactions, evaluations, aversions, expressions, and uses through which the concept ordinarily operates?

The harmony theorist may answer that phenomenal properties possess intrinsic qualitative essences entirely independently of these relations. Pain, on this view, has a determinate phenomenal nature that can be identified independently of everything the subject does, says, believes, desires, or is disposed to do. It is therefore perfectly intelligible to hold the phenomenal essence fixed while permuting all of its psychophysical associations.

But this answer is not philosophically neutral. It is a substantive theory of phenomenal individuation. Indeed, it is close to the very conception of sensation that the Wittgensteinian challenge puts under pressure. The point is not that such a theory has been decisively refuted simply by invoking Wittgenstein. The point is dialectical: **the harmony argument cannot treat that theory as part of the neutral data from which the argument begins.**

This matters because Cutter and Crummett describe psychophysical harmony as a fact about phenomenal states being correlated with physical states, behavior, and judgments in “strikingly fortunate ways.” Normative harmony concerns behavior rationalized by the associated phenomenal state; semantic harmony concerns judgments and reports made true by the associated phenomenal state. But to interpret these relations as a remarkable convergence among independently constituted features, the argument needs those features to be identified independently enough for their convergence to count as contingent.

Consider normative harmony. We are told that pain is bad or unpleasant and therefore gives us reason to avoid its source. Separately, the physical state associated with pain produces avoidance behavior. The two sides allegedly coincide:

$$
\underbrace{Q_{\text{pain}}\rightarrow
\text{reason to avoid}}_{\text{phenomenal/normative side}}
$$

and

$$
\underbrace{P_{\text{pain}}\rightarrow
\text{avoidance behavior}}_{\text{physical/functional side}}.
$$

The resulting match is then declared harmonious. Yet there is a prior conceptual question: to what extent can the unpleasantness, aversiveness, reason-giving character, and identity of pain be cleanly separated from one another? If an alleged phenomenal state had no connection whatsoever to aversion, distress, relief, concern, avoidance, complaint, or any of the characteristic contexts in which the concept of pain operates, it is not obvious that we could simply stipulate that this is nevertheless “the same pain” and then marvel at how differently it functions.

Again, this need not entail that every connection is conceptually necessary or that no inversion scenario can be made intelligible. The weaker point is sufficient: **the harmony theorist bears an individuation burden that is often obscured by the ease with which labels can be permuted in philosophical examples.**

The same issue becomes even sharper in the case of semantic harmony. Suppose a subject is physically disposed to report, “I am experiencing red,” and the phenomenal state associated with that physical condition is indeed a red experience. The harmony theorist contrasts this with a world in which the same subject makes the same report while experiencing something entirely different.

But here several things must remain fixed simultaneously: the physical state, the linguistic disposition, the meaning of the words, the truth conditions of the judgment, and the identity of the phenomenal state. Only the final phenomenal occupant is supposed to vary. A Wittgensteinian can reasonably ask whether the semantics of sensation discourse can really be held fixed in this way while the larger practice connecting phenomenal concepts, judgments, expressions, circumstances, and criteria of application is globally altered. The private-language considerations are relevant precisely because they challenge the idea that sensation terms acquire their meanings through a private relation to independently self-identifying inner objects.

This generates a more general problem for the notion of harmony. Before the argument can tell us that two things match in a striking way, it must establish that those things have not been individuated partly through the very relations subsequently presented as surprising. The issue can be represented schematically. The harmony argument requires something like:

$$
A
\quad\text{independently identified},
$$

$$
B
\quad\text{independently identified},
$$

followed by the discovery that:

$$
R(A,B).
$$

Only then does it become natural to ask why \(A\) and \(B\) happen to stand in relation \(R\). But if the identity or application of \(A\) is partly constituted by its place in a network involving \(B\), then the appearance of independent correspondence weakens:

$$
A(B)
\longrightarrow
R(A,B).
$$

The “discovery” of harmony may then depend partly upon first abstracting the relata away from the practices that give them their intelligible roles and subsequently reintroducing those relations as an external correspondence requiring explanation. This is the first major challenge to psychophysical harmony: **does “harmony” identify an additional metaphysical fact, or does it redescribe ordinary psychophysical relations from within a philosophical framework that has already separated the relata in the way needed to make their reunion look surprising?**

The argument cannot answer this merely by pointing again to the correlations. Those correlations are not in dispute. We can grant that injury, pain, aversion, reports, judgments, and behavior exhibit systematic relations. What remains to be shown is that, once all of those facts have been specified, there is a further independently intelligible fact consisting in the *harmony of their mapping*.

This distinction will matter throughout the rest of the argument. If harmony is not a theoretically neutral datum, then the later probability calculation cannot simply treat it as an observation waiting to be explained. And even if we grant, for the sake of argument, that phenomenal and physical states can be independently individuated, another question immediately arises: does representing their relation mathematically as a mapping license us to treat the space of alternative formal mappings as a space of genuine metaphysical possibilities?

# III. The Modal Warrant: From Harmony to Alternative Worlds

## Formal Representation and Metaphysical Structure

Even if we grant, for the sake of argument, that phenomenal and physical states can be independently individuated, a second problem remains. The psychophysical harmony argument typically represents their relation as though there were a mapping from one independently specified domain to another:

$$
M:P\rightarrow\Phi,
$$

where \(P\) is the set of relevant physical or functional states and \(\Phi\) is the set of phenomenal states. There is nothing objectionable about this as a piece of formal representation. Mathematics routinely represents systematic relations by functions, mappings, structures, graphs, and other abstract objects. The difficulty begins when the formal representation is treated as though its structure automatically reveals the metaphysical structure of reality itself. A function

$$
f:A\rightarrow B
$$

is, in the first instance, a mathematical object defined relative to specified domains and codomains. Once the sets \(A\) and \(B\) are fixed, one may define any number of functions between them. But the existence of those formally specifiable functions does not by itself establish that reality contains an independently existing function-like entity corresponding to each one, nor that every mathematically definable permutation represents a genuine alternative way reality might have been.

This distinction is especially important because the psychophysical harmony argument appears to depend upon more than the fact that the actual correlations can be represented by some function \(M\). Its probabilistic force comes from comparing \(M\) with a much larger collection of alternative mappings:

$$
M_1,M_2,M_3,\ldots,M_n.
$$

The actual mapping is then characterized as unusually harmonious relative to this broader space. But before this comparison can carry metaphysical or probabilistic significance, we need to ask what exactly these mappings are supposed to represent. At least four importantly different notions can be distinguished:

1. a formal expression or function;
2. an interpreted mathematical model;
3. an empirical regularity described by that model;
4. a metaphysically governing law.

These should not be treated as interchangeable. Consider a familiar physical equation such as

$$
F=ma.
$$

Taken merely as a syntactic expression, the equation does not yet describe the physical world. Its terms must be interpreted. We must specify what \(F\), \(m\), and \(a\) denote, which quantities and systems fall within the intended domain, what background assumptions are operative, and how the formal structure is connected to measurement and application. The formal expression acquires empirical significance only within an interpreted framework. The same point applies to psychophysical mappings. Suppose we write

$$
M(P_1)=Q_{\text{pain}}.
$$

This may represent the fact that some physical or functional state \(P_1\) is systematically associated with a painful experience. But the notation itself does not settle whether \(M\) is:

* merely a convenient representation of the observed regularity;
* a lawlike generalization abstracted from those regularities;
* a primitive psychophysical law;
* a metaphysically governing relation between distinct kinds of property;
* or something else entirely.

Yet the harmony argument often gains intuitive force by moving quickly from the first kind of representation toward the strongest metaphysical interpretation. The risk is a form of reification. We begin with the harmless claim:

> Psychophysical correlations can be represented by a mapping.

This becomes:

> There is a psychophysical mapping connecting physical states to phenomenal states.

And then:

> Reality might have contained any of the formally specifiable alternative mappings instead.

But these are progressively stronger claims. The transition can be represented as follows:

$$
\text{formal representation of correlations}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{metaphysically real mapping}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{space of alternative metaphysical mappings}.
$$

Neither arrow is guaranteed merely by the usefulness of the formalism. This is where a broadly model-theoretic perspective is helpful. In formal semantics, a language does not carry its empirical interpretation merely by virtue of its syntax. A structure supplies a domain and interpretations for the nonlogical vocabulary. Truth and satisfaction are then evaluated relative to that interpreted structure. The same formal expressions may admit different interpretations over different structures, and the same subject matter can often be represented through formally different but equivalent systems. The general lesson is modest but important: **formal structure and worldly structure should not be identified without argument**. In Tarskian terms, we may have a language \(L\) and a structure \(\mathcal{M}\) such that

$$
\mathcal{M}\models\varphi.
$$

The statement that \(\varphi\) is satisfied in \(\mathcal{M}\) presupposes an interpretation of the relevant vocabulary. We do not ordinarily infer from the existence of alternative interpretations or models that each represents an independently available metaphysical configuration of reality. Formal possibility is much cheaper than metaphysical possibility.

This point becomes clearer if we consider the sheer number of formally definable psychophysical mappings. Suppose there are \(n\) relevant physical states and \(m\) phenomenal states. The number of possible functions from the first set to the second is

$$
m^n.
$$

If \(n\) and \(m\) are even moderately large, the number of formally possible mappings becomes enormous. It is then tempting to reason that, because only a small proportion of these functions appear harmonious, the actual mapping is correspondingly special. But what exactly has been established by the combinatorics? At most, we have shown:

$$
\text{There exist }m^n\text{ formally specifiable functions from }P\text{ to }\Phi.
$$

It does not follow that:

$$
\text{There are }m^n\text{ metaphysically possible psychophysical laws}.
$$

Still less does it follow that:

$$
\text{Naturalism assigns comparable probability to each of them}.
$$

The first inference is modal; the second is probabilistic. Neither is supplied by the mathematics itself. This distinction can be expressed compactly:

$$
\boxed{\text{formal definability}}
\not\Rightarrow
\boxed{\text{metaphysical possibility}}.
$$

The importance of this point is easy to overlook because the notation makes the permutation operation appear trivial. If the actual mapping is

$$
M(P_1)=Q_1
$$

and

$$
M(P_2)=Q_2,
$$

then we can immediately define another function:

$$
M'(P_1)=Q_2
$$

and

$$
M'(P_2)=Q_1.
$$

As mathematics, this is unproblematic. But the ability to define \(M'\) does not establish that \(M'\) corresponds to a coherent alternative world. One may permute labels and functions within a representation much more freely than one may permute the properties or relations that the representation is intended to describe. The psychophysical harmony argument therefore risks confusing two different kinds of freedom:

$$
\text{freedom to manipulate a formal representation}
$$

with

$$
\text{freedom of reality to instantiate the represented permutations}.
$$

These are not the same. The problem becomes sharper if the relata themselves are theory-dependent. The sets \(P\) and \(\Phi\) do not arrive already carved into uniquely determined elements. We must decide what counts as a physical state type, what counts as a phenomenal state type, and at what level of abstraction each should be individuated. Are the relevant physical states microphysical configurations, neural states, functional roles, computational states, or organism-level dispositions? Are phenomenal states individuated by qualitative character, valence, representational content, conceptual role, or some combination of these? Different choices generate different formal spaces. If we define:

$$
P=\{P_1,P_2,\ldots,P_n\}
$$

and

$$
\Phi=\{Q_1,Q_2,\ldots,Q_m\},
$$

then the resulting function space

$$
\Phi^P
$$

depends upon how \(P\) and \(\Phi\) were constructed in the first place. A different partition of the same underlying phenomena may generate a radically different set of candidate mappings. This gives rise to what may be called the **reification of the product space**. From the fact that we can mathematically construct

$$
P\times\Phi
$$

or the function space

$$
\Phi^P,
$$

the harmony argument is tempted to treat that structure as the privileged metaphysical space of psychophysical possibilities. But the construction of a product space is a formal operation. It does not establish that the resulting combinations correspond one-to-one with genuine ways reality might have been. The issue can be put as a critical question:

> Why should the formally generated function space between the chosen physical and phenomenal state types be identified with the metaphysically relevant possibility space over psychophysical laws?

Unless this question has an independent answer, the enormous number of formally available “disharmonious” mappings may tell us more about the expressive resources of the formalism than about the modal structure of reality. This also clarifies a possible misunderstanding. The objection is not that mathematics fails to describe reality, nor that mathematical representations are somehow arbitrary in the ordinary sense. Scientific models can be extraordinarily successful, informative, and constrained by empirical evidence. The point is instead that a successful representation does not automatically carry with it an ontology of every transformation definable within the representation.

A coordinate system can represent a physical system without every coordinate transformation corresponding to a physically distinct possibility. A formal theory can possess many models without every model representing a metaphysically possible world. A function space can contain innumerable mappings without each mapping corresponding to an independently possible law of nature. The same caution should apply here.

If psychophysical harmony is to be derived from the alleged rarity of the actual mapping among alternatives, the proponent must first establish that the alternatives are not merely formal artifacts. The mapping representation may be useful for displaying the structure of the argument, but it cannot itself determine the modal space against which the actual psychophysical relations are judged. This leaves the harmony argument with a further burden. Even granting independently identifiable physical and phenomenal states, and even granting that their actual relations can be represented as a mapping,

$$
M:P\rightarrow\Phi,
$$

the proponent still must justify the transition from:

$$
\text{I can write down another mapping }M'
$$

to:

$$
\text{Reality genuinely could have instantiated }M'.
$$

That transition is not mathematical. It is modal. And it is here that the language of *imagination*, *conceivability*, and *possible mappings* begins to do some of the most important—and potentially most misleading—work in the argument.

## Conceivability, Possibility, and Probability-Relevant Alternatives

A recurring source of intuitive force in psychophysical harmony arguments is the claim that we can *imagine* radically different psychophysical mappings. We can imagine bodily damage being associated with pleasure rather than pain, visual processing being associated with auditory phenomenology, or physical states that produce reports of red being accompanied by some entirely unrelated experience. These cases are then treated as evidence that the actual psychophysical arrangement is only one member of a much larger field of alternatives.

But the phrase *we can imagine* is far less precise than the argument requires. It can refer to several importantly different epistemic and modal achievements. In ordinary philosophical discussion, these are often allowed to blur together. Yet the argument needs a much stronger conclusion than the mere fact that we can manipulate symbols or tell a coherent-sounding story. At least six different notions should be distinguished:

$$
\text{syntactic formulability}
$$

$$
\Downarrow?
$$

$$
\text{semantic intelligibility}
$$

$$
\Downarrow?
$$

$$
\text{conceivability}
$$

$$
\Downarrow?
$$

$$
\text{epistemic possibility}
$$

$$
\Downarrow?
$$

$$
\text{metaphysical possibility}
$$

$$
\Downarrow?
$$

$$
\text{probability-relevant possibility under naturalism}.
$$

Each transition requires justification. The weakest notion is merely syntactic. We can write:

$$
M(P_{\text{injury}})=Q_{\text{pain}}
$$

and then replace the right-hand side:

$$
M'(P_{\text{injury}})=Q_{\text{pleasure}}.
$$

This demonstrates almost nothing about the modal structure of reality. It shows only that our notation permits substitution. Formal languages are deliberately flexible in this respect. If \(Q_{\text{pain}}\) and \(Q_{\text{pleasure}}\) are treated as two symbols denoting two elements of a codomain, then constructing \(M'\) is trivial.

But the ability to perform a substitution does not yet show that the resulting expression has a determinate interpretation. A string of symbols may be grammatically or formally well-formed while failing to describe a coherent state of affairs. The next question is therefore semantic:

> What exactly would it mean for the same physical-functional state to be associated with “pleasure” while all relevant behavior, judgment, motivation, and practical role remained fixed?

This is not a demand that every conceivable scenario be reducible to ordinary behavior. It is instead a demand for clarity about what is being held constant and what is being varied. Suppose we are told to imagine an organism physically and functionally identical to us. It withdraws from bodily damage, avoids future instances of the damaging stimulus, cries out, judges the state undesirable, reports being in pain, remembers the episode as aversive, seeks analgesia, and behaves in every other relevant respect exactly as we do. We are then instructed to imagine that its phenomenal state is nevertheless not pain but intense pleasure.

At this point the description may appear intelligible because we are treating *pain* and *pleasure* as labels for independently identifiable phenomenal objects. But that was precisely one of the assumptions placed under pressure in the previous section. If the identity of these states is not fully separable from their characteristic roles in judgment, aversion, motivation, language, and practice, then the instruction to “hold everything fixed and swap the qualia” may not describe a transparent possibility. It may amount to little more than relabeling one component of a philosophical model. The distinction is important because formal manipulation can create an illusion of modal freedom. Once psychophysical relations are represented as a function

$$
M:P\rightarrow\Phi,
$$

it becomes visually obvious that the values of the function can be permuted. But what is obvious of the representation is not automatically obvious of the world. The following inference is therefore invalid without further argument:

$$
\text{I can symbolically permute }Q_1\text{ and }Q_2
$$

$$
\therefore
$$

$$
\text{there is a coherent world in which }Q_1\text{ and }Q_2
\text{ exchange psychophysical roles}.
$$

The ease of constructing the notation can conceal the philosophical work required to establish that the permutation preserves the identity of the relata while altering only their relation. Even if semantic intelligibility is granted, however, a further distinction remains between conceivability and possibility. Philosophers have long debated whether conceivability is a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility. There are familiar cases in which something may initially seem conceivable only because the concepts involved are incompletely understood. A person might once have regarded it as conceivable that water is not \(H_2O\), gold has a different atomic number, or a mathematical claim is false, only to discover that the apparent possibility resulted from ignorance about the relevant identities or necessities. Psychophysical cases may or may not behave similarly. The point here is not to settle that larger debate. It is simply that the harmony argument cannot move from

> I seem able to imagine a different psychophysical mapping

directly to

> that mapping represents a genuine metaphysical possibility

without defending some bridge between conceivability and metaphysical possibility. Let the set of apparently conceivable mappings be

$$
\Omega_C.
$$

Let the set of genuinely metaphysically possible mappings be

$$
\Omega_M.
$$

The harmony argument needs something close to:

$$
\Omega_C\approx\Omega_M,
$$

or at minimum it needs reason to believe that the relevant disharmonious members of \(\Omega_C\) also belong to \(\Omega_M\). But that relation cannot simply be assumed. Indeed, the problem becomes particularly sharp if one adopts any view on which phenomenal and physical properties are connected necessarily rather than contingently. A physicalist, functionalist, dispositionalist, or identity theorist may deny that the same total physical state could remain fixed while its phenomenal character varies arbitrarily. A harmony theorist may regard those positions as mistaken, but then the alleged possibility of permutation cannot be used as a neutral datum against them. It depends upon rejecting them first. This generates an important dialectical constraint:

> A possibility that exists only under a controversial metaphysics of mind cannot be treated as neutral evidence in an argument intended to adjudicate among metaphysical theories.

If psychophysical inversion requires a robust form of phenomenal independence, then the argument must defend that independence rather than merely invite the reader to imagine it. There is also an important distinction between metaphysical and epistemic possibility. A proponent may retreat from the claim that alternative mappings are genuinely metaphysically possible and instead argue that they are *epistemically* possible: given what we know, we cannot rule them out. Let

$$
\Omega_E
$$

represent the set of psychophysical arrangements compatible with our current epistemic position. Perhaps the argument then proceeds:

$$
M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_n\in\Omega_E,
$$

and many of these are disharmonious. But this weaker claim does not automatically recover the probabilistic argument. A hypothesis may be epistemically possible in the minimal sense that our evidence does not rule it out while nevertheless receiving extremely low rational probability. Conversely, multiple hypotheses may all remain epistemically possible while rational credence is distributed very unevenly among them. Thus:

$$
M_i\in\Omega_E
$$

does not imply:

$$
P(M_i\mid N)>0
$$

in any substantial sense, much less:

$$
P(M_i\mid N)\approx P(M_j\mid N).
$$

This point is crucial. The harmony argument does not ultimately need only a modal claim. It needs a probabilistic one. The fact that we cannot rule out an alternative mapping does not tell us how much probability naturalism should assign to that mapping. An open possibility is not automatically a live competitor of equal or comparable weight. The hierarchy therefore extends beyond metaphysical possibility:

$$
\boxed{\text{conceivable}}
\not\Rightarrow
\boxed{\text{metaphysically possible}}
\not\Rightarrow
\boxed{\text{probable enough to matter}}.
$$

This is where the phrase *possible mapping* becomes especially dangerous. In informal discussion, it can shift between several meanings without notice. At one moment it may mean a mapping that can be written down. At another, a mapping that can be coherently described. At another, a world that genuinely could have existed. And at yet another, a possibility over which naturalistic probability ought to be distributed. Those are not equivalent claims. The distinction can be made more explicit by separating several spaces:

$$
\Omega_F
=
\{\text{formally specifiable mappings}\},
$$

$$
\Omega_C
=
\{\text{conceivable mappings}\},
$$

$$
\Omega_E
=
\{\text{epistemically open mappings}\},
$$

$$
\Omega_M
=
\{\text{metaphysically possible mappings}\},
$$

and

$$
\Omega_N
=
\{M : P(M\mid N)>0\}.
$$

The harmony argument often proceeds as though these spaces substantially coincide:

$$
\Omega_F
=
\Omega_C
=
\Omega_E
=
\Omega_M
=
\Omega_N.
$$

But that identity is precisely what requires defense. A formal system can define mappings that fail to correspond to coherent states of affairs. A conceivable description may reflect conceptual confusion. An epistemically open hypothesis may nevertheless have negligible rational credence. A metaphysically possible world may be ruled out by a specific version of naturalism. And a naturalistic theory may impose structural constraints that make some formally definable mappings impossible or highly disfavored. Thus, the mere multiplication of imaginable alternatives does not yet generate the space needed for the probabilistic argument. This also exposes a subtle shift in the rhetoric of the argument. The phrase

> “We can imagine many other mappings”

sounds modest. It merely invites intellectual flexibility. But the later conclusion requires something much stronger:

> “Naturalism must distribute substantial probability across a very large range of these mappings, most of which are disharmonious.”

The first claim concerns our imaginative capacities. The second concerns the probability distribution induced by a metaphysical theory. There is a large inferential gap between them. To see this more clearly, suppose I can describe a thousand different assignments:

$$
M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_{1000}.
$$

Perhaps only five of them strike me as harmonious. From this I may infer the purely combinatorial fact:

$$
\frac{5}{1000}=0.005.
$$

But I have not yet established:

$$
P(H\mid N)=0.005.
$$

The fact that I can conceptualize or symbolically generate one thousand alternatives does not show that naturalism treats them as equally open, equally natural, equally simple, equally lawful, or equally probable. This reveals why the modal issue cannot be cleanly separated from the probabilistic one. The argument needs not merely *possibilities*, but a principled account of which possibilities belong in the relevant comparison class and how much weight each should receive. The modal premise therefore carries a much heavier burden than its ordinary-language presentation suggests. To say that “we can imagine different mappings” must ultimately be unpacked into something like the following: **First**, the alternative description is semantically coherent. **Second**, the relevant phenomenal and physical states retain stable identities across the comparison. **Third**, the alternative represents a genuine metaphysical possibility rather than merely a formal permutation. **Fourth**, that possibility is compatible with the naturalistic hypothesis under consideration.

**Fifth**, naturalism assigns it enough probability for its inclusion to affect the likelihood of harmony. Only after these steps have been established can the abundance of alternative mappings begin to bear on

$$
P(H\mid N).
$$

Without them, the argument risks deriving modal and probabilistic conclusions from the expressive flexibility of our language and mathematics. This is not a minor technical objection. Much of the intuitive force of psychophysical harmony comes precisely from the ease with which bizarre alternatives can be described. Pain might have felt pleasant. Injury might have produced a sensation of blue. Visual experience might have been associated with auditory reports. Consciousness might have consisted of undifferentiated buzzing.

But the philosophical question is not whether sentences expressing these permutations can be produced. It is whether those sentences successfully identify a well-defined class of relevant alternatives and, ultimately, whether naturalism assigns those alternatives sufficient probability to render the actual world surprising. The transition from imagination to probability therefore cannot be accomplished merely by accumulating increasingly strange examples. The more important question is what licenses the transition from

$$
\text{“I can describe }M'\text{”}
$$

to

$$
\text{“}M'\text{ belongs to the probability space relevant to }N\text{.”}
$$

That question leads directly to the central probabilistic weakness in the argument. Even if we grant that a large number of alternative psychophysical mappings are genuinely possible, nothing yet follows about how probability should be distributed across them. The next step in the harmony argument—from the abundance of disharmonious possibilities to the improbability of harmony—is therefore not a matter of modality at all. It is a problem of measure.

# IV. The Probabilistic Warrant: From Possibility to Improbability

## A Possibility Space Is Not a Probability Measure

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the previous objections are set aside. Grant that phenomenal and physical states can be independently individuated. Grant that radically different psychophysical mappings are semantically coherent. Grant further that these alternatives represent genuine metaphysical possibilities rather than merely formal permutations. Even after granting all of this, the central probabilistic inference of the psychophysical harmony argument has not yet been established. The reason is simple but important: **possibility is not probability**.

The argument derives much of its intuitive force from the thought that there are enormously many ways phenomenal states might have been associated with physical or functional states, while comparatively few of these mappings would count as harmonious. The actual mapping therefore seems exceptional. From there it is tempting to conclude that harmony must have been improbable under naturalism. But this transition moves between two mathematically and philosophically distinct claims. Let \(\Omega\) be the relevant space of possible psychophysical mappings, and let \(\mathcal{H}\subseteq\Omega\) represent those mappings classified as harmonious. One might discover that, under some chosen representation,

$$
|\mathcal{H}| \ll |\Omega|.
$$

If \(\Omega\) is finite, one might even calculate a ratio:

$$
\frac{|\mathcal{H}|}{|\Omega|}\ll1.
$$

But what the argument ultimately needs is not a statement about cardinality. It needs:

$$
P(H\mid N)\ll1.
$$

These are not equivalent. The ratio

$$
\frac{|\mathcal{H}|}{|\Omega|}
$$

becomes a probability only under additional assumptions about how probability is distributed over \(\Omega\). In the simplest finite case, one would need something like a uniform probability assignment:

$$
P(M_i\mid N)
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
$$

for every \(M_i\in\Omega\). Only then would it follow that

$$
P(H\mid N)
=
\frac{|\mathcal{H}|}{|\Omega|}.
$$

Yet naturalism does not, merely by being naturalism, imply such a distribution. It does not tell us to place every conceivable psychophysical mapping into an urn and sample one uniformly at random. This is the first major probabilistic gap in the argument. The harmony theorist therefore requires not merely a possibility space but a **probability measure** over that space. Formally, what is needed is something like:

$$
\mu_N:\Sigma\rightarrow[0,1],
$$

where \(\Sigma\) is an appropriate collection of subsets of \(\Omega\) and \(\mu_N\) represents the probability measure induced by naturalism. Only then can we meaningfully write:

$$
P(H\mid N)=\mu_N(\mathcal{H}).
$$

The obvious question is:

> What determines \(\mu_N\)?

Listing possibilities does not answer this question. Neither does noting that naturalism appears to provide no obvious reason to privilege one mapping over another. The latter move risks appealing implicitly to the **principle of indifference**: where our evidence does not favor one possibility over another, assign equal probability to each. This principle has an intuitive appeal in simple finite cases. If a prize is known to be behind exactly one of three otherwise indistinguishable doors, assigning probability \(1/3\) to each door seems natural. But the philosophical literature on probability has long emphasized that the principle becomes problematic when the relevant partition or parameterization is not independently fixed. Different ways of carving up the same state space can generate incompatible “uniform” probability assignments. This matters directly for psychophysical harmony. Suppose there are one thousand candidate mappings:

$$
M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_{1000},
$$

and only five satisfy our chosen criterion of harmony. If each mapping receives equal probability, then:

$$
P(H\mid N)
=
\frac{5}{1000}
=
0.005.
$$

But the equal weighting is doing all the probabilistic work. The mere existence of one thousand descriptions does not establish it. One could partition the alternatives differently. Several mappings might be grouped together as instances of one more general law. Other mappings might be distinguished into many fine-grained subcases. Some may possess simpler descriptions than others. Some may arise naturally from a particular underlying theory, while others require highly complicated or gerrymandered specifications. Changing the partition can therefore change the apparent frequency of harmony.

This is not an exotic technicality. It is a general problem for attempts to derive probabilities from ignorance. The principle of indifference can yield different probability assignments when the same problem is parameterized in different ways. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy illustrates this using Bertrand-style cases in which uniformity over one parameter produces one probability while uniformity over an equally legitimate alternative parameter produces another. The broader lesson is that ignorance does not by itself tell us which parameterization should be treated as fundamental. A simple mathematical example illustrates the point. Suppose a quantity \(x\) lies somewhere between \(0\) and \(1\). If we assume a uniform distribution over \(x\), then:

$$
P(x<1/2)=\frac{1}{2}.
$$

Now define:

$$
y=x^2.
$$

A distribution uniform in \(x\) is not uniform in \(y\). Conversely, if we insist that \(y\) rather than \(x\) should be distributed uniformly, we obtain a different distribution over the same underlying possibilities. Thus,

$$
\text{uniform in one representation}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{uniform in another}.
$$

The relevant question for psychophysical harmony is therefore:

> What is the privileged parameterization of psychophysical possibility?

If physical and phenomenal states can be individuated at different levels of grain, represented using different predicates, or grouped into different law-types, then there may be no uniquely privileged way to count “the mappings.” This connects directly with the formal concerns of the previous section. Suppose the same psychophysical regularity can be described under two formally equivalent systems. In one representation, the harmonious region may occupy a very small fraction of the formally generated mappings. In another, a different choice of variables or state-types may make that same region comparatively simple or large. If the alleged improbability changes with the representation, then the improbability has not yet been shown to be a representation-independent feature of reality. This yields an important critical question:

> **Is the claim that psychophysical harmony is rare invariant under equivalent redescriptions of the possibility space?**

If not, the argument risks confusing a feature of its formal representation with a feature of naturalistic probability. The problem becomes even more severe when the relevant space is infinite. Psychophysical state spaces are not obviously finite, and once infinitely many alternatives are admitted, the intuitive idea of “divide the favorable possibilities by all possibilities” can break down entirely. There is generally no uniform probability distribution over a countably infinite set that assigns every member the same positive probability. If every member receives probability \(p>0\), the total probability diverges; if every member receives probability \(0\), their countable sum is \(0\) rather than \(1\). Thus, for an infinite space, statements such as

> “Only a tiny proportion of all possible mappings are harmonious”

may fail to determine any probability at all. A measure has to be specified independently. The issue can be summarized as follows:

$$
\boxed{\text{many alternatives}}
\not\Rightarrow
\boxed{\text{equal weighting}}
\not\Rightarrow
\boxed{\text{low probability}}.
$$

These are three separate steps. The harmony argument can also obscure this distinction through the language of *surprise*. To say that psychophysical harmony is “surprising” can mean several different things. First, it may refer to **psychological surprise**:

$$
S_{\text{psych}}(H),
$$

meaning merely that an observer did not expect harmony. But an individual's expectations may be uninformed, confused, or theory-dependent. Psychological surprise has little direct evidential significance. Second, “surprise” may refer to **combinatorial rarity**:

$$
\frac{|\mathcal{H}|}{|\Omega|}\ll1.
$$

This says that relatively few formally represented possibilities possess a given property. But as already noted, rarity under a chosen counting scheme is not yet Bayesian probability. Third, it may refer to **Bayesian surprise**, in the sense that:

$$
P(H\mid N,B)\ll1,
$$

where \(B\) represents relevant background information. This is the sense required by the evidential argument. If harmony is to confirm theism relative to naturalism, the relevant question is not whether we find harmony psychologically impressive, nor whether harmony occupies a small region under some arbitrary formal partition. The question is whether a rational agent should assign harmony a low conditional probability given naturalism and the appropriate background information. Fourth, “surprise” may evoke the technical information-theoretic notion of **surprisal**:

$$
I(H)=-\log P(H).
$$

An event with lower probability carries greater information-theoretic surprisal. But this notion cannot solve the problem facing the harmony argument because the probability distribution must already be specified before surprisal can be calculated. Information theory tells us what surprisal follows from \(P(H)\); it does not tell us what \(P(H)\) should be. Thus:

$$
I(H)=-\log P(H)
$$

presupposes rather than generates the relevant probability. The rhetoric of “surprise” can therefore hide the very step that requires justification. Something can appear striking without being antecedently improbable. Something can occupy a small formally described subset without being assigned small probability. And something can be improbable in one parameterization without being improbable in another. This distinction is familiar outside philosophy of mind. Every particular sequence of fifty fair coin flips is individually extremely improbable:

$$
P(\text{any specific 50-flip sequence})
=
\frac{1}{2^{50}}.
$$

Yet observing some particular fifty-flip sequence is not surprising. One of the sequences had to occur. What makes a result evidentially interesting is not merely that its exact description has low probability but that it belongs to some independently specified class whose occurrence was antecedently unlikely. For example:

$$
HHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHH
$$

may attract attention because “all heads” is a simple and antecedently salient pattern. But after observing an ordinary random sequence, we can always construct some highly specific predicate that uniquely characterizes it and declare that predicate astonishingly rare. That maneuver would confuse **post hoc description** with antecedent probability.

The same concern applies to harmony. If the property \(H\) is identified only after inspecting the actual psychophysical arrangement, then we need to ask whether it was independently privileged before the observation. Otherwise the argument may resemble a look-elsewhere problem: search through sufficiently many possible predicates and relations, and some strikingly describable pattern will inevitably be found.

This issue becomes especially important because “harmony” is evaluative. We observe pain, avoidance, pleasure, approach, introspective judgments, perceptual reports, and other psychophysical relations. We then select certain of these relations and characterize their coordination as normatively or semantically fortunate. Having identified that feature of the actual world, we construct a class of alternative mappings that fail to preserve it. The resulting contrast may be genuine. But the relevant probability cannot simply be inferred from the fact that our chosen property is uncommon among the alternatives generated after the fact. We need to know why \(H\) itself constitutes a privileged event-type. In statistical terms, there is a difference between asking:

$$
P(H)
$$

for a hypothesis or pattern specified in advance, and asking something closer to:

$$
P(\text{some comparably striking pattern is discoverable}).
$$

The second probability may be vastly larger. This connects with a broader problem of arbitrary partitioning. Suppose one divides psychophysical mappings according to whether they exhibit familiar normative alignment. Another theorist might divide the same mappings according to aesthetic symmetry, computational simplicity, color-valence correspondences, numerical properties, or some indefinitely large number of other structural features. The world will instantiate many highly specific patterns simply because sufficiently many ways of describing it are available.

A further complication arises if \(H\) bundles several kinds of harmony together. Normative harmony in pain and pleasure, semantic harmony in introspective judgment, and other psychophysical alignments may look like multiple converging pieces of evidence. But their evidential contributions cannot simply be multiplied as though they were conditionally independent. A single underlying psychophysical principle could make several of them obtain together. The relevant likelihood is therefore the joint probability of the evidence given each hypothesis, with any dependence among the observations explicitly respected. This does not dissolve the harmony argument, but it prevents a collection of related manifestations from acquiring evidential force merely by being counted several times. The harmony theorist therefore needs to establish not merely that:

$$
\mathcal{H}\subset\Omega
$$

is small, but that \(\mathcal{H}\) is a **non-arbitrarily privileged subset** under the relevant theory. This is closely related to a Goodman-style problem of projectibility. Formal languages permit indefinitely many predicates and classifications that fit the same observed data. The difficult question is why some classifications track genuine natural structure while others are gerrymandered artifacts of description. The same problem arises here: why should *harmony* carve the psychophysical possibility space at a metaphysically privileged joint? Without an answer, the inference

$$
\text{few mappings are harmonious}
$$

$$
\therefore
$$

$$
\text{harmony is improbable}
$$

remains underdetermined. There is another possible response. The defender of the harmony argument might concede that mappings should not receive equal probability and instead claim that naturalism should favor **simple** psychophysical laws. Perhaps probability should decline as complexity increases. This would avoid a crude uniform distribution over all conceivable mappings. But this simply relocates the problem. We would now require some measure of complexity:

$$
K(M),
$$

and perhaps a probability assignment of the general form:

$$
P(M\mid N)\propto 2^{-K(M)}.
$$

Such ideas resemble algorithmic probability, where shorter descriptions receive greater weight. But now the result depends upon what counts as a description language, which computational representation is privileged, and why naturalism should induce that particular measure over psychophysical laws.

Complexity measures themselves are not automatically representation-independent. A law that is simple under one vocabulary can become complicated under another. Unless some privileged descriptive language can be justified, appeals to simplicity cannot simply generate the missing probability distribution. More fundamentally, naturalism is not itself a stochastic mechanism for selecting psychophysical laws. It is a broad metaphysical hypothesis. To write

$$
P(H\mid N)
$$

is therefore already to ask how rational credence should be distributed across a large family of more specific naturalistic theories:

$$
N_1,N_2,N_3,\ldots.
$$

If these subtheories differ in their psychophysical commitments, then:

$$
P(H\mid N)
=
\sum_i
P(H\mid N_i)\,P(N_i\mid N)
$$

in the discrete case. The probability of harmony under naturalism therefore depends not only upon how harmony behaves under particular naturalistic theories, but upon the conditional weights

$$
P(N_i\mid N).
$$

Again, those weights do not emerge simply from counting conceivable mappings. This exposes a deeper problem with the phrase:

> “Naturalism gives us no reason to expect this mapping rather than another.”

Even if true, this expresses **ignorance**, not a probability distribution. From:

$$
\text{N does not uniquely determine }M
$$

it does not follow that:

$$
P(M_i\mid N)=P(M_j\mid N)
$$

for all alternatives. The philosophical literature on Bayesian epistemology treats the assignment of priors as a substantive problem precisely because coherence alone generally fails to determine unique prior probabilities. Objective Bayesian attempts to supplement coherence with principles such as indifference or maximum entropy remain controversial, and different partitions can produce conflicting recommendations. This point can be stated more sharply:

$$
\boxed{\text{lack of explanation}}
\neq
\boxed{\text{uniform probability}}.
$$

And likewise:

$$
\boxed{\text{ignorance}}
\neq
\boxed{\text{improbability}}.
$$

A naturalist who lacks a theory explaining why one psychophysical law obtains rather than another is not thereby committed to regarding all conceivable laws as equiprobable. The failure to supply an explanation may be an explanatory deficit, but it does not automatically determine the numerical or comparative likelihood required by the Bayesian argument. This distinction is crucial because the psychophysical harmony argument is ultimately comparative. It requires:

$$
\frac{P(H\mid T,B)}
{P(H\mid N,B)}
\gg1.
$$

If the denominator is generated by an unjustified transition from conceivable alternatives to equal weighting, then the likelihood ratio may acquire its apparent magnitude from assumptions that have never been defended. The issue is not that probabilities over broad metaphysical theories are impossible in principle. Nor is it necessary to demand precise numerical values. Comparative Bayesian reasoning can often proceed with qualitative inequalities. But even a qualitative claim such as

$$
P(H\mid N)\ll P(H\mid T)
$$

requires some warrant for believing that naturalism genuinely places little probability on harmony. It cannot be established merely by pointing to the large number of disharmonious mappings that can be conceived or formally generated. The probabilistic structure required by the argument is therefore much richer than its ordinary-language formulation suggests. At minimum, the proponent must identify:

1. a principled possibility space \(\Omega_N\) relevant under naturalism;
2. a non-arbitrary criterion defining the harmonious subset \(\mathcal{H}\);
3. a justified measure \(\mu_N\) over that space;
4. some reason to think

$$
\mu_N(\mathcal{H})\ll1;
$$

5. and some reason to think this result is stable under equivalent redescriptions and changes of parameterization.

Without these ingredients, the movement from “there are many imaginable disharmonious mappings” to “harmony is surprising on naturalism” remains incomplete. The problem can be summarized in one sequence:

$$
\text{conceivable alternatives}
$$

$$
\not\Rightarrow
$$

$$
\text{equiprobable alternatives}
$$

$$
\not\Rightarrow
$$

$$
\text{combinatorial rarity}
$$

$$
\not\Rightarrow
$$

$$
\text{Bayesian improbability}.
$$

The word *surprising* can make this sequence feel continuous. Formally, it is not. And once this distinction is recognized, a broader worry emerges. If the probability of harmony depends upon how the state space is carved up and which features are selected as salient, then perhaps the problem is not merely that the argument has failed to assign the correct probability. Perhaps the procedure by which “striking harmonies” are identified is itself too permissive. Given enough freedom in selecting relata, predicates, partitions, and evaluative standards, one may be able to manufacture indefinitely many apparently remarkable mappings. That is the next problem.

## Independently Privileged Rarity and Manufactured Harmonies

The previous section argued that the relative number of harmonious and disharmonious mappings does not, by itself, determine a probability. But there is a deeper problem. Even before asking how probability should be distributed across a space of mappings, we must ask why *this particular feature* of the actual psychophysical arrangement has been selected as the relevant feature to measure. The problem is one of descriptive permissiveness.

A sufficiently complicated world can be partitioned, classified, and redescribed in indefinitely many ways. Given enough freedom in choosing the relevant relata and predicates, one can identify an enormous number of correlations, correspondences, symmetries, and mappings. Many of these can be made to look highly special once a comparison class is constructed around them. This raises an uncomfortable question for psychophysical harmony:

> What distinguishes a genuine metaphysical harmony from a pattern generated by the way we have chosen to describe the world?

Consider the following abstract structure. Let there be two collections of objects or states,

$$
A=\{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\}
$$

and

$$
B=\{b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n\}.
$$

Given these sets, one may define a mapping

$$
f:A\rightarrow B.
$$

If the mapping happens to exhibit some property \(R\), one may then describe \(R\) as remarkable. Perhaps \(R\) expresses symmetry, usefulness, semantic correspondence, aesthetic similarity, numerical regularity, or some other relation. But nothing prevents us from defining many alternative properties:

$$
R_1,R_2,R_3,\ldots,R_k,
$$

each of which picks out some aspect of the actual mapping. If the available descriptive vocabulary is sufficiently flexible, it will often be possible to formulate a property \(R_i\) under which the actual arrangement appears highly distinctive. We may then compare the actual mapping with all mappings that fail to instantiate \(R_i\) and conclude that the observed arrangement belongs to an extraordinarily small class. Formally:

$$
\mathcal{R}_i
=
\{M\in\Omega:M\text{ satisfies }R_i\}.
$$

If

$$
|\mathcal{R}_i|\ll|\Omega|,
$$

we may be tempted to say that the actual world exhibits a strikingly rare correspondence. But this conclusion can be manufactured cheaply if \(R_i\) was selected because the actual world happens to satisfy it. The most extreme case makes the problem obvious. Define:

$$
R^*(M)
=
\text{“}M\text{ is exactly the actual mapping.”}
$$

Then:

$$
|\mathcal{R}^*|=1.
$$

Relative to a sufficiently large possibility space, the actual mapping is now maximally rare. But nothing interesting follows. Every actual state of affairs can be uniquely characterized after the fact. Its uniqueness does not automatically turn it into an explanatory anomaly. The important distinction is therefore between:

$$
\text{a rare description of the actual outcome}
$$

and

$$
\text{an independently privileged rare outcome}.
$$

Psychophysical harmony must belong to the second category if it is to support the intended inference. This resembles a familiar problem in inductive logic associated with Nelson Goodman's discussion of projectible predicates. A body of evidence may be described using ordinary predicates such as *green*, but the same observations can be redescribed using artificially constructed predicates such as *grue*. The empirical data alone do not mechanically determine which predicates constitute genuine lawlike regularities and which are merely gerrymandered ways of organizing the observations. The relevance to psychophysical harmony is not that the term *harmony* is exactly analogous to *grue*. The broader lesson is that **formal fit is cheap unless the predicates defining the fit are independently privileged**. The harmony theorist identifies relations such as:

$$
\text{pain}\leftrightarrow\text{avoidance},
$$

$$
\text{pleasure}\leftrightarrow\text{approach},
$$

and

$$
\text{phenomenal judgment}\leftrightarrow
\text{phenomenal state making the judgment true}.
$$

These are then grouped together as instances of a higher-order property:

$$
H=\text{psychophysical harmony}.
$$

The next step is to contrast \(H\) with a vast collection of alternative mappings that lack it. But why is \(H\) the relevant higher-order property? There may be indefinitely many other ways of classifying the same total psychophysical arrangement. One could construct correspondences according to informational complexity, temporal ordering, neural geometry, numerical properties, evolutionary age, linguistic categories, affective dimensions, computational roles, or arbitrarily complicated combinations of these. For almost any sufficiently rich system, there will be many predicates under which the actual arrangement occupies a tiny and highly distinctive subset of formally possible arrangements. This creates what may be called an **explanatory gerrymandering problem**.

The concern is not that psychophysical harmony is literally arbitrary. Pain and avoidance plainly bear an intelligible relationship. Rather, the concern is that the argument needs some principled account of why this relationship constitutes the kind of independently privileged pattern whose rarity requires explanation. Otherwise the procedure risks becoming:

$$
\text{observe world}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{identify salient feature }F
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{construct comparison class in which }F\text{ is rare}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{declare }F\text{ surprising}.
$$

That sequence can be repeated for an enormous number of features. This is closely related to what statisticians call a **look-elsewhere effect**. If one tests a single hypothesis specified in advance, an unusual result may provide substantial evidence. But if one searches through hundreds or thousands of possible patterns and reports only the most striking one, the evidential significance of that pattern must be adjusted for the size and flexibility of the search. The intuitive reason is straightforward. If enough possible relationships are inspected, finding *some* apparently remarkable relationship becomes much less surprising. The same distinction can be represented as:

$$
P(\text{this independently specified pattern})
$$

versus:

$$
P(\text{some strikingly describable pattern can be found}).
$$

The second probability may be much larger than the first. Psychophysical harmony potentially faces an analogous issue. We do not encounter the world with a pretheoretically specified random variable labeled “psychophysical harmony” and then discover, to our astonishment, that it takes an extremely unusual value. We first encounter a complicated network of phenomenal, neural, behavioral, linguistic, evolutionary, and practical relations. Philosophical analysis then singles out certain of those relations and groups them under the category *harmony*. That category may be perfectly legitimate. But if its evidential force depends upon its alleged rarity, then its privileged status must be established independently of the fact that the actual world exhibits it. This is particularly important because the word *harmony* is evaluatively loaded. To describe a relation as harmonious is not merely to describe its structure. It suggests appropriateness, fit, coordination, or successful arrangement. Compare:

> Pain is correlated with avoidance behavior.

with:

> Pain is harmoniously mapped onto the behavior that its phenomenal character rationalizes.

The second description invites us to see a remarkable fit between independently constituted structures. But some of that impression may arise from the evaluative vocabulary itself. The same fact can be described at multiple levels:

$$
\text{correlation}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{coordination}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{harmony}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{fortunate harmony}.
$$

Each description adds interpretive content. This matters because *fortunate* can easily slide into *unlikely*. Yet these are entirely different concepts. A state of affairs may be beneficial without being improbable:

$$
\operatorname{Valuable}(H)
\not\Rightarrow
P(H)\ll1.
$$

Likewise, an event may be extremely improbable without being beneficial, meaningful, or explanatory. Thus:

$$
\boxed{\text{fortunate}}
\not\Rightarrow
\boxed{\text{rare}}
\not\Rightarrow
\boxed{\text{improbable}}.
$$

The rhetoric of harmony can make these transitions seem more natural than they actually are. Consider an ordinary biological example. Human bodies contain numerous regulatory systems whose sensory outputs interact with behavior. Hunger accompanies certain physiological conditions and tends to motivate food-seeking. Thirst accompanies other physiological conditions and tends to motivate drinking. Feelings of fatigue influence rest. Various forms of discomfort influence posture, movement, and environmental response. Each of these can be redescribed in the vocabulary of harmony:

$$
\text{physiological deficit}
\leftrightarrow
\text{phenomenal signal}
\leftrightarrow
\text{corrective behavior}.
$$

Once represented this way, we can imagine countless alternative assignments. Hunger might have produced an urge to sing. Dehydration might have produced visual pleasure. Fatigue might have generated a powerful desire to sprint. For every regulatory system, one can construct indefinitely many “disharmonious” permutations. The question is whether repeatedly generating such permutations reveals independent metaphysical fine-tuning or merely demonstrates how easy it is to redescribe organized biological systems as mappings and then permute the labels assigned to their components.

The issue becomes more acute if the relevant behavior is partly learned. Many connections between sensation and action are not fixed one-step responses. They emerge through development, feedback, conditioning, conceptual learning, cultural practice, and practical reasoning. In such cases, the apparent static relation:

$$
Q_i\longrightarrow B_i
$$

may actually abbreviate a complicated process:

$$
Q_i
+
\text{learning}
+
\text{memory}
+
\text{environment}
+
\text{conceptual organization}
\longrightarrow
B_i.
$$

Describing the endpoints as a “harmonious mapping” can suppress the causal history that makes their relation intelligible. This does not eliminate every psychophysical question. One may still ask why there is phenomenal consciousness at all, why particular neural structures are associated with particular phenomenal characters, or why consciousness bears the causal and normative features that it does. These may remain serious metaphysical problems. But they should not be conflated with the stronger claim that the world displays an independently specified, astronomically unlikely *mapping* selected from a vast space of alternatives. That stronger claim requires answering several prior questions: Why are these the relevant relata? Why are they individuated at this level rather than another? Why is this particular correspondence classified as harmony? Why are these alternative mappings the relevant comparison class? Why should the property of harmony have been regarded as evidentially privileged independently of observing the actual psychophysical organization?

And how many other properties of the same organization could have been presented as equally striking had philosophers chosen to focus on them instead? Until these questions are answered, psychophysical harmony risks being less like discovering that a dart has landed in an independently marked microscopic target and more like drawing a tiny circle around the dart after it has landed. That analogy is useful because it separates two importantly different situations. In the first:

$$
\text{target specified first}
\rightarrow
\text{dart lands inside it}.
$$

The smallness of the target may genuinely contribute to surprise. In the second:

$$
\text{dart lands}
\rightarrow
\text{small target drawn around landing point}.
$$

The same geometric rarity can be manufactured without evidential significance. The harmony theorist needs to show that psychophysical harmony resembles the first case rather than the second. This requirement becomes especially pressing when the argument moves from the explanandum to its proposed explanation. For there is a parallel danger on the explanatory side. Just as one can define a property around the observed data, one can construct hypotheses specifically designed to predict that property. Given any observed feature \(H\), consider the hypothesis:

$$
D_H=
\text{“There exists an agent whose overriding aim is to produce }H\text{.”}
$$

Then, unsurprisingly,

$$
P(H\mid D_H)
$$

will be high. But this does not automatically constitute strong evidence for \(D_H\). The hypothesis has been tailored to the evidence. The same danger arises if theism is enriched with a specific claim about divine intentions:

$$
T_H=
T+
\text{“God strongly prefers psychophysical harmony.”}
$$

The resulting theory may predict harmony very well. But its predictive success matters only if the preference claim is independently justified rather than introduced because harmony is already known to obtain. Thus, the permissiveness problem occurs on both sides of the explanation:

$$
\boxed{
\text{arbitrarily selected explanandum}
}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{
\text{arbitrarily tailored explanans}
}.
$$

This is why the problem of manufactured harmonies is not merely an amusing side issue. It places a constraint on the entire argumentative method. Before an observed correspondence can function as evidence, the argument must show that the correspondence is not simply one salient pattern extracted from an indefinitely large space of possible descriptions. And before a hypothesis can receive evidential credit for predicting that correspondence, the hypothesis must have independent content that makes the prediction non-ad hoc. Psychophysical harmony may satisfy these requirements. But they cannot be assumed merely because the actual relations strike us as fortunate.

The burden is therefore twofold. The proponent must explain why *harmony* is a natural and independently privileged feature of psychophysical reality rather than an instance of explanatory gerrymandering, and then explain why the proposed theory predicts that feature for reasons not themselves derived from the observation.

The second requirement becomes particularly important for the proposed theistic explanation. Even if every preceding objection is granted—even if harmony is a genuine, well-defined, metaphysically possible, and genuinely improbable feature under naturalism—the argument still requires the other half of its likelihood ratio:

$$
P(H\mid T).
$$

And determining that probability turns out to require a substantive theory of what God would be likely to do.

# V. The Comparative Warrant: From Improbability to Theistic Evidence

## The Divine Psychology Problem

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that every objection developed so far is set aside. Grant that psychophysical harmony is a genuine and independently specifiable feature of reality. Grant that phenomenal and physical states can be individuated independently, that radically different mappings are metaphysically possible, and that there is some principled probability measure according to which harmonious mappings are highly improbable under naturalism. In other words, grant the harmony theorist the controversial claim:

$$
P(H\mid N)\ll1.
$$

Even with all of this conceded, the argument for theism is not complete. A comparative likelihood argument requires not merely a low probability under naturalism but a substantially higher probability under theism:

$$
P(H\mid T)>P(H\mid N).
$$

The problem is that the value of the left-hand side is far from obvious. To estimate \(P(H\mid T)\), we need to know what God would be likely to create. But answering that question requires substantive assumptions about divine motivation, preference, practical reasoning, and choice. Bare theism does not obviously provide enough information to determine those probabilities.

This problem is familiar from the literature on fine-tuning arguments. Miles Donahue calls it the **divine psychology objection**: even if some observed feature of the universe is extremely improbable under naturalism, a design argument still requires warrant for thinking that it is sufficiently probable given God. If our knowledge of God's motives is inadequate, the relevant likelihood may simply be inscrutable. Donahue ultimately argues that the objection can be answered in the fine-tuning case, but he explicitly agrees that the proponent of the design argument bears the burden of supplying warrant for the relevant theistic likelihood; merely observing that the event is improbable under chance does not establish that it is probable under design. The same problem arises for psychophysical harmony.

Let \(T\) denote bare theism: roughly, the hypothesis that there exists an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good creator. Let \(H\) denote psychophysical harmony. The harmony argument suggests that a good God would have reasons to create conscious agents whose experiences are appropriately coordinated with their behavior and whose phenomenal judgments are generally true. Cutter and Crummett explicitly present these fortunate psychophysical correlations as evidence for theism. But the existence of a reason favoring \(H\) does not by itself establish that:

$$
P(H\mid T)
$$

is high. There is an important difference between saying:

$$
\text{God has some reason to produce }H
$$

and saying:

$$
\text{God would probably produce }H.
$$

The first is a normative claim. The second is a probabilistic claim about an agent's behavior. Moving from one to the other requires a theory of how God's reasons determine God's choices. This point becomes clearer if we introduce a variable \(\psi\) representing the relevant aspects of divine psychology: God's preferences, ranking of goods, intentions, decision principles, and dispositions toward creation. What the argument can more straightforwardly discuss is:

$$
P(H\mid T,\psi).
$$

For example, suppose \(\psi_1\) contains the following principle:

> God places extremely high value on creating embodied conscious agents whose phenomenal states rationalize their behavior and accurately ground their introspective judgments.

Conditional on \(T\) and \(\psi_1\), perhaps psychophysical harmony is indeed highly probable:

$$
P(H\mid T,\psi_1)\approx1.
$$

But bare theism is not obviously identical to:

$$
T+\psi_1.
$$

If different divine motivational structures are compatible with theism, then the relevant likelihood must somehow average across them. In a simplified discrete representation,

$$
P(H\mid T)
=
\sum_i
P(H\mid T,\psi_i)\,P(\psi_i\mid T).
$$

In a continuous representation, the same point can be expressed as:

$$
P(H\mid T)
=
\int
P(H\mid T,\psi)\,dP(\psi\mid T).
$$

Now the difficulty becomes explicit. To determine \(P(H\mid T)\), one needs some account of:

$$
P(\psi\mid T).
$$

What probability does bare theism assign to different possible divine priorities, intentions, or rankings of reasons? Without an answer, the claim that harmony is substantially more likely under theism remains underdetermined. This problem should not be expressed by saying that God can do infinitely many things and therefore the probability of any particular thing must be extremely small. That would repeat the same mistake criticized earlier in connection with naturalism. The number of possible outcomes does not itself determine their probability. An agent may be capable of performing thousands of actions while being almost certain to perform one of them because of overwhelmingly strong preferences. Thus:

$$
|\Omega_T|\text{ is large}
$$

does not imply:

$$
P(H\mid T)\text{ is small}.
$$

Nevertheless, divine omnipotence makes the psychology problem more, not less, important in another sense. If God's abilities impose few restrictions on what God can actualize, then predictive content must come primarily from God's reasons and preferences.

For an ordinary physical mechanism, the range of outcomes may be tightly constrained by its causal structure. For an omnipotent agent, the feasible action space is extraordinarily broad. Consequently, prediction depends heavily upon the agent's motivational structure:

$$
\text{broad capacity}
+
\text{divine psychology}
\longrightarrow
\text{prediction}.
$$

Omnipotence does not give us the probability distribution. It removes many capacity constraints that might otherwise have helped narrow it. The defender of psychophysical harmony may respond that classical theism already tells us something crucial about God's psychology: God is perfectly good. Psychophysical harmony is good for conscious creatures. Therefore a perfectly good God has reason to produce it. This may provide some evidence regarding divine motivation, but it still does not yield the needed likelihood without further assumptions. Suppose:

$$
V(H)>0,
$$

where \(V(H)\) represents the value of harmony. It does not follow that:

$$
P(H\mid T)\approx1.
$$

The reason is that a perfectly good agent may recognize indefinitely many goods. God might have reasons concerning freedom, autonomy, beauty, diversity, simplicity, regularity, moral development, varieties of consciousness, the independence of created agents, or goods beyond our epistemic access. Some of these reasons may complement harmony; others may compete with it. Knowing that harmony is valuable does not tell us how that value is ranked relative to every other consideration relevant to divine creation. A complete prediction would require answers to questions such as:

* Does God always maximize value?
* Is there a uniquely best possible world?
* If several worlds are equally good, how does God choose among them?
* If there is no best possible world, what principle governs divine selection?
* Does God have reason to create at all?
* How are competing goods weighed?
* Does perfect goodness uniquely determine a creative intention?
* How strongly does God value psychophysical harmony relative to other possible goods?

These are not marginal questions. They are what determine whether harmony is likely under the hypothesis.

Perfect goodness is therefore not yet a divine decision theory.

The gap can be represented as:

$$
\text{H is good}
$$

$$
\not\Rightarrow
$$

$$
\text{God overwhelmingly prefers H}
$$

$$
\not\Rightarrow
$$

$$
P(H\mid T)\text{ is high}.
$$

The second implication is particularly important. Even if we know that a perfectly good agent has reason for some outcome, prediction requires knowing how reasons translate into choices. This becomes even more difficult when the evidence is highly specific. The observation is not merely that God created something valuable. It is that God allegedly selected one particular form of psychophysical organization. To predict that organization, we need not merely the proposition:

> God values conscious welfare.

We need enough information about divine priorities to infer:

> God would be sufficiently likely to instantiate conscious creatures governed by psychophysical relations of roughly this kind.

That is a considerably stronger claim. One apparent solution is to enrich the theistic hypothesis. Instead of bare theism \(T\), consider:

$$
T_H
=
T
+
\text{God strongly intends to create psychophysically harmonious agents}.
$$

Conditional on \(T_H\), the probability of harmony may indeed become very high:

$$
P(H\mid T_H)\approx1.
$$

But this maneuver threatens to make the evidential argument trivial. Given virtually any observation \(E\), we can construct:

$$
T_E
=
T+\text{God strongly intends to produce }E.
$$

Then:

$$
P(E\mid T_E)
$$

will predictably be high. If we observe an enormous universe, we can posit that God values enormous universes. If we observe mathematical elegance, we can posit that God strongly prefers mathematical elegance. If we observe biological complexity, we can posit a divine preference for biological complexity. If we observe psychophysical harmony, we can posit a divine preference for psychophysical harmony. This method can produce a highly predictive theological hypothesis for almost any datum. But explanatory success cannot be obtained merely by adding the observed evidence to the agent's motivational profile.

Donahue discusses essentially this strategy in the fine-tuning context: one might incorporate God's intention to create a life-permitting universe directly into the hypothesis. He argues that doing so does not solve the original probabilistic problem, because the question has merely been displaced to the probability that God would possess that intention in the first place. The same reasoning applies here. Suppose:

$$
P(H\mid T_H)=1.
$$

What matters is then the conditional probability of the auxiliary claim:

$$
P(T_H\mid T),
$$

or equivalently the probability that, given bare theism, God would possess the relevant intention. The divine psychology problem has not disappeared. It has simply moved one level upward. The structure is:

$$
T
\longrightarrow
\text{divine intention for }H
\longrightarrow
H.
$$

If the first arrow is inscrutable, making the second arrow deterministic does not rescue the overall prediction. This also reveals a danger of **ad hoc accommodation**. A hypothesis should receive evidential credit for predicting a datum when the features generating the prediction have independent motivation. If the divine preference for harmony is introduced primarily because harmony has already been observed, then the theory risks being fitted to the evidence rather than independently predicting it. This point is especially important because the psychophysical harmony argument is presented as a comparative Bayesian argument. The relevant comparison should not be:

$$
P(H\mid T_H)
$$

against:

$$
P(H\mid N),
$$

if \(T_H\) contains substantially more evidence-specific information than \(N\). A fair comparison requires comparable attention to auxiliary assumptions on both sides. Naturalism is itself a broad hypothesis. Earlier we represented it as containing more specific theories:

$$
N_1,N_2,\ldots,N_n.
$$

Likewise, bare theism contains more specific hypotheses about divine intentions:

$$
T_1,T_2,\ldots,T_m.
$$

Thus:

$$
P(H\mid N)
=
\sum_i
P(H\mid N_i)P(N_i\mid N),
$$

while:

$$
P(H\mid T)
=
\sum_j
P(H\mid T_j)P(T_j\mid T).
$$

This exposes an important **symmetry requirement**. The argument should apply the same standard of probabilistic specification to both hypotheses. It would be methodologically asymmetric to reason:

> Naturalism does not determine which psychophysical law obtains; therefore harmony is extremely improbable under naturalism.

and then immediately reason:

> God could have a good reason to prefer harmony; therefore harmony is highly probable under theism.

The standards are different. On the naturalistic side, underdetermination is treated as evidence of low probability. On the theistic side, the mere availability of a plausible motivation is treated as evidence of high probability. But neither move follows automatically.

The naturalist's lack of a unique prediction does not entail a uniform distribution over every imaginable psychophysical mapping. Likewise, the theist's ability to identify one divine reason favoring harmony does not entail a probability distribution concentrated heavily on harmonious worlds. In both cases, the argument needs an additional weighting principle. The symmetry can be stated compactly:

$$
\boxed{\text{naturalistic underdetermination}}
\neq
\boxed{\text{low probability}}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\text{divine reason}}
\neq
\boxed{\text{high probability}}.
$$

This matters because the desired evidential conclusion is a likelihood ratio:

$$
\Lambda
=
\frac{P(H\mid T)}
{P(H\mid N)}.
$$

Earlier sections challenged the denominator. This section challenges the numerator. If \(P(H\mid N)\) lacks a principled measure over psychophysical possibilities, while \(P(H\mid T)\) lacks a principled model of divine choice, then the impressive-looking expression

$$
\frac{P(H\mid T)}
{P(H\mid N)}
$$

may conceal the fact that neither term has been adequately established. The problem does not require proving that:

$$
P(H\mid T)<P(H\mid N).
$$

Nor does it require claiming that \(P(H\mid T)\) literally has no mathematical value in some objective metaphysical sense. The more modest conclusion is enough:

> The proponent has not yet provided epistemic warrant for the inequality required by the argument.

If the relevant theistic likelihood is inscrutable, then the comparative Bayesian inference cannot proceed. Donahue makes precisely this burden-of-proof point in discussing fine-tuning. Even while ultimately resisting the divine psychology objection, he argues that the defender of the design inference must provide affirmative warrant for the claim that the observed evidence is not exceedingly improbable given God. It is not enough to insist that the skeptic has failed to prove otherwise. Applied to psychophysical harmony, the requirement is straightforward. The proponent must establish something like:

$$
P(H\mid T,B)\gg P(H\mid N,B),
$$

not merely:

$$
\text{God could have a reason for }H.
$$

That distinction becomes especially important once theism is compared with other purposive hypotheses. If the explanatory advantage comes simply from positing an agent with motivations favorable to harmony, then God is not the only available candidate. A simulator, demiurge, cosmic engineer, teleological principle, or other purposive mechanism could be specified in ways that make harmony highly probable as well.

Indeed, the ease with which such alternatives can be generated exposes something important about the evidential structure of the argument. Once an outcome has been classified as valuable or “harmonious,” it becomes relatively easy to construct an agent hypothesis under which that outcome was intended. But the fact that:

$$
P(H\mid D_H)
$$

is high for some suitably specified designer hypothesis \(D_H\) does not by itself tell us that \(D_H\) is independently plausible, nor that theism specifically is favored. Thus, even after granting the harmony argument its entire naturalistic improbability claim, a second inferential burden remains. The proponent must move from:

$$
P(H\mid N)\ll1
$$

to a defensible claim about:

$$
P(H\mid T).
$$

That move requires a theory of divine psychology sufficient to generate comparative expectations about creation. If the theory is too thin, the likelihood remains underdetermined. If it is enriched specifically to predict the observed harmony, it risks ad hoc accommodation. And if the relevant motivation is simply that some intelligent agent would value harmonious conscious creatures, then the argument no longer points uniquely toward theism.

The result is a dilemma. **Bare theism appears too psychologically unspecific to yield the required likelihood, while sufficiently specific theism risks obtaining its predictive success by incorporating precisely the preferences needed to fit the datum.**

This does not prove that no non-ad hoc account of divine motivation can be supplied. It does show that the comparative probability cannot be treated as obvious. The argument's theistic half requires substantive philosophical work just as surely as its naturalistic half. And once this is recognized, a further question becomes unavoidable: even if psychophysical harmony really does provide evidence for intentional design, why should that designer be identified with God rather than with one of the many other purposive hypotheses capable of producing exactly the same prediction?

## Underdetermination: Why Not a Simulator?

Suppose the harmony theorist successfully answers every objection raised so far. Suppose psychophysical harmony is a genuine phenomenon, the alternative mappings are coherent metaphysical possibilities, harmony is genuinely improbable under naturalism, and purposive agency provides a plausible explanation of why the actual mapping is harmonious. Even then, an important problem remains: **why should the relevant purposive hypothesis be theism?** Once the explanatory structure is stated abstractly, the difficulty becomes obvious. Let \(H\) denote psychophysical harmony. The argument claims:

$$
P(H\mid T)>P(H\mid N),
$$

because a rational and benevolent creator would have reason to produce creatures whose phenomenology is appropriately coordinated with their behavior and judgments. But now consider a different hypothesis:

$$
S=
\text{our world is an intentionally designed simulation}.
$$

More specifically, suppose the simulators deliberately create conscious agents and have reason to make those agents function coherently within the simulated environment. If they are capable of controlling the relation between simulated physical states and phenomenal states, then they too might deliberately arrange:

$$
\text{bodily damage}
\longrightarrow
\text{pain}
\longrightarrow
\text{avoidance},
$$

rather than:

$$
\text{bodily damage}
\longrightarrow
\text{intense pleasure}
\longrightarrow
\text{avoidance}.
$$

Likewise, they might arrange for the physical or computational states producing phenomenal judgments to be associated with the experiences that make those judgments true:

$$
\text{report “I see red”}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{red phenomenology}.
$$

If the simulation designers want cognitively coherent conscious agents, psychophysical harmony would be a natural design feature. We might therefore have:

$$
P(H\mid S)>P(H\mid N).
$$

Indeed, conditional on a sufficiently specific simulation hypothesis, the probability could be very high:

$$
P(H\mid S_H)\approx1,
$$

where \(S_H\) specifies simulators intending to create psychophysically coherent conscious agents. The point is not that the simulation hypothesis is therefore true, or even that it is particularly plausible. The point is argumentative. **The same evidential form used to support theism appears capable of supporting a non-theistic designer hypothesis.** If psychophysical harmony is evidence for God because God could intentionally coordinate phenomenal and functional states, then any sufficiently capable purposive agent with similar intentions appears able to occupy the same explanatory role. This exposes an underdetermination problem. The relevant inference begins to look less like:

$$
H
\longrightarrow
T
$$

and more like:

$$
H
\longrightarrow
\text{some purposive or teleological explanation}.
$$

The latter category is substantially broader. It might include:

$$
T=\text{classical theism},
$$

$$
S=\text{simulation design},
$$

$$
D=\text{a finite cosmic designer},
$$

$$
A=\text{axiarchic or value-selecting principles},
$$

$$
L_T=\text{teleological psychophysical laws},
$$

or other hypotheses according to which valuable or coherent psychophysical relations are selected rather than arising without purposive constraint. The psychophysical harmony datum by itself does not obviously discriminate among these hypotheses. This is especially important because the harmony argument already characterizes the explanandum in a way that invites an engineering interpretation. We are asked to imagine two independently specified domains,

$$
P=\{\text{physical or functional states}\}
$$

and

$$
\Phi=\{\text{phenomenal states}\},
$$

connected by a mapping

$$
M:P\rightarrow\Phi.
$$

The puzzle is then:

> Why was the “right” mapping instantiated rather than one of the countless wrong ones?

But this way of representing the problem makes an intentional assignment mechanism immediately natural. An engineer confronted with two domains and a mapping relation could simply assign the outputs in accordance with some goal. The very ontology that makes psychophysical harmony appear mysterious under naturalism therefore makes an artificial-design explanation unusually easy to formulate. Suppose, for example, that a simulator has access to the following design choice:

$$
P_{\text{damage}}
\mapsto
Q_i,
$$

where \(Q_i\) can be selected from a range of phenomenal states. If the simulator wants agents to respond intelligently to damaging conditions, it may select:

$$
Q_i=Q_{\text{pain}}.
$$

Likewise, if the simulator wants introspective reports to be reliable, it may ensure that:

$$
P_{\text{report-red}}
\mapsto
Q_{\text{red}}.
$$

The resulting world exhibits precisely the kind of normative and semantic harmony that the theistic argument treats as evidence of purposive arrangement. At this point, one might object that a simulator is itself part of some larger physical reality. The simulation hypothesis therefore does not ultimately explain psychophysical harmony. If the simulators themselves are conscious physical beings, then their own world may exhibit the same psychophysical correspondence:

$$
H_{\text{simulators}}.
$$

The question has merely been displaced upward. This is a legitimate objection, but it does not rescue the original inference to theism. It shows, at most, that simulation design may provide only a **proximate** explanation of our psychophysical harmony rather than an ultimate explanation of psychophysical harmony as such.

That distinction matters.

The evidential argument initially asks why *our* psychophysical states are harmoniously arranged. A simulation hypothesis can potentially answer:

> Because the simulators intentionally engineered them that way.

If the response is:

> But then we must explain the simulators' own psychophysical harmony,

a new explanatory question has been introduced. Perhaps the chain must eventually terminate in some deeper law, metaphysical necessity, teleological principle, brute fact, or divine explanation. But the original evidence does not determine which terminus is correct. To get from:

$$
\text{our harmony has a purposive proximate explanation}
$$

to:

$$
\text{the ultimate explanation is classical theism},
$$

one needs additional premises. The simulation example therefore serves an important methodological function. It reveals that the alleged evidence is not naturally **theism-specific**. The same point can be generalized. For any observed harmony \(H\), consider a designer hypothesis:

$$
D_H=
\text{a sufficiently capable designer intends to produce }H.
$$

Then:

$$
P(H\mid D_H)
$$

will predictably be high. But the existence of such hypotheses does not tell us which designer hypothesis should receive significant prior probability. Nor does it establish that classical theism possesses a uniquely favorable likelihood. This interacts directly with the divine psychology problem from the previous section. In order to make psychophysical harmony highly probable under theism, we had to specify divine motives:

$$
T_H=
T+\text{God strongly prefers psychophysical harmony}.
$$

But once those motives are specified, a structurally parallel hypothesis becomes available:

$$
S_H=
S+\text{simulators strongly prefer psychophysical harmony}.
$$

If:

$$
P(H\mid T_H)\approx1,
$$

then plausibly:

$$
P(H\mid S_H)\approx1
$$

as well. The evidential comparison therefore cannot stop at likelihoods. One would need to compare prior probabilities, theoretical complexity, independent motivation, explanatory scope, and other evidential consequences:

$$
P(T_H\mid B),
$$

$$
P(S_H\mid B),
$$

and the corresponding likelihoods for evidence beyond psychophysical harmony.

That is a much larger philosophical project than the harmony argument initially appears to require.

The same reasoning applies to non-agentive alternatives. Suppose one posits a fundamental teleological principle:

$$
L_H=
\text{psychophysical laws tend toward normatively valuable configurations}.
$$

Then harmony may be highly expected under \(L_H\). Or suppose one proposes an axiarchic principle according to which valuable worlds are metaphysically favored. Again, harmony might receive substantial probability. One need not endorse any of these theories. Their significance is dialectical: **if the explanandum is simply that the psychophysical arrangement is valuable, coherent, or appropriate, then many hypotheses can be constructed under which such an arrangement is expected.** The psychophysical harmony argument therefore faces two separate problems of underdetermination. The first is the problem discussed previously:

$$
T
\not\Rightarrow
\text{a uniquely specified divine psychology}.
$$

The second is:

$$
\text{purposive explanation}
\not\Rightarrow
T.
$$

Even if the first problem were solved by showing that a perfectly good God would strongly favor harmony, the second would remain. Other purposive or teleological hypotheses may favor harmony as well. This does not mean that psychophysical harmony could never provide differential evidence among these hypotheses. Perhaps classical theism, once fully specified, possesses independent virtues that simulation hypotheses or teleological laws lack. Perhaps broader evidence favors one explanatory framework over another. But those considerations would need to do the discriminatory work. Harmony alone cannot simply be treated as though:

$$
P(H\mid T)>P(H\mid N)
$$

settled the matter. Bayesian evidence is contrastive. Showing that \(H\) is more expected under \(T\) than under \(N\) establishes, at most, that \(H\) favors \(T\) *relative to that particular alternative*. It does not establish that \(T\) is the uniquely best explanation or that \(H\) discriminates strongly between \(T\) and other hypotheses under which harmony is equally expected. Thus, even granting the entire explanatory setup, the strongest conclusion may be significantly weaker than the rhetorical presentation suggests:

$$
H
\text{ may favor some purposive or value-sensitive theories over some forms of brute naturalism}.
$$

That is very different from:

$$
H
\text{ provides distinctive evidence for God}.
$$

The simulation hypothesis makes this difference unusually vivid because it mirrors the structure of the harmony argument so closely. A designer confronts a space of possible mappings, has reasons to prefer some mappings over others, and intentionally implements the mapping conducive to coherent conscious agency.

But once that structure is visible, it also reveals how permissive the underlying form of argument may be. If almost any useful psychophysical coordination can be redescribed as a “striking harmony,” and almost any such harmony can then be explained by stipulating an agent who values it, the method threatens to generate design arguments far more freely than its proponents may intend.

At this point, the abstract criticisms can be tested against an ordinary case. If the argumentative form is sound, it should not become obviously ridiculous merely because the psychophysical relation under discussion is mundane rather than philosophically elevated. Consider, then, one of the least elevated examples available: the relationship between the physiological need to defecate, the phenomenal urge associated with it, and the learned behavior of finding a toilet.

# VI. Stress Test: Gastro-Phenomenological Harmony

At this point it is useful to test the argumentative form against a less dignified example. The psychophysical harmony argument is usually presented using pain, pleasure, introspection, perceptual judgment, and other philosophically respectable phenomena. This gives the argument a certain natural gravitas. Pain is serious. Consciousness is mysterious. Phenomenology is metaphysically imposing. Once terms such as *normative harmony*, *semantic alignment*, and *psychophysical mapping* are introduced, even fairly ordinary biological facts can begin to sound like evidence of a deep structural mystery. So let us apply the same argumentative machinery to defecation.

Human beings possess gastrointestinal systems. When certain physiological conditions arise, they are often accompanied by a distinctive phenomenal state: the urge to defecate. That state is not usually experienced as a detached item of neutral information. It has motivational significance. Under ordinary circumstances, the subject recognizes the need, interrupts whatever else they are doing, and attempts to find an appropriate place to relieve themselves. We can represent the basic situation as:

$$
P_{\text{bowel}}
\longrightarrow
Q_{\text{urge}},
$$

where \(P_{\text{bowel}}\) represents the relevant physiological state and \(Q_{\text{urge}}\) represents the associated phenomenal urge. The subject then tends toward behavior such as:

$$
Q_{\text{urge}}
\longrightarrow
B_{\text{toilet-seeking}}.
$$

Already, we seem to have the ingredients of a striking psychophysical harmony. The bodily state that requires defecation is associated with a phenomenal state that alerts the subject to precisely that need. The phenomenal state, in turn, motivates behavior that is appropriate to the underlying physiological condition. The whole arrangement seems almost suspiciously convenient. One might therefore propose the following argument.

**P1.** Certain gastrointestinal states are correlated with a distinctive phenomenal urge to defecate.

**P2.** This phenomenal state motivates behavior that is strikingly appropriate to the underlying physiological condition.

**P3.** There are innumerable conceivable alternative psychophysical mappings under which the same gastrointestinal condition would have been associated with some entirely different phenomenal state.

For example:

$$
P_{\text{bowel}}
\longrightarrow
Q_{\text{euphoria}},
$$

or:

$$
P_{\text{bowel}}
\longrightarrow
Q_{\text{visual-redness}},
$$

or:

$$
P_{\text{bowel}}
\longrightarrow
Q_{\text{Mozart}},
$$

or perhaps:

$$
P_{\text{bowel}}
\longrightarrow
Q_{\text{urge-to-do-calculus}}.
$$

One might even imagine:

$$
P_{\text{bowel}}
\longrightarrow
Q_{\text{profound-metaphysical-awe}}.
$$

There appears to be no obvious formal obstacle to writing down any of these mappings. Yet, astonishingly, we do not normally experience an irresistible desire to derive the quadratic formula whenever defecation becomes physiologically necessary. Nor do we typically experience the color blue, hear a sustained B-flat, become overwhelmed by nostalgia for the Roman Empire, or acquire an intense desire to purchase patio furniture. Instead, we experience something that seems almost tailor-made to the biological problem at hand: *the need to go*. What are the odds? We may now define:

$$
G
=
\text{gastro-phenomenological harmony}.
$$

The relevant harmony consists in the fact that the phenomenal state generated by the gastrointestinal system is normatively and behaviorally coordinated with the physical condition that produces it. Out of the enormous number of imaginable mappings:

$$
M_1,M_2,M_3,\ldots,M_n,
$$

only a tiny subset appears to produce the useful relation:

$$
\text{full bowel}
\longrightarrow
\text{urge to defecate}
\longrightarrow
\text{behavior facilitating defecation}.
$$

Most mappings would be catastrophically disharmonious. Indeed, one can imagine entire worlds in which otherwise normal human beings routinely perish in confusion because bowel distension produces an intense urge to study Norwegian grammar. Another world might contain creatures who, whenever they need to defecate, experience profound aesthetic appreciation of triangles. In yet another, the physiological need to use the bathroom could produce an overwhelming sense that someone, somewhere, has forgotten an appointment. The combinatorial possibilities are limited only by our ingenuity. And yet here we are. Against all apparent odds, the phenomenal state associated with this particular bodily condition is exactly the sort of state that alerts us to the condition and tends to produce behavior conducive to resolving it. Surely this is not mere coincidence. Perhaps we should therefore compare:

$$
P(G\mid N)
$$

with:

$$
P(G\mid T).
$$

Under naturalism, why should the gastrointestinal system happen to be associated with *this* phenomenal state rather than one of the countless alternatives? Natural selection can perhaps explain the physical mechanisms governing digestion. It can explain why organisms capable of effectively eliminating waste tend to fare better than organisms incapable of doing so. But if the phenomenal mapping is independently variable, natural selection seems unable to explain why the relevant physiological state is accompanied by this particular phenomenal urge. After all, by stipulation we could hold the entire physical system fixed while changing only the phenomenology. The organism might still walk toward the toilet while experiencing the phenomenology of hearing bagpipes. Or it might sit on the toilet while enjoying phenomenal states exactly like those ordinarily associated with tasting mango. Why, then, this exquisitely appropriate urge? Theism appears to have an answer.

A benevolent God would presumably prefer that embodied conscious agents possess some awareness of when they need to defecate. God might reasonably judge that creatures whose gastrointestinal systems communicated their needs through appropriate phenomenology would have an easier time navigating embodied life. So perhaps:

$$
P(G\mid T)>P(G\mid N).
$$

We may therefore announce a new argument from natural theology:

> **The Argument from Gastro-Phenomenological Harmony.**

Its conclusion is modest. The urge to defecate does not prove God exists. It merely provides evidence for theism over naturalism. One could even quantify its evidential force. Suppose, entirely for illustrative purposes, that there are one million conceivable phenomenal states that could have accompanied the relevant physiological condition, while only ten would appropriately motivate defecatory behavior. Then:

$$
\frac{10}{1,000,000}
=
0.00001.
$$

Remarkable. A one-in-one-hundred-thousand gastro-phenomenological alignment. Naturalism, apparently, has some explaining to do. The parody can easily be extended. Consider hunger:

$$
\text{caloric deficit}
\longrightarrow
\text{hunger}
\longrightarrow
\text{food-seeking}.
$$

Why hunger? Why not the sensation of having just remembered an embarrassing event from middle school? Consider thirst:

$$
\text{dehydration}
\longrightarrow
\text{thirst}
\longrightarrow
\text{drinking}.
$$

Why should dehydration produce a sensation that motivates fluid consumption rather than an urge to reorganize one's bookshelves? Consider fatigue:

$$
\text{physiological exhaustion}
\longrightarrow
\text{tiredness}
\longrightarrow
\text{rest}.
$$

Astonishing. One can apparently construct an entire natural theology from ordinary interoception. The point of the parody is not that bodily regulation is uninteresting, nor that the relation between physiology and phenomenology poses no philosophical questions. The point is that the harmony vocabulary can make an ordinary regulatory system sound mysteriously fine-tuned merely by redescribing it at the right level of abstraction. Once the system is written as:

$$
P_i\longrightarrow Q_i,
$$

and the alternatives are represented as permutations:

$$
P_i\longrightarrow Q_j,
$$

the actual relation begins to look extraordinarily special. But this appearance depends heavily upon what has been omitted. Take toilet-seeking behavior. A mature adult who experiences the urge to defecate may locate a restroom, delay gratification, navigate social norms, lock the door, use the appropriate fixture, and clean up afterward. It would be absurd to treat the entire behavioral sequence as though it were primitively encoded in the phenomenal quality itself:

$$
Q_{\text{urge}}
\longrightarrow
B_{\text{toilet}}.
$$

Much of the relevant behavior is learned. Infants do not encounter a mysterious inner quale and immediately infer the existence of porcelain sanitation technology. They are toilet trained. They gradually learn to discriminate bodily cues, associate those cues with elimination, communicate them, delay action, recognize culturally appropriate locations, and coordinate bodily regulation with social expectations. The mature relation is therefore closer to:

$$
P_{\text{bowel}}
\longrightarrow
Q_{\text{urge}}
$$

followed by:

$$
Q_{\text{urge}}
+
\text{learning}
+
\text{memory}
+
\text{social norms}
+
\text{environmental recognition}
+
\text{practical reasoning}
\longrightarrow
B_{\text{toilet-seeking}}.
$$

That is a very different picture from:

$$
\text{physiology}
\xleftrightarrow{\text{mysterious harmony}}
\text{appropriate behavior}.
$$

The difference matters because the harmony description compresses an extended causal and developmental process into a static correspondence. Once compressed, the endpoints can be detached from the machinery connecting them and then represented as two independently constituted structures that happen, miraculously, to line up. The very abstraction that makes the relationship easy to represent also makes it easier to mystify. The same point applies to pain. Consider the slogan:

> It is surprising that pain maps onto avoidance behavior.

But actual avoidance behavior is not generally one primitive output mechanically attached to one phenomenal input. Pain interacts with learning, memory, attention, prior beliefs, social expectations, competing goals, conditioning, and practical judgment. An athlete may continue despite pain. A patient may deliberately tolerate pain to complete a medical procedure. A child may need to learn which bodily sensations indicate injury. Someone may experience chronic pain that is poorly correlated with current tissue damage. Others may suffer congenital or acquired disruptions in nociception, interoception, or behavioral response. The real structure therefore resembles:

$$
\text{physical condition}
+
\text{neural processing}
+
\text{phenomenology}
+
\text{learning}
+
\text{context}
+
\text{cognition}
\longrightarrow
\text{behavior}.
$$

The harmony argument risks replacing this complicated system with:

$$
P_{\text{avoidance}}
\mapsto
Q_{\text{pain}},
$$

and then asking why those two independently conceived items are so perfectly matched. The toilet parody makes the abstraction easier to notice because no one is initially tempted to treat defecation as metaphysically sublime. There is another revealing feature of the example: the alleged harmony is far from perfect. People misread bodily signals. Children have accidents. Adults have accidents. People experience constipation, urgency, incontinence, gastrointestinal pain, false alarms, impaired interoception, neurological dysfunction, medication side effects, and conditions in which the normal relation among bodily state, sensation, and behavior becomes unreliable. The system works well enough much of the time, but it is not difficult to find disharmony. One could, of course, respond that the argument requires only approximate harmony. Perhaps a benevolent designer need not produce perfection. But notice what happens once failures are introduced. When the system works, its success is evidence of harmony.

When it fails, the failure is dismissed as compatible with approximate harmony. This introduces another potential flexibility into the explanandum. The category must be specified carefully enough that we know in advance which outcomes count as evidence and which count as tolerated exceptions. Otherwise the harmony thesis risks becoming difficult to falsify. The parody also clarifies the problem with the word *surprising*. Is it psychologically surprising that bodily conditions produce sensations relevant to bodily regulation? Perhaps not. Is it combinatorially unusual under a formally constructed space of every imaginable sensation assignment? Almost certainly. But that is cheap. Given any actual biological relation, one can generate indefinitely many absurd alternatives. For example:

$$
\text{low blood sugar}
\longrightarrow
\text{urge to recite Homer},
$$

$$
\text{dehydration}
\longrightarrow
\text{experience of purple triangles},
$$

$$
\text{bladder pressure}
\longrightarrow
\text{desire to learn Icelandic},
$$

and so forth. The existence of these descriptions does not mean the organism's actual regulatory phenomenology was selected uniformly from a cosmic menu containing all verbally formulable alternatives. Yet the rhetoric of psychophysical harmony gains much of its force precisely by inviting the reader to populate such a menu. The pattern is:

$$
\text{actual relation}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{invent arbitrarily many bizarre alternatives}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{notice that the actual relation looks sensible}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\text{declare the sensible relation improbable}.
$$

But the absurdity of the alternatives does not establish the improbability of the actual arrangement. Indeed, if anything, the fact that many of the alternatives sound absurd may indicate that they are not being generated from an independently justified probability model at all. They are being generated from the combinatorial freedom of language. We can always say:

> What if bowel pressure produced the phenomenal character of middle C?

The sentence is easy. The metaphysics is another matter. And the probability is another matter still. The parody therefore reproduces nearly every weakness identified in the preceding sections. First, **loaded description**:

$$
\text{bodily regulation}
\longrightarrow
\text{gastro-phenomenological harmony}.
$$

Second, **formal permutation**:

$$
Q_{\text{urge}}
\longrightarrow
Q_{\text{red}}
$$

is treated as though symbolic substitution straightforwardly identifies another metaphysical possibility. Third, **modal inflation**:

> “I can imagine bowel pressure feeling like euphoria”

becomes evidence for a large space of genuine alternatives. Fourth, **probabilistic inflation**:

> “There are many imaginable alternatives”

becomes:

$$
P(G\mid N)\ll1.
$$

Fifth, **teleological accommodation**:

> “A good designer would have a reason to make creatures aware of their bodily needs”

becomes:

$$
P(G\mid T)\text{ is high}.
$$

Sixth, **designer underdetermination**: perhaps the system was programmed by simulators who wanted their virtual humans to stop defecating in inconvenient places. The joke, in other words, is not external to the argument. The joke is that the inferential form survives almost unchanged. This is why parody can be philosophically useful. A good parody preserves enough of an argument's structure that whatever becomes ridiculous cannot be dismissed merely as distortion. If the following reasoning seems obviously suspicious:

> There are countless imaginable phenomenal states that bowel distension might have produced. Only a tiny subset would motivate behavior appropriate to defecation. Yet, remarkably, humans experience exactly the sort of urge that directs them toward eliminating waste. Naturalism gives us no reason to expect this fortunate gastro-phenomenological correspondence, whereas a benevolent creator would have reason to ensure that embodied agents receive phenomenally appropriate signals. Therefore the urge to defecate provides evidence for God.

then we should ask exactly which premise makes it ridiculous. That question is more revealing than the joke itself. Is the problem that defecation is too mundane to count as philosophically significant? That would be arbitrary. If the inferential structure is sound, its soundness cannot depend upon whether the relevant biological function is solemn enough. Is the problem that we already possess a causal and evolutionary story about gastrointestinal regulation? Perhaps. But the harmony theorist can insist, exactly as in the pain case, that evolution explains only the physical and behavioral machinery while leaving the phenomenal occupant unexplained. Is the problem that toilet-seeking is partly learned? Certainly this weakens the simple mapping picture. But many instances of pain behavior, introspective reporting, and practical response are also mediated by learning and cognition. Is the problem that the bizarre alternatives are not genuinely equiprobable? Now we are getting closer.

Is the problem that the phrase *gastro-phenomenological harmony* makes an ordinary organized system sound like a separately existing metaphysical coincidence? Closer still. Is the problem that a formal space of imaginable substitutions has quietly been converted into a probability distribution? Again, yes. And is the problem that the proposed theistic likelihood depends upon attributing to God precisely the sort of preference that would make the observed feature likely? That too. The parody works because it strips away the philosophical prestige of the original examples while preserving the argumentative machinery. Once that prestige is removed, several inferential transitions become easier to see. There is a physical process. There is phenomenology. There is behavior. There is learning. There is regulation. There are causal connections among these things. From these facts, however, it does not immediately follow that reality contains a further object called a **harmonious mapping**, selected from an independently defined space of equiprobable psychophysical possibilities.

Nor does the fact that we can invent ridiculous counterfactual assignments tell us how probable the actual system was under naturalism. And the fact that one can tell a story about why God might like the actual arrangement does not yet determine:

$$
P(G\mid T).
$$

The gastro-phenomenological argument is therefore intentionally ridiculous, but its ridiculousness is diagnostic. It shows how easily the rhetoric of harmony can elevate an ordinary fact into an apparently profound probabilistic anomaly:

$$
\text{bodily state}
\rightarrow
\text{useful sensation}
$$

becomes:

$$
\text{an astonishingly fortunate mapping across ontological domains}.
$$

Once described that way, the rest of the design argument nearly writes itself. That should make us cautious. The appropriate conclusion is not that every appeal to psychophysical harmony is equivalent to an argument from defecation. The point is rather that the argumentative form needs constraints strong enough to explain why one case constitutes genuine evidence while the parody does not. If the proponent cannot specify those constraints, then the parody has exposed a problem of overgeneration: the method generates “striking harmonies” too easily. A successful psychophysical harmony argument must therefore explain why pain and introspection belong to an independently privileged evidential category while the urge to defecate does not—or else concede that gastro-phenomenological harmony belongs in the evidential ledger alongside them. And if the latter is the correct answer, natural theology may have acquired a rather larger gastrointestinal component than its defenders originally anticipated.

# VII. What a Successful Harmony Argument Would Need

The preceding discussion does not show that every psychophysical harmony argument is impossible. It shows that the intuitive formulation compresses several different philosophical tasks into a single impression of fortunate fit. If harmony is to provide genuine Bayesian evidence for theism, each inferential bridge has to be defended on its own terms. The argument can be represented compactly as:

$$
O
\overset{W_1}{\longrightarrow}
H
\overset{W_2}{\longrightarrow}
\Omega_N
\overset{W_3}{\longrightarrow}
P(H\mid N,B)\ll1
\overset{W_4}{\longrightarrow}
P(H\mid T,B)>P(H\mid N,B),
$$

where \(O\) is the ordinary psychophysical evidence, \(H\) is the claim that these facts constitute an independently significant harmony, \(\Omega_N\) is the relevant naturalistic possibility space, and \(B\) is the background information held fixed in the comparison. The argument succeeds only if the four warrants \(W_1,W_2,W_3,W_4\) are defensible.

| Warrant | What must be established | Critical questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **\(W_1\): Conceptual** | Psychophysical harmony is a genuine and independently specifiable feature of reality, not merely an evaluative redescription of ordinary psychophysical relations. The phenomenal and physical or functional states must be individuated with enough independence for their relation to constitute a contingent fit. | What exactly constitutes the harmony? Which features of phenomenal states are intrinsic, and which are identified partly through behavior, judgment, motivation, or linguistic practice? Why does the relation count as an additional explanandum rather than a different description of the same organized system? |
| **\(W_2\): Modal** | There is a principled space \(\Omega_N\) of relevant alternative psychophysical arrangements. The alternatives are not merely writable functions or verbally imaginable substitutions; they are semantically coherent, genuinely possible, and compatible with the naturalistic hypothesis under consideration. | What makes the phenomenal relata retain their identities across the permutation? Why should a formal function space represent metaphysical possibility? What bridge connects conceivability to possibility, and possibility to naturalistically live alternatives? |
| **\(W_3\): Probabilistic** | Naturalism, together with \(B\), supports a justified probability measure \(\mu_N\) over the relevant alternatives, the harmonious subset \(\mathcal H\) is independently and non-arbitrarily defined, and \(\mu_N(\mathcal H)=P(H\mid N,B)\) is sufficiently low. | What measure should be placed over the alternatives? Why should mappings receive equal or otherwise specified weights? Is the result invariant under equivalent redescriptions or changes of parameterization? Was harmony specified independently rather than selected after observing the actual world? Are different manifestations of harmony conditionally independent, or are they generated by a common structure? |
| **\(W_4\): Comparative** | Theism supports a sufficiently determinate model of divine choice under which harmony is substantially more likely than it is under naturalism, and the evidence discriminates theism from relevant competing purposive or teleological hypotheses. | What motives follow from bare theism? How do those motives translate into choices? How are competing goods ranked? Why does harmony receive high probability under that model? Why does classical theism predict it better than simulation design, finite designers, teleological laws, or axiarchic principles? |

This framework contains the substance of the more fine-grained reconstruction. A strengthened harmony argument would need to establish, at minimum, that harmony is independently specifiable; that the relata can be individuated with sufficient independence; that a principled naturalistic possibility space exists; that its members are genuine rather than merely formal alternatives; that the harmonious subset is non-arbitrary; that naturalism supports a justified measure over the space; that harmony receives low measure under it; that theism supports a sufficiently determinate model of divine choice; and that the resulting theistic likelihood is substantially higher. Those requirements can be stated as nine premises, but grouping them by warrant makes clear which philosophical job each premise is doing. The informal formulation compresses nearly all of this work into a sentence such as:

> Most conceivable psychophysical mappings would be disharmonious, while God would have reason to create harmonious ones.

Each phrase conceals a separate burden. “Conceivable mappings” must become a well-defined and relevant possibility space. “Most” must become a justified probability measure rather than a count over a convenient representation. “Disharmonious” must pick out an independently privileged property rather than a category selected after inspecting the actual world. “Would have reason” must become a theory of divine action strong enough to support a comparative likelihood. And the resulting probabilities must be evaluated relative to the same background information. The problem is therefore not simply that the argument lacks numerical precision. Bayesian arguments do not always require exact numbers; in favorable cases a qualitative inequality may be enough. But even the qualitative claim

$$
P(H\mid T,B)>P(H\mid N,B)
$$

requires bridge principles on both sides. On the naturalistic side, one needs something like:

$$
N+B
\longrightarrow
\text{constraints over psychophysical possibilities}
\longrightarrow
\mu_N.
$$

On the theistic side, one needs:

$$
T+B
\longrightarrow
\text{divine motivational structure}
\longrightarrow
\mu_T.
$$

Without these bridges, the likelihoods remain insufficiently grounded. Naturalism is not equivalent to a uniform lottery over conceivable psychophysical laws, and theism is not equivalent to a divine decision rule that maximizes psychophysical harmony. A properly specified comparison should therefore satisfy a **symmetry requirement**: the same standards of probabilistic specification should be applied to both hypotheses. If naturalism is criticized because it does not uniquely determine the psychophysical mapping, then bare theism should likewise be criticized if it does not uniquely determine God's creative intentions. If naturalism must justify a measure over psychophysical possibilities, then theism must justify a measure over divine choices. If auxiliary assumptions are permitted on the theistic side, comparable auxiliary assumptions must be permitted on the naturalistic side. Otherwise the comparison risks becoming artificially favorable to one hypothesis. This can be represented as a comparison between sufficiently specified theories:

$$
N+\alpha_N+B
$$

and

$$
T+\alpha_T+B,
$$

where \(\alpha_N\) and \(\alpha_T\) are the auxiliary assumptions required to generate predictions. The relevant comparison is not simply between the labels *naturalism* and *theism*, but between theories rich enough to assign expectations to the evidence.

This also explains why the argument cannot be rescued merely by insisting that harmony is unexplained under naturalism. Lack of explanation and low probability are different claims. A naturalist may admit that no deeper explanation is currently available for why certain psychophysical relations obtain without thereby conceding that

$$
P(H\mid N,B)\ll1.
$$

One may lack an explanation for a fact without possessing a theory on which the fact was antecedently improbable. The converse distinction matters as well: explanatory availability does not automatically imply probabilistic prediction. A theist may be able to tell a plausible story about why God would value harmony without thereby establishing that \(P(H\mid T,B)\) is high. In compact form:

| Explanatory claim | Probabilistic claim it does **not** by itself establish |
| --- | --- |
| Naturalism does not explain why \(H\) obtains. | \(P(H\mid N,B)\) is low. |
| Theism can explain why a good God might value \(H\). | \(P(H\mid T,B)\) is high. |
| Many disharmonious mappings can be described. | Naturalism gives those mappings substantial or equal probability. |
| Harmony is valuable or fortunate. | Harmony was antecedently improbable, or God would probably choose it. |

The distinction is easy to lose because explanatory and probabilistic vocabulary often travel together. We say that a theory “expects” an observation, that an outcome is “natural” under a hypothesis, or that an event would be “surprising” otherwise. But those expressions should not substitute for the inferential work required to establish comparative likelihoods.

The cumulative criticism is consequently narrower than a denial of psychophysical harmony. The critic need not produce a complete naturalistic explanation for every psychophysical fact. It is enough to ask whether the argument has supplied the warrants required for its own inference. A failure to explain why psychophysical relations obtain would certainly be an interesting problem for naturalism, but an argument for theism requires the additional claim that the evidence is more probable under the competing hypothesis. If either side of the likelihood comparison remains epistemically underdetermined, the intended Bayesian argument has not yet succeeded. Once the requirements are made explicit, the guiding question changes. Instead of beginning with:

> Why is psychophysical reality so harmonious?

we must first ask:

> Have we established that there is a probabilistically anomalous harmony here in the first place?

That is the question the conclusion must answer.

# VIII. Conclusion: From Harmony to Probability Without a Warrant

Psychophysical harmony is initially compelling because it invites us to view familiar features of conscious life from an unusual angle. Pain accompanies injury and motivates avoidance; pleasure tends to accompany approach; phenomenal judgments are often accurate; and conscious experience seems coordinated with behavior, cognition, and physical organization in ways that are useful, intelligible, and normatively appropriate. Once those relations are represented as a mapping between independently specified physical and phenomenal domains, the actual arrangement can begin to look unusually fortunate. Countless alternative assignments can apparently be described, most seem bizarre or maladaptive, and it becomes tempting to infer that the actual mapping was improbable under naturalism but expected under theism.

The central argument of this essay has been that this progression is not automatic. It depends upon four inferential warrants. The first is conceptual: before asking why psychophysical states are harmoniously related, one must establish that *harmony* identifies an independently significant feature over and above the ordinary relations among experience, behavior, judgment, physiology, and practical life. The issue is not resolved merely by labeling phenomenal and functional states independently and then treating their fit as a further fact; the argument owes an account of how those states are individuated and why their relation constitutes the relevant explanandum.

The second warrant is modal. Representing psychophysical relations by a function \(M:P\rightarrow\Phi\) makes alternative functions mathematically easy to define, but formal permutation is not yet metaphysical possibility. Nor does the ability to formulate or imagine a different assignment establish that the assignment is semantically coherent, metaphysically possible, compatible with the naturalism being evaluated, or probability-relevant. The formalism can display a space of descriptions without itself establishing that the world could have instantiated every member of that space.

The third warrant is probabilistic. Even if a large class of alternative mappings is granted, the argument still needs a measure. A small ratio of harmonious descriptions to all formally generated descriptions is not yet \(P(H\mid N,B)\). Naturalism does not by itself impose a uniform lottery over imaginable psychophysical laws, ignorance does not entail equal weighting, and the apparent rarity of harmony can vary with the partition or parameterization of the space. Moreover, the property being measured must itself be independently privileged. If “harmony” is selected after inspecting the actual psychophysical arrangement, then a small harmonious subset may reflect explanatory gerrymandering rather than antecedent improbability. Related manifestations of harmony must also be treated with their dependence structure intact rather than automatically counted as independent pieces of evidence.

The fourth warrant is comparative. Granting, for the sake of argument, that \(P(H\mid N,B)\) is low still does not establish a design inference. The argument needs a substantially higher \(P(H\mid T,B)\). Yet the claim that harmony is good, or that a perfectly good God would have some reason to favor it, does not by itself determine how likely God is to actualize it. Prediction requires assumptions about divine priorities, the ranking of competing goods, and the relation between reasons and choices. Enriching theism with the stipulation that God strongly prefers harmony can raise the likelihood, but it risks fitting the hypothesis to the observation and invites structurally parallel hypotheses involving simulators, finite designers, teleological laws, or axiarchic principles. Even if harmony favors purposive explanation over some forms of brute naturalism, further argument is required to show that it distinctively favors classical theism.

The gastro-phenomenological parody was intended to stress-test this structure rather than to trivialize consciousness. Ordinary bodily regulation can be redescribed using the same vocabulary of physical states, phenomenal signals, appropriate behavior, bizarre alternative mappings, apparent rarity, and benevolent design. If that procedure generates “striking harmonies” too easily, the method needs principled constraints explaining why the original cases are evidentially privileged. The parody therefore reinforces the general lesson: the philosophical work lies in the warrants connecting the stages of the argument, not in the ease with which a fortunate-looking mapping can be described.

None of this shows that psychophysical harmony is meaningless, that phenomenal inversion is impossible, that consciousness poses no explanatory challenge, or that theism could not participate in a satisfactory explanation. There may be genuine questions about why conscious states exist, why they possess the characters they do, why pain is systematically related to avoidance, and why introspective judgments are as reliable as they are. Naturalistic theories may owe substantive accounts of those facts. But identifying an explanatory question is not yet establishing a Bayesian design argument.

The methodological danger is that the language of the argument can perform work that the inference itself has not yet earned. A correlation becomes a *harmony*; a useful relation becomes *fortunate*; a formally available alternative becomes *possible*; a large collection of alternatives becomes *improbability*; improbability becomes *surprise*; and a plausible divine reason becomes a *high theistic likelihood*. None of these words is illegitimate. The problem is that each tends to appear exactly where an inferential bridge is required.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore not that psychophysical harmony has been disproved. It is that its apparent evidential force currently outruns the warrants supplied for the central likelihood comparison. Before asking why the universe contains such an astonishing harmony, a more basic question comes first:

$$
\boxed{\text{Have we shown that there is anything probabilistically astonishing here at all?}}
$$
