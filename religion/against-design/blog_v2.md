## I. Taking the Design Analogy Seriously

Arguments from design begin with a familiar intuition: certain features of the world appear ordered, structured, functional, or finely arranged, and these features are said to be more readily explained by intelligence than by unguided processes. The basic inference is often presented by analogy with ordinary artifacts. We infer that a watch has a watchmaker, that a building has an architect, or that a machine has an engineer because their organization bears the marks of intentional construction. The design argument proposes that we extend this pattern of reasoning to nature itself. If complex systems within the world display organization, apparent purpose, or highly constrained structure, then perhaps the world, or some important part of it, should likewise be understood as the product of a designer.

In an earlier argument, I criticized one feature of this reasoning by focusing on divine psychology. Claims about what a divine designer would create often rely on assumptions about what such a being would value, desire, prefer, or intend. We are told, for example, that a rational God would desire intelligent life, that a good God would value moral agents, or that a divine intellect would prefer elegant and comprehensible laws of nature. But such claims already contain substantive psychological assumptions about a being whose psychology is otherwise largely unknown. The design argument therefore risks using an assumed theory of divine motivation to establish the very designer whose preferences are supposed to explain the evidence.

That problem becomes even more pronounced when the design analogy is examined from the perspective of systems engineering. If the world is to be treated as an engineered system, then it is worth asking how engineered systems are actually understood. Engineering does not normally begin with an implementation and immediately infer a designer merely from the fact that the implementation is complicated. A mature systems-engineering process begins much earlier. One identifies a need or capability gap, determines who the relevant stakeholders are, develops an operational concept, derives requirements, identifies constraints, evaluates competing architectures, and only then converges toward an implementation. Schematically, the forward process looks something like:

$$
\text{Needs}
\rightarrow
\text{Stakeholders}
\rightarrow
\text{CONOPS}
\rightarrow
\text{Requirements}
\rightarrow
\text{Architecture}
\rightarrow
\text{Implementation}.
$$

The importance of this sequence is not merely procedural. Each stage constrains the stages that follow. An architecture can only be evaluated as successful or unsuccessful relative to some set of objectives, requirements, operating conditions, stakeholder priorities, and design constraints. A solution is not simply "well designed" in the abstract. It is well designed for some purpose, under some constraints, for some set of users or stakeholders.

The theological design argument attempts to move in the opposite direction. We begin with the implementation—the observed universe, biological organisms, physical constants, consciousness, or some other feature of reality—and attempt to reason backward toward an otherwise unobserved designer:

$$
\text{Observed system}
\rightarrow
\text{Design objectives}
\rightarrow
\text{Designer}.
$$

But this is an inverse problem. And inverse problems are generally much more difficult than forward ones. The same implemented system may be compatible with many different requirements, and the same requirement may be satisfied by many different architectures. If one only observes the final system, the intentions and constraints that produced it are often radically underdetermined. In ordinary engineering contexts, we overcome this difficulty by relying on a large body of background knowledge about the kinds of agents involved, the needs they typically possess, the constraints they face, the purposes of familiar artifacts, and the range of alternative designs available to them.

That background knowledge is precisely what becomes uncertain in the divine case.

This also reveals why probabilistic reformulations of the design argument do not automatically solve the problem. Contemporary versions are often expressed in Bayesian terms. Rather than claiming that order deductively proves a designer, one claims that some observation $E$ is more probable if God exists than if God does not:

$$
P(E\mid G) > P(E\mid \neg G).
$$

The language is more modest, and the formalism appears more rigorous. But the central question has merely been moved into the likelihood. What model of divine agency allows us to determine what kinds of worlds should be probable conditional on God? What preferences, goals, constraints, or decision procedures generate the quantity $P(E\mid G)$ in the first place?

This is where systems engineering and Bayesian model criticism converge. Systems engineering asks for the missing forward model from needs and objectives to architecture. Bayesian reasoning asks for the corresponding probability distribution over observations conditional on the hypothesis. In both cases, the design argument attempts to solve an inverse problem while leaving the forward-generating structure largely unspecified.

The issue, then, is not simply whether the universe is complex, ordered, or improbable under some alternative. The deeper question is whether the hypothesis of a divine designer is sufficiently specified to generate meaningful expectations about what we should observe. Before asking whether the universe looks like an engineered solution, we must first ask what problem it was supposedly designed to solve—and what grounds we have for believing that a divine engineer would have selected this solution rather than some other one.

The argument that follows proceeds in stages. First, I will ask what makes ordinary design inference possible and why reverse-engineering an observed system is an inverse problem. I will then ask what requirements, objectives, constraints, and stakeholder priorities would be needed to evaluate a universe as a design. From there, I will translate the same problem into Bayesian terms: the missing forward model becomes a missing or underconstrained likelihood. Finally, I will examine the comparison class, the evidence-generation process, and the distinction between formal Bayesian coherence and substantive model justification before returning to the central issue of identification.

## II. What Makes Ordinary Design Inference Work

Consider a much simpler system than the universe: a living room. At first glance, a living room hardly seems like the sort of thing that requires systems engineering. Yet even an ordinary living space is, in a limited sense, a sociotechnical system. It exists within an architectural environment, serves multiple users, incorporates manufactured components, reflects cultural practices, and is organized around a collection of functional and social needs. Its arrangement may depend on everything from human physiology and electrical infrastructure to family structure, entertainment preferences, accessibility requirements, building codes, available space, and cost.

Suppose I enter an unfamiliar living room and observe two couches facing one another, a small television positioned off to the side, lamps beside several seats, electrical outlets near tables, and an open area through the center of the room. I might begin forming hypotheses about why the room was arranged this way. Perhaps the couches face one another because the occupants value face-to-face conversation. Perhaps the television is relatively small and peripheral because television is not intended to dominate the room. Perhaps the open center provides circulation space, accommodates children, or satisfies an accessibility requirement. Perhaps charging ports near the seating areas reflect the expectation that occupants will use electronic devices while socializing.

These are reasonable inferences. But notice how little of the reasoning comes from the arrangement of the furniture by itself.

I already know that humans sit on couches. I know that people converse while facing one another. I know what televisions are used for. I know that lamps provide task or ambient lighting. I know that people charge electronic devices. I know something about the dimensions of human bodies, the importance of circulation space, common patterns of domestic life, and the distinction many cultures draw between private spaces such as bedrooms and semipublic spaces used to receive guests.

Even more importantly, I already know that living rooms are ordinarily designed, furnished, and rearranged by human beings. I have encountered many other examples of them. I know that furniture is manufactured, houses are constructed, electrical systems are installed, and occupants deliberately organize spaces around their activities. My interpretation of this one room therefore takes place within an enormous body of prior knowledge about both the objects I am observing and the kinds of agents responsible for them. The inference can be represented as something like:

$$
\text{Observed arrangement}
+
\text{Background knowledge of humans}
+
\text{Knowledge of artifacts}
+
\text{Knowledge of comparable rooms}
\rightarrow
\text{Hypotheses about design intent}.
$$

This matters because it is easy to focus only on the first term and overlook everything else. When I infer that two couches were positioned to facilitate conversation, I am not extracting the intention "facilitate conversation" directly from the geometry of the couches. I am combining that geometry with a model of human behavior. If the same physical arrangement were encountered in a context involving organisms with radically different perceptual systems, bodies, or social behavior, the inference could change completely. Nor is the inference uniquely determined even within an ordinary human context.

Two couches facing one another could reflect a preference for conversation, but they might also have been arranged that way because of the shape of the room. The location of a fireplace may constrain furniture placement. Electrical outlets may restrict where certain devices can be positioned. The occupants may have copied an arrangement from a catalog. The furniture may simply fit nowhere else. One occupant may value conversation while another cares primarily about symmetry. The current arrangement may even be an accident produced by a previous use of the room.

The same implementation is therefore compatible with many different histories and objectives:

$$
R_1 \rightarrow A
$$

$$
R_2 \rightarrow A
$$

$$
R_3 \rightarrow A,
$$

where different sets of requirements $R_1$, $R_2$, and $R_3$ can all produce the same observed architecture $A$. The reverse is also true. A single requirement can often be satisfied by many different architectures:

$$
R \rightarrow A_1,
$$

$$
R \rightarrow A_2,
$$

$$
R \rightarrow A_3.
$$

If the requirement is simply to provide a space in which several people can comfortably converse, there may be dozens of acceptable furniture arrangements. Nothing about the requirement uniquely specifies one implementation. This many-to-many relationship between requirements and architectures is important. It means that design inference is not merely a matter of inspecting an object and "seeing" its purpose. We are solving an inverse problem in which multiple unseen causes are compatible with the same visible outcome.

In ordinary cases, this inverse problem remains manageable because it is heavily constrained by background information. We know approximately what humans are capable of. We know the kinds of needs they have. We know the physical constraints under which they operate. We know something about economic limitations, available materials, manufacturing practices, social conventions, and typical design processes. We can compare the observed system with thousands of other systems whose purposes and origins are already known.

In effect, ordinary design inference is heavily regularized. If we denote the observed architecture by $A$, the designer by $D$, the designer's objectives by $O$, and relevant constraints by $C$, then what we are really reasoning about is not simply:

$$
P(D\mid A).
$$

Our inference relies on something much richer:

$$
P(D,O,C\mid A,B),
$$

where $B$ represents the enormous body of background knowledge we possess about humans, artifacts, environments, and alternative causal processes. The background term $B$ is doing much of the work.

This becomes especially clear when we imagine removing it. Suppose we encountered an unfamiliar structure on another planet but knew nothing about the possible organisms that lived there, their sensory systems, their bodies, their social behavior, their technology, or even whether the structure had been produced by organisms at all. We might still identify patterns in the object, but inferring what those patterns were *for* would be far more difficult. The less we know about the possible designers and the environment in which they operate, the larger the space of compatible explanations becomes. This is the point at which the analogy between ordinary artifacts and divine design begins to weaken.

In the case of the living room, we possess extensive independent knowledge of the relevant designer class before inspecting the particular object. In the divine case, the object under investigation—the universe itself—is supposed to provide much of our evidence not only for what the designer wanted, but for whether the designer exists in the first place.

We have no independently observed population of divine engineers from which to infer typical design practices. We have no collection of known divine artifacts against which the universe can be compared. We do not possess an empirical distribution of divine preferences, constraints, or objectives. And unlike human engineers, a classical divine being is often described as possessing capacities so unlike ours that even the constraints responsible for familiar engineering tradeoffs may no longer apply. The relevant comparison is therefore not simply:

$$
\text{living room} \leftrightarrow \text{universe}.
$$

It is between two very different inferential situations. In the first, we infer intentions from an artifact using an independently established and richly constrained model of the agents who produce such artifacts. In the second, we attempt to infer both the existence and intentions of an otherwise unobserved agent from the system itself.

The fact that both arguments can be described informally as "inferring a designer from a design" obscures this difference. Ordinary artifact recognition does not show that organized structure, considered in isolation, licenses an inference to intelligence. It shows that organized structure can support such an inference when embedded within substantial prior knowledge about possible designers, their purposes, their constraints, and the processes by which artifacts are produced.

That distinction will become crucial when we turn from the living room to the universe. The central question is not whether features of the universe can be *interpreted* as serving purposes. Given enough freedom in specifying the purpose, almost any architecture can be interpreted that way. The more difficult question is whether those purposes can be inferred independently enough to make the design hypothesis genuinely predictive rather than merely retrospective.

### The Inverse Design Problem

Identifiability provides a useful name for the problem. Roughly, an inverse inference is well identified when importantly different hidden causes or models generate sufficiently different observable implications that the observations can discriminate among them. When many combinations of objectives, constraints, and design choices can generate the same architecture, the inverse problem is weakly identified. The difficulty becomes sharper once the design argument is stated as an inverse problem.

In ordinary engineering, the forward problem begins with some set of needs, objectives, constraints, and requirements and asks what architecture could satisfy them. The inverse problem begins with an architecture and asks what needs, objectives, constraints, or design decisions produced it. The distinction matters because forward and inverse problems are not equally informative. Suppose an engineer is told that a room must accommodate six adults, support conversation, allow wheelchair access, provide task lighting, and remain within a specified budget. Those requirements sharply constrain the range of acceptable designs. The engineer may still have many options, but the direction of explanation is relatively clear:

$$
\text{Objectives}
+
\text{Constraints}
+
\text{Requirements}
\rightarrow
\text{Candidate architectures}.
$$

Now reverse the problem. Suppose instead that we are shown the completed room and asked to reconstruct the original requirements from the final arrangement alone. Immediately, many possibilities emerge. A wide doorway may have been included for wheelchair access. It may instead have been chosen for aesthetic reasons, to move large furniture, to satisfy a building standard, or simply because that doorway already existed before the room was renovated.

A small television may indicate that the occupants place little value on television. Or it may indicate budget constraints. Perhaps the occupants primarily use tablets. Perhaps a larger television was removed. Perhaps the room was designed by one person and later occupied by another.

The observed implementation does not uniquely encode its originating requirements. More generally, let an architecture be represented by $A$, requirements by $R$, constraints by $C$, and objectives by $O$. A forward model might take the form:

$$
A = f(R,C,O).
$$

But observing $A$ does not ordinarily allow us to uniquely recover the inputs:

$$
A \not\Rightarrow (R,C,O).
$$

There may be many combinations such that

$$
f(R_1,C_1,O_1)
=
f(R_2,C_2,O_2)
=
A.
$$

This is a basic form of underdetermination. Multiple hidden causal structures can produce the same observable result.

Human design inference works reasonably well despite this because we can restrict the space of possibilities. Human designers have recognizable capacities and limitations. They need food, space, energy, money, materials, time, information, and labor. They operate under physical law. They inherit technological traditions. Their designs are constrained by manufacturing processes, institutions, regulations, organizational structures, and the expectations of other human beings.

These constraints are epistemically useful. If I know that a spacecraft was designed by human engineers, then facts about mass, fuel, thermal tolerance, communications latency, launch capacity, reliability, and cost make many possible architectures unlikely. Constraints reduce the solution space. This point creates an interesting problem for divine design arguments because the classical divine designer is often characterized precisely by the absence of many such limitations. A human designer may choose a particular solution because stronger materials are unavailable, because energy is scarce, because manufacturing is expensive, because a deadline exists, or because some physical limitation makes alternatives impossible.

But an omnipotent creator is not ordinarily assumed to face such limitations. That means the hypothesis space may become larger rather than smaller.

If God wishes to produce conscious life, for example, why should we expect consciousness to arise through carbon chemistry rather than some radically different substrate? Why through biological evolution rather than direct creation? Why through billions of years of cosmic development rather than immediately? Why in a universe of this size? Why under these physical laws rather than others?

The point is not that any of these choices are incompatible with divine design. It is almost the opposite. Too many architectures may be compatible with divine design. If the designer is sufficiently powerful and the objective sufficiently general, then the mapping from objective to implementation may become extraordinarily non-unique:

$$
O_{\text{conscious life}}
\rightarrow
\{A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots,A_n\}.
$$

And if $n$ is extremely large, observing one architecture among them tells us relatively little unless we have some additional theory explaining why the designer would prefer this architecture in particular. This suggests a counterintuitive principle:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Fewer known constraints on the designer}
\;\Rightarrow\;
\text{weaker inference from architecture to intent}
}
$$

In ordinary engineering, constraints help us reverse-engineer a design. In divine design arguments, omnipotence removes many of those constraints. Unless they are replaced by independently justified facts about divine preferences, values, or decision principles, the inverse problem becomes less identifiable. This is where the issue of divine psychology returns.

Suppose we observe a universe in which intelligent biological organisms eventually emerge. A defender of the design argument may suggest that this is expected because God would desire conscious or rational beings. But that inference introduces an objective:

$$
O_G = \text{produce conscious rational agents}.
$$

Where did that objective come from? It cannot simply be inferred from the fact that conscious rational agents exist, because then the reasoning becomes circular. We observe an outcome, infer that God must have wanted that outcome, and then argue that the outcome is evidence for God because it is what God would have wanted. The structure would be:

$$
E
\rightarrow
\text{God probably values }E
\rightarrow
E\text{ is probable given God}
\rightarrow
E\text{ supports God}.
$$

But the crucial middle premise has itself been reverse-engineered from the observation.

The same problem appears with almost any proposed divine objective. If the universe contains stable laws, one may say that God values order. If it contains complexity, one may say that God values complexity. If it contains autonomy, one may say that God values freedom. If it contains suffering, one may say that suffering serves soul formation, moral growth, free will, or some greater unknown good.

Any one of these explanations might conceivably be true. The problem is methodological: if the designer's objective function is sufficiently flexible, almost any observation can be incorporated after the fact. That severely weakens the evidential value of the observation. A useful way to see this is to compare two kinds of models. Model $M_1$ predicts only a narrow range of outcomes:

$$
P(E\mid M_1)
$$

is high for some observations and low for many others. Model $M_2$, by contrast, can accommodate almost anything because its hidden objectives are adjusted after the observation is known. Then $M_2$ may appear extraordinarily explanatory, but much of that explanatory success comes from flexibility rather than predictive constraint. In engineering language, this resembles writing the requirements document after the system has already been built.

Suppose a contractor delivered an arbitrary system and then claimed that it perfectly satisfied the customer's intentions. If challenged, the contractor simply reconstructed a set of requirements that happened to match whatever the system already did. Such a process would not demonstrate that the architecture was a successful response to independently established needs. It would merely guarantee agreement between the requirements and the implementation by defining the former in terms of the latter. The same danger arises in theological design reasoning.

If we observe life, divine preferences are reconstructed to favor life. If we observe long periods without life, those periods are assigned some divine purpose. If we observe stable natural laws, God values regularity. If we observe apparent exceptions, God has reasons to intervene. If we observe beauty, God values beauty.

If we observe brutality, it is permitted for some higher-order good. Again, none of these responses is logically impossible. The problem is that a hypothesis capable of absorbing almost any observation through unspecified intentions risks losing the very predictive structure required for the observation to count strongly in its favor. This does not show that an unknown designer can never be inferred from an artifact. That would be too strong.

Archaeology provides obvious counterexamples. An archaeologist may reasonably infer that a stone was intentionally shaped even without knowing who shaped it. Similarly, one can imagine detecting an artificial extraterrestrial signal without knowing anything about the individual beings responsible for it. But these cases do not involve inference from structure alone.

An archaeologist possesses independent knowledge of natural fracture processes, human tool production, material properties, and comparative artifacts. A hypothetical SETI inference would likewise depend upon detailed knowledge of astrophysical processes, information-bearing signals, physical constraints, and alternative natural explanations. The identity of the designer may be unknown, but the relevant agent class and competing causal processes remain constrained. The more defensible principle is therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Design inference does not require identifying the designer,}
}
$$

but

$$
\boxed{
\text{it does require a sufficiently constrained model of design.}
}
$$

That distinction is crucial. The theological case is difficult not because the proposed designer is personally anonymous, but because the designer's objectives, preferences, constraints, and design behavior are precisely the variables required to connect the hypothesis to the observation. Without those variables, "God designed the universe" remains compatible with an enormous range of possible universes. And this gives the design argument a problem that will become even more important once Bayesian language is introduced. If a hypothesis does not sufficiently constrain what we should expect to observe, then it becomes difficult to say not merely that the observed world is *compatible* with the hypothesis, but that the world is *especially probable* given the hypothesis.

That distinction between compatibility and probability is where the next stage of the argument begins.

## III. The Requirements Problem: What Is the Universe Supposed to Do?

Once the design argument is viewed through a systems-engineering lens, another problem appears almost immediately: a system cannot be evaluated as well designed without first specifying what the system is supposed to accomplish. This seems obvious in ordinary engineering. A bridge can be evaluated relative to load requirements, environmental conditions, service life, maintenance constraints, cost, safety margins, and expected traffic. A communications system can be evaluated relative to throughput, latency, reliability, interoperability, security, and user needs. A spacecraft architecture can be judged only relative to its mission.

The phrase "well designed" is therefore incomplete unless it is indexed to some objective. Formally, if an architecture is represented by $A$ and a set of objectives or requirements by $R$, then engineering evaluation is not simply a function of the architecture:

$$
Q = Q(A).
$$

It is more properly something like:

$$
Q = Q(A\mid R,C,S),
$$

where $C$ represents constraints and $S$ represents stakeholder priorities. The same architecture can be excellent under one requirement set and poor under another. A room with no windows might be a terrible living room but an excellent photographic darkroom. A vehicle optimized for fuel efficiency may perform poorly if the actual requirement is maximum towing capacity. A highly redundant system may be desirable in aviation and wasteful in a low-cost disposable device. So when someone says that the universe appears "well designed," the immediate engineering question is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Well designed for what?}}
$$

This question is much harder than it first appears. Perhaps the universe is intended to maximize life. But if so, why does the overwhelming majority of observable space appear inhospitable to life? Perhaps the goal is not life in general but intelligent life. Perhaps it is specifically moral agency.

Perhaps the relevant objective is conscious experience, rationality, freedom, stable natural law, aesthetic value, spiritual development, mathematical elegance, or some combination of these. Each candidate objective defines a different design problem. Suppose, for instance, we hypothesize that the principal divine objective is to produce embodied rational agents. We might then write a notional requirement such as:

$$
R_1:
\text{The created system shall support the emergence of embodied rational agents.}
$$

But perhaps the goal also includes stable and intelligible physical laws:

$$
R_2:
\text{The created system shall exhibit regularities discoverable by those agents.}
$$

Perhaps the agents must possess substantial autonomy:

$$
R_3:
\text{The created system shall permit morally significant autonomous action.}
$$

Perhaps overt divine intervention must be limited:

$$
R_4:
\text{The created system shall operate primarily through persistent natural regularities.}
$$

Once requirements like these are specified, particular features of the world may become easier to interpret. Evolution might be defended as a mechanism compatible with autonomous natural development. Stable physical laws might be understood as supporting rational inquiry. A degree of uncertainty might be said to preserve epistemic or moral freedom. But notice what has happened. The explanatory power did not come from the generic proposition:

$$
G = \text{God exists}.
$$

It came from a much richer model:

$$
G^* =
\text{God exists and possesses objectives }R_1,R_2,R_3,R_4,\ldots
$$

The design argument has therefore imported a requirements specification. And that requirements specification is doing substantial explanatory work. This is not necessarily illegitimate. Any serious model requires structure. The problem is that the structure must be justified independently enough to generate real expectations.

If the requirements are inferred from the observed universe itself, then the analysis risks becoming circular. Suppose we observe that the universe contains rational agents. We then infer that God values rational agents. We next claim that rational agents are probable given God because God values rational agents. Finally, we conclude that the existence of rational agents is evidence for God. The reasoning takes the form:

$$
E
\rightarrow
R_G(E)
\rightarrow
P(E\mid G,R_G)
\text{ is high}
\rightarrow
E\text{ supports }G.
$$

But $R_G$ was itself constructed from $E$. The observation is effectively being used to generate the requirement that later makes the observation expected. In ordinary systems engineering, that would reverse the intended order of analysis. Requirements are supposed to constrain the architecture before the architecture is evaluated. If the architecture is observed first and the requirements are then written so that the architecture necessarily satisfies them, the resulting "verification" is nearly vacuous.

This is especially clear if we imagine a design review. Suppose a team presents a finished system and claims that it is optimal. The reviewers ask for the requirements baseline. The team responds:

> We inferred the requirements from the system we already built.

The reviewers ask whether alternative architectures could have satisfied those requirements more effectively. The team responds:

> Any feature of the current architecture reflects some requirement, whether or not we can independently state what that requirement was.

At that point, the claim that the design is successful becomes extremely difficult to test. The same issue appears in theological reasoning whenever observations are retroactively assigned purposes. If we observe regularity, God values order. If we observe randomness, God values openness. If we observe suffering, suffering contributes to growth or freedom.

If we observe hiddenness, hiddenness preserves autonomy. If we observe beauty, beauty reflects divine aesthetic preference. If we observe apparent waste, the waste serves some unknown higher-order objective. Again, any of these explanations might conceivably be correct. The problem is not logical possibility.

The problem is that if every observed feature can be paired with an unstated objective after the fact, then the requirement model ceases to constrain the expected architecture. A model that can accommodate any outcome has very little discriminatory power. This can be expressed in terms of a design objective function. Suppose the designer chooses an architecture $A$ to maximize some utility function:

$$
A^* = \arg\max_A U(A).
$$

If $U$ is known or at least constrained, then observing $A^*$ may tell us something about the designer. But if $U$ is completely unspecified, then for almost any architecture $A_i$ we can construct some utility function $U_i$ for which that architecture is optimal:

$$
A_i = \arg\max_A U_i(A).
$$

The mere fact that some objective function could rationalize the observed design therefore tells us very little. The evidential task is not to show that there exists a conceivable divine objective under which the universe makes sense. It is to show that the relevant objective was independently plausible before observing the features it is being used to explain. Stakeholder analysis produces the same problem in another form.

Complex systems are rarely optimized for only one variable. They typically involve multiple stakeholders whose interests conflict. A transportation system may balance passenger convenience, operator cost, regulator requirements, environmental impact, safety, maintenance, and local community concerns. Improving performance for one stakeholder can worsen it for another. The world, if it is a designed system, appears to contain a similarly vast range of affected beings.

Humans are affected. Other animals are affected. Future organisms are affected. Perhaps ecosystems themselves have value. A theological model may also posit divine interests that are not reducible to creature welfare.

Once multiple stakeholders are introduced, the assessment of whether the system is well designed depends on their relative weighting. Let the total objective be:

$$
U(A)
=
w_1U_1(A)
+
w_2U_2(A)
+
\cdots
+
w_nU_n(A),
$$

where each $U_i$ represents the interests of some stakeholder or objective and each $w_i$ gives its relative importance. Then evaluating the architecture requires knowing the weights. Without them, apparently beneficial or harmful features can be reinterpreted almost indefinitely. Large amounts of animal suffering may look like poor design under one weighting scheme and acceptable under another. Divine hiddenness may be a defect if relationship is strongly weighted and a feature if autonomy is strongly weighted.

Natural regularity may be highly desirable if predictability is important, but less important if direct divine responsiveness is the primary objective. The same universe can therefore be rated very differently depending on the stakeholder model. This is why appeals to "greater goods" are methodologically significant. They effectively introduce latent requirements or stakeholder weights after an apparent design deficiency has been identified. The move may be philosophically defensible in some cases, but it carries a cost: each new hidden objective makes the design model more flexible.

And flexibility reduces predictive constraint unless those objectives are independently justified. This is closely related to the engineering concept of a CONOPS, or Concept of Operations. A CONOPS describes how a system is expected to operate in its intended environment, for whom, and toward what mission objectives. Without something like a CONOPS, an observer can inspect the system's components indefinitely while remaining uncertain about what the system is for. The theological analogue would require answers to questions such as:

* Who or what is the system for?
* What counts as mission success?
* Which outcomes are primary and which are secondary?
* What tradeoffs are acceptable?
* What operational constraints matter?
* What role is the designer expected to play after implementation?
* What conditions would count as system failure?

Without answers to questions of this kind, descriptions such as "fine-tuned," "optimized," or "well designed" risk outrunning the analysis. A design can only be fine-tuned relative to some performance criterion. An architecture can only be optimal relative to some objective function. And a system can only be validated against requirements that are specified independently enough to constrain what counts as success. This is where the systems-engineering critique begins to merge directly with the Bayesian one. If the design advocate wants to claim that a particular observation $E$ is likely under God, then the requirements, objectives, and stakeholder priorities attributed to God must somehow determine the distribution:

$$
P(E\mid G).
$$

In other words, the theological requirements model is not merely an interpretive accessory. It is the structure required to generate the likelihood. Without it, saying that the observed universe is "what we would expect from God" is not yet a probabilistic claim in any substantive sense. It is an intuition waiting for a model.

## IV. From Engineering to Bayes: The Likelihood Is the Forward Model

At this point, a defender of the design argument might object that the preceding analysis demands too much certainty. Design arguments are rarely presented today as deductive demonstrations. The claim is not that the existence of life, consciousness, order, or fine-tuning logically entails a divine designer. Rather, these observations are said to make theism more probable. This is where Bayesian language often enters the discussion.

Let $G$ represent the hypothesis that God exists and let $E$ represent some observed feature of the world. Bayes' theorem gives:

$$
P(G\mid E)
=
\frac{P(E\mid G)P(G)}
{P(E)}.
$$

Or, when comparing two hypotheses, the argument can be expressed in terms of posterior odds:

$$
\frac{P(G\mid E)}
{P(\neg G\mid E)}
=
\frac{P(E\mid G)}
{P(E\mid \neg G)}
\cdot
\frac{P(G)}
{P(\neg G)}.
$$

The quantity

$$
\frac{P(E\mid G)}
{P(E\mid \neg G)}
$$

is the Bayes factor. If the observed evidence is substantially more probable under $G$ than under $\neg G$, then the evidence favors $G$ relative to $\neg G$. There is nothing objectionable about this mathematics. The problem begins earlier.

The difficult question is not how Bayes' theorem manipulates the probabilities once they have been supplied. The difficult question is why the relevant probabilities should have those values in the first place. Suppose an argument is presented as:

$$
P(E\mid G)=0.9
$$

and

$$
P(E\mid\neg G)=0.01.
$$

The arithmetic that follows is trivial. The substantive analysis is hidden inside the numbers. Why is $E$ assigned probability $0.9$ if God exists? What model generates that number? What facts about God constrain the set of possible worlds strongly enough to make $E$ expected?

What assumptions about divine goals, preferences, actions, or decision procedures have been used? And why should those assumptions be accepted independently of the observation that they are being used to explain? These are not secondary questions. They constitute most of the evidential problem.

This distinction is obvious in ordinary Bayesian statistical analysis. Bayes' theorem does not begin the analysis from nowhere. Before a posterior distribution can be interpreted, one ordinarily specifies a model of how the data are generated. In schematic form:

$$
\text{Substantive assumptions}
\rightarrow
\text{Model}
\rightarrow
\text{Likelihood}
\rightarrow
\text{Prior}
\rightarrow
\text{Posterior}.
$$

And even this is an oversimplification. One may also need to specify an observation process, dependence structure, latent variables, measurement error, selection mechanisms, and model uncertainty. The expression

$$
P(E\mid G)
$$

therefore should not be mistaken for a model merely because it has been written in probabilistic notation. It is a quantity that a model would have to justify. This distinction can be put simply:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Bayes' theorem updates a model; it does not construct one.}
}
$$

A person may possess a perfectly coherent system of probabilities and update them perfectly according to Bayes' rule while still beginning from a poor representation of reality. If the hypothesis space is badly specified, the likelihood is unjustified, or important latent structure has been omitted, correct conditionalization does not repair the problem. Bayesian coherence and evidential adequacy are different questions. This is especially important in arguments for divine design because the hypothesis $G$ is often radically underspecified. Consider again the claim:

> The existence of a life-permitting universe is more probable if God exists.

To make that statement substantive, we need more than the proposition that some divine being exists. We need a model connecting divine existence to divine action. Let $\theta$ represent whatever additional properties determine how a divine designer chooses among possible worlds. These might include divine goals, values, preferences, design principles, and intended relationships with created beings. Then a more realistic expression for the likelihood would be something like:

$$
P(E\mid G)
=
\int
P(E\mid G,\theta)
P(\theta\mid G)
\,d\theta.
$$

The variable $\theta$ is doing enormous work. It might include assumptions such as:

$$
\theta =
\{
\text{preference for conscious life},
\text{preference for stable laws},
\text{preference for moral agency},
\text{preference for elegant mathematics},
\ldots
\}.
$$

If we already know that God strongly desires embodied rational agents governed by stable physical laws, then perhaps a life-permitting universe becomes more expected. But that conclusion is conditional on those additional assumptions. The generic proposition

$$
G=\text{God exists}
$$

does not itself tell us how probability mass should be distributed across possible worlds. This reveals the connection with the systems-engineering argument developed earlier. In systems language, we required a mapping from divine needs, objectives, requirements, and constraints to an architecture:

$$
(G,O,R,C)
\rightarrow
A.
$$

In Bayesian language, we require a probability distribution over architectures conditional on those same features:

$$
P(A\mid G,O,R,C).
$$

The likelihood is therefore the probabilistic form of the forward design model. The correspondence can be summarized compactly:

| Systems-engineering concept | Bayesian analogue | Theological question |
|---|---|---|
| Designer or design hypothesis | Hypothesis or model class | What exactly is meant by $G$? |
| Objectives and stakeholder priorities | Latent preferences or parameters | What would the designer value? |
| Requirements and constraints | Restrictions on expected outcomes | What kinds of worlds should those values favor? |
| Architecture or implementation | Possible world or observable structure | What sort of universe is generated? |
| Forward design model | Likelihood or generative model | Why should $P(E\mid G)$ have the claimed value? |
| Reverse engineering | Inverse inference | What does the observed world tell us about the designer? |
| Verification and validation | Predictive or model checking | What observations would count against the model? |

The table is not meant to collapse engineering into statistics. It makes visible the common inferential structure: in both settings, evidence about an observed architecture becomes informative only through a sufficiently constrained account of how candidate causes generate that architecture. If the forward model is missing, the likelihood is missing with it. Writing

$$
P(E\mid G)
$$

does not solve this problem any more than writing

$$
A=f(R,C,O)
$$

would tell us what the function $f$ actually is. The notation labels the quantity we need. It does not derive it. This is particularly important when probabilistic language gives an argument an appearance of precision that exceeds the underlying analysis. Suppose one person says:

> I think the probability of fine-tuning given God is $0.95$.

Another says:

> I think it is $0.75$.

A third says:

> I think it is $0.30$.

Bayes' theorem can operate on all three numbers without difficulty. But the theorem cannot tell us which assignment is justified. The disagreement has simply been pushed upstream into the likelihood model.

This is not unique to theology. Every Bayesian analysis depends on assumptions. Priors, likelihoods, parameterizations, and model classes all require justification. The difference is that in ordinary empirical contexts these assumptions are often constrained by independent information. A model of disease transmission can be constrained by observed epidemiology.

A model of financial time series can be checked against historical data. A reliability model can be informed by failure records. A physical model can generate predictions that are tested against new observations. The divine-design case is unusually difficult because the entity whose decision process is supposed to generate the data is itself the object of inference. We do not first observe divine behavior and then use those observations to build a probabilistic model of divine design.

Instead, the observed universe is simultaneously being used to infer the designer and to infer the designer's preferences. This creates an identification problem. Suppose we observe $E$ and choose $\theta$ because $\theta$ would make $E$ probable:

$$
E
\rightarrow
\theta_E.
$$

We then calculate:

$$
P(E\mid G,\theta_E)
$$

and find, unsurprisingly, that it is high. But this does not provide the same kind of evidential support as independently specifying $\theta$ and then discovering that the predicted observation occurs. The order of inference matters. A model that is sufficiently flexible can often be made to assign high probability to data after those data have already been observed. This is why serious modeling distinguishes explanation from prediction and why model fit alone is not equivalent to evidential success.

The theological analogue is straightforward. If we observe that the universe contains life, we posit that God values life. If we observe consciousness, we posit that God values consciousness. If we observe comprehensible natural laws, we posit that God values intelligibility. Each assumption may make the corresponding observation more probable under the expanded theological model.

But unless those assumptions have support independent of the observations they explain, the resulting likelihood comparison risks becoming retrospective. The design argument can then appear to possess the form:

$$
P(E\mid G) \gg P(E\mid\neg G),
$$

while the substantive reasoning is really closer to:

$$
P(E\mid G,\text{God wants }E)
\gg
P(E\mid\neg G).
$$

That is a very different claim. Once written explicitly, the source of the high likelihood becomes visible. The evidence may not be strongly supporting the bare hypothesis of God at all. It may be supporting a much more specific theological model whose auxiliary assumptions were selected because they fit the evidence. This is why Bayesian notation can obscure as easily as it clarifies.

Probability notation creates the appearance that the central inferential quantities have been disciplined simply because they have been named. But naming a likelihood is not the same thing as specifying a likelihood, and specifying one is not the same thing as justifying it. The appropriate question is therefore not merely:

$$
\text{Does }E\text{ raise }P(G)?
$$

It is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{What independently justified model makes }P(E\mid G)
\text{ different from }P(E\mid H)?
}
$$

for whatever alternative hypothesis $H$ is under consideration. This returns us to the underlying methodological point. Bayesian reasoning is powerful because it forces us to make probabilistic structure explicit. But that advantage disappears if the quantities inside the formalism are merely verbal intuitions translated into mathematical notation. The central difficulty in probabilistic design arguments is not performing the update.

It is constructing the model that makes the update evidentially meaningful.

## V. A Scope Clarification: Bayesian Epistemology Is Not Bayesian Statistics

At this stage, an important objection should be addressed directly. A philosopher using Bayesian language may reasonably respond that the preceding discussion imposes standards borrowed from applied statistics onto a different intellectual project. Bayesian epistemology is not the same thing as fitting a Bayesian model to a dataset. Philosophers are often concerned with questions about rational belief, coherence, confirmation, conditionalization, and how evidence should change credences. They are not necessarily trying to estimate parameters, construct sampling distributions, or run MCMC.

That objection is correct as far as it goes. There is nothing illegitimate about asking questions such as:

* Should rational degrees of belief satisfy the probability axioms?
* Under what conditions should an agent update by conditionalization?
* How should evidence alter comparative confidence in competing hypotheses?
* What constraints should coherence impose on an agent's credences?

Those are legitimate philosophical questions, and they do not require every discussion of probability to look like an applied statistical analysis. The criticism developed here is therefore not:

> Philosophers are using Bayes' theorem incorrectly because they are not doing statistics.

Nor is it:

> A Bayesian argument is invalid unless it includes posterior sampling, convergence diagnostics, or a fully estimable empirical model.

That would be a category mistake. The more precise distinction is between two different claims. The first is formal:

$$
\text{Given }P(H), P(E\mid H), \text{ and }P(E\mid\neg H),
\text{ how should }P(H\mid E)\text{ change?}
$$

Bayes' theorem answers this cleanly. If the relevant quantities are supplied, then:

$$
P(H\mid E)
=
\frac{P(E\mid H)P(H)}
{P(E)}.
$$

There is no controversy here. The second claim is epistemic and substantive:

$$
\text{These particular probability assignments accurately represent
the evidential relationship between }E\text{ and }H.
$$

That claim requires additional justification. Bayes' theorem cannot supply it. This is the central distinction. A formal relationship among probabilities does not by itself tell us whether the probabilities correspond to a defensible representation of the world. One can write:

$$
P(E\mid H)=0.95
$$

and:

$$
P(E\mid\neg H)=0.05,
$$

and the posterior calculation may be perfectly correct. But the theorem is silent about whether $0.95$ and $0.05$ were responsibly assigned. That question belongs to model construction, evidential interpretation, and substantive reasoning. The problem can be stated in a compact way:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Conditionalization is not model justification.}
}
$$

This is true even outside statistics. Suppose two philosophers agree on Bayes' theorem but disagree sharply about whether some observation strongly supports a hypothesis. Their disagreement will often lie not in the rule of updating but in the likelihoods. One believes:

$$
P(E\mid H)\gg P(E\mid\neg H),
$$

while the other denies that asymmetry. At that point, repeatedly invoking Bayes' theorem does not resolve the dispute. The theorem only tells us what follows if the likelihood comparison is granted. The real philosophical work lies in defending that comparison. This is precisely why the critique matters for arguments about God. When someone says:

> Fine-tuning is more likely under theism.

or:

> Consciousness is more expected if God exists.

or:

> Moral experience confirms theism.

the claim is no longer merely about formal rational coherence. It is a substantive statement about how reality would be expected to look under competing hypotheses. That is already a form of model comparison. One need not be doing applied statistics for questions about model specification to arise. If the argument asserts:

$$
P(E\mid G) > P(E\mid H),
$$

then it must explain why the left-hand model assigns more probability to the observation than the right-hand model. This is where the earlier systems-engineering critique becomes relevant again. The quantity:

$$
P(E\mid G)
$$

depends on what kinds of worlds a divine agent would be disposed to create. That, in turn, depends on assumptions about divine objectives, preferences, values, constraints, and decision procedures. A philosopher is free to discuss those assumptions qualitatively rather than numerically. But the epistemic burden does not disappear merely because the analysis is informal. The issue is not whether one has produced a computable likelihood function.

The issue is whether one has supplied a sufficiently constrained account of why the evidence should be expected under the hypothesis. This suggests a useful distinction between three levels of Bayesian reasoning. At the first level is pure probability theory:

$$
P(H\mid E)
\propto
P(E\mid H)P(H).
$$

This is formal. At the second level is epistemic interpretation:

$$
\text{What should these probabilities mean as rational credences?}
$$

This is philosophical. At the third level is model justification:

$$
\text{Why should this hypothesis assign this likelihood to this evidence?}
$$

This is where substantive assumptions about the world enter. The mistake occurs when success at the first level is treated as though it automatically validates the third. A beautifully written application of Bayes' theorem can still contain an unjustified likelihood. The mathematics may be flawless while the evidential model remains weak. This is why numerical precision can sometimes be misleading. Suppose someone assigns:

$$
P(G)=0.2,
$$

$$
P(E\mid G)=0.8,
$$

and:

$$
P(E\mid\neg G)=0.1.
$$

The resulting posterior can be calculated to several decimal places. But the precision of the posterior does not exceed the epistemic precision of the inputs. If the likelihood assignments are little more than intuitions, then the posterior is a precise transformation of imprecise judgments. The arithmetic has not created information. It has only propagated assumptions. This can be represented schematically as:

$$
\text{Weakly justified inputs}
+
\text{exact probability calculus}
\rightarrow
\text{exactly transformed weakly justified inputs}.
$$

That result may still be useful for clarifying how assumptions interact. But it should not be confused with an independently validated probability estimate. This distinction is especially important when Bayesian notation is used rhetorically. Probability symbols can create an impression of rigor because they resemble the language of statistics, decision theory, and formal science. But the notation itself does not certify the evidential structure underneath it. The expression:

$$
P(E\mid G)
$$

may represent a carefully constrained model. Or it may simply mean:

> I find $E$ much less surprising if God exists.

Those are not the same thing. The latter may be a legitimate philosophical judgment. But translating the judgment into probability notation does not automatically make it more rigorous. The real question remains:

> Why should $E$ be less surprising under $G$?

If the answer appeals to divine preferences, then those preferences must be defended. If it appeals to alternative cosmological models, those alternatives must be characterized. If it appeals to multiple pieces of evidence, their dependence must be considered. If it relies on observations selected through some process, that process may matter. None of these questions is answered by Bayes' theorem itself.

This is why the distinction between Bayesian epistemology and Bayesian statistics ultimately strengthens rather than weakens the present critique. The argument does not require every philosophical probability claim to behave like a statistical estimator. It requires only a more modest principle:

$$
\boxed{
\text{If a Bayesian argument makes a substantive claim about evidential support,}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{the likelihood judgments carrying that support must themselves be justified.}
}
$$

That principle should be acceptable regardless of whether one approaches probability as a statistician, philosopher, or decision theorist. It also clarifies the role of the statistical analogy used throughout this discussion. The point of invoking model specification, sampling, dependence, diagnostics, and likelihood construction is not to demand that theology literally become an applied statistical science. It is to expose how much inferential structure can be hidden behind apparently simple probability statements. Statistical practice makes that hidden structure difficult to ignore because every likelihood must come from somewhere.

Philosophical arguments are not exempt from the same conceptual question merely because their probabilities represent credences rather than long-run frequencies or estimated parameters. This leaves the criticism in a narrower but stronger form. Bayesian epistemology is not the target. Bayesian rhetoric is not the target simply because it is informal. The target is the move from:

$$
\text{Given these probability assignments, Bayes' theorem implies this update}
$$

to:

$$
\text{Therefore these probability assignments constitute a rigorous account
of the evidence for God}.
$$

The first statement may be impeccable. The second depends entirely on whether the underlying model has earned the probabilities assigned to it. And that brings us back to the central theme of this essay: the design argument requires a forward model of divine agency before an inverse inference from the observed world can carry substantial evidential weight.

## VI. The Alternative-Model Problem: “Not God” Is Not a Model

The preceding Bayesian discussion has focused primarily on the difficulty of specifying

$$
P(E\mid G).
$$

But the problem does not end there. A Bayesian design argument needs a comparison. To say that some observation supports theism is to say, at minimum, that the observation is more expected under theism than under some relevant alternative. This is typically expressed as:

$$
\frac{P(E\mid G)}{P(E\mid \neg G)} > 1.
$$

Yet the denominator introduces a second modeling problem that is often even less appreciated. The proposition

$$
\neg G
$$

is not itself a generative model. It is simply the negation of one proposition. This distinction is fundamental. Suppose $G$ means something like:

> A divine intelligence intentionally created or designed the universe.

Then $\neg G$ includes every possible state of affairs in which that proposition is false. It may include universes governed by brute physical laws, metaphysically necessary laws, multiverse mechanisms, unknown cosmological processes, impersonal teleological principles, simulation scenarios that do not involve a divine creator, or physical theories not yet conceived. These alternatives need not resemble one another in any substantive way. The complement of a hypothesis is therefore not automatically a coherent explanatory theory. Consider an analogy. Suppose I want to evaluate the hypothesis:

$$
H = \text{The patient has influenza}.
$$

Its negation is:

$$
\neg H = \text{The patient does not have influenza}.
$$

But "not influenza" is not a disease model. It includes COVID-19, bacterial pneumonia, allergies, lung cancer, asthma, common cold viruses, environmental exposure, psychosomatic symptoms, and many other possibilities. If the evidence is a cough, it would be meaningless to ask for

$$
P(\text{cough}\mid \neg H)
$$

without specifying how probability is distributed across these alternatives. A cough may be very likely under one non-influenza diagnosis and very unlikely under another. To calculate the aggregate quantity, we need something like:

$$
P(E\mid \neg H)
=
\sum_i
P(E\mid M_i)
P(M_i\mid\neg H),
$$

where the $M_i$ are mutually relevant alternative models. The same problem arises in arguments about God, except that the alternative model space is vastly larger and much less empirically constrained. If

$$
\mathcal{M}_{\neg G}
=
\{M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_n\}
$$

represents the relevant set of non-theistic explanatory models, then:

$$
P(E\mid\neg G)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
P(E\mid M_i)
P(M_i\mid\neg G).
$$

If the space is continuous or indefinitely large, the expression becomes something more like:

$$
P(E\mid\neg G)
=
\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\neg G}}
P(E\mid M)
\,dP(M\mid\neg G).
$$

Now the difficulty becomes visible. It is not enough to estimate how probable the observation is under each alternative model. We must also specify a probability distribution across the alternatives themselves. Where does that distribution come from? How much prior weight should be assigned to a multiverse model compared with a brute-fact model?

How much to some unknown deeper physical necessity? How much to models in which the apparent constants are not fundamental at all? How much probability should be reserved for theories we have not yet formulated? The simple expression

$$
P(E\mid\neg G)
$$

conceals all of these choices. This is particularly important in fine-tuning arguments. Suppose $E$ is the observation that certain physical parameters fall within a range compatible with complex life. One may argue:

$$
P(E\mid G)
\gg
P(E\mid\neg G).
$$

But what exactly generates the non-theistic probability? If one assumes that each physical constant is independently and uniformly distributed over some broad interval, then a life-permitting region may appear extraordinarily improbable. But why should those constants be treated as independent? Why should the distribution be uniform? Why should the chosen parameterization be privileged?

What determines the relevant range? Would a deeper physical theory constrain the constants? Would changing variables alter what appears "uniform"? Would a multiverse mechanism generate a highly nonuniform distribution over possible constants?

These questions are not peripheral details. They determine the denominator of the likelihood ratio. One cannot infer:

$$
P(E\mid\neg G)\ll 1
$$

merely from the intuition that the observed configuration occupies a small portion of some imagined possibility space. A probability distribution is not determined by a possibility space alone. Suppose a parameter $x$ could, in principle, take values in an interval:

$$
x\in[a,b].
$$

That tells us the support. It does not tell us the probability density:

$$
p(x).
$$

A uniform distribution is one possibility:

$$
p(x)=\frac{1}{b-a},
$$

but infinitely many other distributions are compatible with the same support. The inference

$$
\text{small region of possibility space}
\Rightarrow
\text{low probability}
$$

therefore requires a measure. Without one, geometric smallness and probabilistic improbability are not the same thing. The issue becomes more severe when the "space of possible universes" is itself unclear. What counts as one possible universe? Which parameters are free to vary?

Which laws are fixed? Can the mathematical structure of the laws vary? Can the number of dimensions vary? Are different parameterizations different possibilities or merely different descriptions of the same possibility? Is there a natural measure over such a space?

Without answers, statements about the probability of "a randomly selected universe" may have no well-defined probabilistic interpretation at all. And once again, simply writing the quantity in Bayesian notation does not resolve these questions. This problem also exposes an asymmetry that sometimes appears in theological arguments. Theism may be treated as a highly structured hypothesis containing divine attributes such as omniscience, omnipotence, rationality, goodness, and purpose, while the alternative is compressed into a single undifferentiated category such as "naturalism" or "chance." But these are not symmetrical model classes.

A richly specified hypothesis is being compared to the union of many heterogeneous alternatives. Calling the second category "chance" can obscure this fact even further. Naturalistic explanation is not synonymous with randomness. A deterministic physical law is naturalistic but not random. A metaphysically necessary structure would not be random.

A multiverse model may contain stochastic components but still generate highly structured distributions. A deeper unknown law could make currently free-looking parameters inevitable. Thus the comparison

$$
\text{design}
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\text{chance}
$$

often fails to exhaust the actual hypothesis space. There is another subtle issue here. Suppose the design advocate retreats from numerical probabilities and makes only a qualitative claim:

> Whatever the non-theistic explanation is, a life-permitting universe is surely more surprising without God.

This may be a reasonable intuition to investigate, but the Bayesian language does not strengthen it unless the comparative likelihoods can actually be defended. The formal expression

$$
P(E\mid G) > P(E\mid \neg G)
$$

still requires an argument for both sides. It cannot be established merely by arguing that $E$ makes sense under $G$. Compatibility with one hypothesis tells us nothing by itself about comparative evidential support. An observation can be highly compatible with two competing hypotheses. Indeed, even if

$$
P(E\mid G)
$$

is high, the evidence does not strongly favor $G$ if

$$
P(E\mid H)
$$

is similarly high for a relevant alternative $H$. What matters is comparative predictive performance. This point is easy to miss because explanations are often evaluated rhetorically rather than probabilistically. If a theological story can be constructed under which the observation is unsurprising, the observation may begin to feel like evidence for the theological story. But Bayesian confirmation requires more. For two hypotheses $G$ and $H$, the relevant quantity is:

$$
BF_{G,H}
=
\frac{P(E\mid G)}{P(E\mid H)}.
$$

If both assign similar likelihood to the observation, then the Bayes factor is approximately:

$$
BF_{G,H}\approx 1,
$$

and the observation provides little discriminatory evidence between them. This is why the design argument cannot be rescued simply by showing that the universe is *consistent* with divine purposes. The question is whether the observed features are substantially more expected under a sufficiently specified divine model than under sufficiently specified competing models. That is a much more demanding claim. There is also a practical consequence for how the argument should be framed. Instead of comparing:

$$
G
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\neg G,
$$

a more disciplined analysis would compare particular models:

$$
G_1
\quad\text{versus}\quad
M_1,
$$

or perhaps a collection such as:

$$
\{G_1,G_2,\ldots,G_k\}
$$

against

$$
\{M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_n\}.
$$

Here $G_1$ might represent a specific theological model with specified divine objectives, while $M_1$ might represent a particular cosmological theory. At least then the question

$$
P(E\mid G_1)
\quad\text{versus}\quad
P(E\mid M_1)
$$

has a chance of being well defined. But notice the cost. The argument is no longer a simple comparison between "God" and "no God." It has become a comparison among detailed models whose evidential merits depend on all of their auxiliary assumptions. That is exactly what serious model comparison should look like.

It is also far less rhetorically convenient. The deeper lesson is that hypothesis negation and model specification are different operations. From:

$$
H
$$

we can always formally construct:

$$
\neg H.
$$

But probability theory does not thereby supply a generative account of what happens when $\neg H$ is true. A complement is a logical object. A likelihood requires a probabilistic model. Confusing those two things makes expressions like

$$
P(E\mid\neg G)
$$

look far more informative than they may actually be. And so both halves of the design argument's Bayes factor face parallel difficulties. On the theistic side, we lack a well-constrained model connecting divine psychology and objectives to possible worlds. On the non-theistic side, we face a heterogeneous collection of alternative models over which no obvious probability distribution is supplied. The resulting ratio may therefore be formally writable without being substantively determined:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{P(E\mid G)}
{P(E\mid\neg G)}
}
$$

can be mathematically meaningful as notation while remaining epistemically underconstrained as an actual quantity. This does not prove that no Bayesian comparison between theological and non-theological hypotheses can ever be made. It does show that such a comparison requires far more than labeling one side "God" and the other side "not God."

Both terms require models.

And until those models are supplied, the likelihood ratio is not an analysis. It is a placeholder for one.

## VII. The Evidence-Model Problem: Evidence Is Not Just a Proposition

So far, the criticism has focused primarily on the hypotheses. The proposition that God exists is too underspecified to determine a unique distribution over possible worlds, while its negation combines an enormous range of heterogeneous alternatives. But there is a third part of the Bayesian framework that deserves equal scrutiny: the evidence itself. In philosophical arguments, evidence is often represented by a proposition $E$. For example:

$$
E_1 = \text{The universe is fine-tuned for life},
$$

$$
E_2 = \text{Consciousness exists},
$$

$$
E_3 = \text{Moral experience exists},
$$

or

$$
E_4 = \text{Reports of miracles exist}.
$$

The analysis then asks whether:

$$
P(E_i\mid G)
>
P(E_i\mid\neg G).
$$

There is nothing formally wrong with representing evidence propositionally. The problem arises when the proposition is treated as though it contains everything relevant about how the evidence was obtained. In serious statistical reasoning, the observation process matters. The evidential situation is often not merely:

$$
E.
$$

It is closer to:

$$
E \text{ was observed through process } S,
$$

where $S$ represents some sampling, selection, measurement, or observation mechanism. More generally, what we actually need may be:

$$
P(E,S\mid H),
$$

or a model in which the observation mechanism itself is conditioned upon explicitly. This matters because the same apparent observation can have very different evidential significance depending on how it entered our possession. Consider a simple example. Suppose a researcher specifies in advance that a particular measurement will be treated as extraordinary if it exceeds some threshold. The experiment is performed once, and the threshold is exceeded.

Now compare this with a researcher who examines thousands of variables, time intervals, subgroups, and transformations and eventually discovers one unusually large value. In both cases, the final report may be summarized propositionally as:

$$
E=\text{An extraordinary value was observed}.
$$

But the evidential situations are not remotely equivalent. The second observation was selected from a much larger search process. Its probability must therefore be evaluated relative to that selection mechanism. The general point is:

$$
P(E\mid H)
$$

may not be the relevant quantity if the actual evidence is:

$$
\text{I encountered }E\text{ through procedure }S.
$$

In that case, the evidential model must account for $S$ as well. This becomes important in several arguments for God.

### Anthropic Selection

Fine-tuning arguments provide perhaps the clearest example. We observe a universe compatible with the existence of observers like ourselves. But we could not have observed a universe in which our existence was impossible. Whatever conclusions one ultimately draws from anthropic reasoning, this fact means that the observation mechanism cannot simply be ignored. Let:

$$
L = \text{The universe permits observers},
$$

and let:

$$
O = \text{An observer exists who can make the observation}.
$$

Then our actual evidential situation is not simply the discovery of $L$ from some neutral position outside the universe. It is conditioned on something like:

$$
O.
$$

Consequently, quantities such as:

$$
P(L\mid H)
$$

and

$$
P(L\mid H,O)
$$

need not play the same evidential role. The existence of an observation-selection effect does not by itself refute fine-tuning arguments. But it does mean that treating the datum simply as "the constants are life-permitting" can suppress part of the inferential structure. The question is not merely what universe exists. It is also what kinds of universes could contain observers in a position to report their existence.

Once again, the observation process is part of the model.

### Historical and Testimonial Evidence

The same issue appears in arguments involving miracles or religious testimony. Suppose someone argues:

> We have reports of an extraordinary event, and such reports would be unlikely if the event had not occurred.

The relevant evidence is not merely:

$$
E=\text{A miracle report exists}.
$$

One must consider the process through which that report was generated, transmitted, selected, preserved, and eventually encountered. A more realistic model might contain latent variables for:

* the original event;
* the perceptions of witnesses;
* memory;
* social transmission;
* copying and textual alteration;
* incentives to embellish;
* dependence among witnesses;
* selection for preservation;
* the historical corpus through which the report survived.

The probability of the present-day evidence is therefore not simply a matter of asking whether people would report a miracle if a miracle occurred. It may look more like:

$$
P(E_{\text{present}}
\mid
H,
W,
T,
S),
$$

where $W$ describes witness behavior, $T$ describes transmission, and $S$ describes selection and preservation. This does not mean that historical testimony is worthless. It means that the evidential object must be modeled at the level at which it actually reaches us. A remarkable report encountered after searching centuries of religious literature is probabilistically different from a prespecified observation generated by a controlled experiment. Compressing both situations into the symbol $E$ can conceal that difference.

This was one of the central points in the earlier discussion: statistical Bayesian reasoning forces us to ask how the observation entered the dataset, rather than treating the mere truth of an evidential proposition as the entire inferential event.

### Dependence Between Pieces of Evidence

A second major problem concerns cumulative arguments. Arguments for God are often presented as collections of mutually reinforcing considerations:

* fine-tuning;
* consciousness;
* moral experience;
* the intelligibility of nature;
* religious experience;
* miracle testimony;
* the existence of rational agents.

Each is said to increase the probability of theism. There is nothing wrong with cumulative evidence. But multiple observations can only be combined correctly if their dependence structure is represented. Suppose we have evidence:

$$
E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_n.
$$

The relevant likelihood is:

$$
P(E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_n\mid H).
$$

One cannot generally replace this with:

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(E_i\mid H).
$$

That factorization is valid only under the appropriate conditional independence assumptions. In general, the joint distribution decomposes according to the chain rule:

$$
P(E_1,\ldots,E_n\mid H)
=
P(E_1\mid H)
P(E_2\mid E_1,H)
\cdots
P(E_n\mid E_1,\ldots,E_{n-1},H).
$$

The distinction matters because many pieces of evidence used in cumulative theological arguments may be connected through common latent assumptions. Suppose, for example, that someone argues:

1. consciousness favors God because God would value conscious beings;
2. morality favors God because God would value moral beings;
3. rationality favors God because God would value rational beings;
4. religious awareness favors God because God would desire creatures capable of relating to God.

At first, these may look like four independent confirmations. But perhaps all four depend upon the same underlying theological assumption:

$$
D =
\text{God desires creatures with rich mental and moral capacities}.
$$

Then the observations may be conditionally related through $D$. A simplified structure might look like:

$$
G
\rightarrow
D
\rightarrow
\{E_1,E_2,E_3,E_4\}.
$$

If so, repeatedly treating each observation as though it independently contributes a large Bayes factor can overstate the cumulative evidence. The same underlying assumption may effectively be counted multiple times. This is why dependence is not a technical nuisance. It determines how much genuinely new information each additional observation provides. The earlier dialogue emphasized precisely this point:

$$
P(E_1,\ldots,E_5\mid H)
\neq
\prod_i P(E_i\mid H)
$$

unless the relevant conditional independence assumptions actually hold.

### Evidence Can Be Selected After the Hypothesis

There is another related issue: the evidential set itself may be chosen after the argument has already been constructed. Suppose one begins with the hypothesis that God exists and then surveys the world for features that can be interpreted as expected under that hypothesis. One may identify:

* mathematical elegance;
* consciousness;
* moral intuitions;
* beauty;
* life;
* religious experiences;
* apparently meaningful coincidences.

Each is then offered as evidence. But this procedure raises a model-selection question. How many possible features of the world were considered before the favorable ones were selected? What happened to features that appeared less consistent with the hypothesis? Were the evidential categories specified before observing their apparent theological significance, or constructed afterward?

The problem resembles a general issue in data analysis: searching a large space of possible patterns and then reporting only the successful ones changes the significance of the resulting pattern. The theological analogue does not require literal hypothesis testing to inherit the same conceptual concern. If an evidential category is constructed retrospectively because it fits the hypothesis, then its confirmatory significance should not be treated as though the prediction had been made independently in advance. For example, consider the claim:

> The mathematical intelligibility of the universe is evidence for God.

Perhaps it is. But we should distinguish:

$$
\text{Before examining reality, }G\text{ strongly predicts mathematical intelligibility}
$$

from:

$$
\text{Reality is mathematically intelligible, so we infer that }G\text{ values intelligibility}.
$$

The latter may provide an explanation after the fact. It does not automatically provide the same evidential force as a constrained prediction.

The analogy to overfitting is useful here, provided it is not taken too literally. A model with enough adjustable latent purposes can achieve excellent retrospective fit to the observed world, just as a highly flexible statistical model can fit observed data closely. But fit obtained by adding flexibility after seeing the data is not the same as out-of-sample predictive success. The evidential question is therefore not merely whether a theological story can be made to fit the world, but how much of that fit was earned by constraints that were in place independently of the observations.

### Model Flexibility and Evidential Asymmetry

This also exposes a possible asymmetry in how favorable and unfavorable observations are treated. Suppose orderly features of the world are counted as evidence for divine rationality:

$$
E_{\text{order}}
\Rightarrow
\text{support for }G.
$$

But disorderly or apparently wasteful features are explained by appealing to unknown divine purposes:

$$
E_{\text{disorder}}
\Rightarrow
\text{no significant evidence against }G.
$$

Likewise, beauty may be counted positively while ugliness is neutralized; apparent benevolence may be counted positively while suffering is assigned to unknown greater goods. The resulting evidential procedure becomes asymmetric. Favorable observations are allowed to move the posterior upward, while unfavorable observations are absorbed by additional flexibility in the model. A probabilistic framework should not permit this selectively. If a theological model generates expectations, then observations contrary to those expectations must also be capable of reducing its probability.

The same likelihood function that allows evidence to confirm a hypothesis must permit evidence to disconfirm it. If no conceivable observation can appreciably lower:

$$
P(G\mid E),
$$

then the hypothesis may possess enormous explanatory flexibility but very weak empirical discrimination. This is one reason model criticism matters. In applied Bayesian work, obtaining a posterior is not the end of the analysis. One asks whether the model can reproduce important features of the observed data, whether conclusions are sensitive to assumptions, whether predictions generalize, and whether systematic discrepancies reveal misspecification. That suggests an illuminating question for theological Bayesianism:

$$
\boxed{
\text{What is the theological analogue of a posterior predictive check?}
}
$$

Suppose we specify a theological model $M_G$. That model should imply some distribution over possible observations:

$$
\tilde{E}\sim P(E\mid M_G).
$$

We can then ask whether the world we actually observe resembles the kinds of worlds the model claims to generate. Does the model anticipate:

* the quantity and distribution of suffering;
* divine hiddenness;
* widespread religious disagreement;
* the history of biological evolution;
* long periods of cosmic history without life;
* stable natural laws;
* the apparent rarity of overt intervention;
* the mixture of beauty and destruction found in nature?

The point is not that any one of these observations refutes theism. The point is methodological. A model should confront the entire evidential pattern, not merely the subset most easily interpreted in its favor. If every surprising feature can be accommodated by adding another hidden divine objective, then the model may achieve excellent retrospective fit while possessing little predictive discipline. That returns us to a distinction made earlier:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Probabilistic coherence}
\neq
\text{good evidential reasoning}.
}
$$

Bayes' theorem can correctly update whatever probabilities are supplied to it. But the quality of the resulting inference depends on what counts as evidence, how that evidence was selected, how observations depend upon one another, what observation mechanism generated them, and whether the model is genuinely constrained by what it encounters. This is why simply writing:

$$
P(E\mid G)
$$

can hide more than it reveals. The symbol $E$ may compress an entire evidential pipeline:

$$
\text{World}
\rightarrow
\text{Observation process}
\rightarrow
\text{Selection}
\rightarrow
\text{Measurement}
\rightarrow
\text{Transmission}
\rightarrow
\text{Evidence available to us}.
$$

A serious probabilistic argument must ask where in that pipeline the evidence came from and what assumptions are required to connect it to the hypothesis. The question is therefore not merely whether some feature of reality can be described as evidence for God. It is whether the process by which that feature became evidence has been modeled carefully enough for the likelihood comparison to mean what the argument claims it means.

## VIII. Synthesis: The Missing Forward Model

The systems-engineering critique and the Bayesian critique may initially appear to be two separate objections. One concerns requirements, stakeholders, constraints, and architecture. The other concerns priors, likelihoods, evidence, and model specification. But they are ultimately pointing to the same missing object. Both require a forward model. From a systems-engineering perspective, the forward model explains how a designer's goals and constraints produce an implementation:

$$
\text{Designer}
\rightarrow
\text{Objectives}
\rightarrow
\text{Requirements}
\rightarrow
\text{Architecture}.
$$

From a Bayesian perspective, the forward model determines how probable an observed architecture should be under a hypothesis:

$$
\text{Hypothesis}
\rightarrow
\text{Generative structure}
\rightarrow
P(E\mid H).
$$

These are not merely analogous. They are two descriptions of the same inferential requirement. A likelihood is, in effect, a probabilistic forward model. To say that:

$$
P(E\mid G)
$$

is high is to say that a sufficiently specified model of divine agency tends to generate worlds containing $E$. That claim cannot be separated from assumptions about what the divine agent is trying to accomplish and how it chooses among possible implementations. The earlier systems formulation might be written as:

$$
A = f(G,O,R,C),
$$

where:

* $G$ represents the designer;
* $O$ represents objectives;
* $R$ represents requirements;
* $C$ represents constraints;
* $A$ represents the resulting architecture.

A probabilistic version would replace the deterministic mapping with a distribution:

$$
P(A\mid G,O,R,C).
$$

The second expression is simply the first model generalized to allow uncertainty. This is the central connection. If the design advocate cannot specify, or at least sufficiently constrain, the variables $O$, $R$, and $C$, then the quantity:

$$
P(A\mid G)
$$

is correspondingly underdetermined. More explicitly:

$$
P(A\mid G)
=
\int
P(A\mid G,O,R,C)
P(O,R,C\mid G)
\,dO\,dR\,dC.
$$

This expression makes the hidden structure visible. The probability of observing a particular universe given God depends on a distribution over divine objectives, requirements, and constraints. If those quantities are unknown, then the likelihood is not something that can simply be read off from the proposition that God exists. The systems-engineering problem therefore becomes the Bayesian problem. And the Bayesian problem reveals exactly why the systems-engineering problem matters. Without a forward model, the inverse inference:

$$
A
\rightarrow
G
$$

is weakly identified. This gives a more general form to the argument developed throughout this essay. Ordinary design inference succeeds because the forward model is already heavily constrained. When we infer that a room was designed for conversation, we know a great deal about:

* human bodies;
* human social behavior;
* furniture;
* architectural constraints;
* cultural conventions;
* manufacturing;
* cost;
* typical room usage.

These facts constrain:

$$
P(A\mid D),
$$

where $D$ is a human-designer hypothesis. They narrow the space of plausible designs. They tell us what sorts of implementations humans are likely to produce under familiar needs and limitations. In the divine case, many of those constraints disappear. We do not have an empirical theory of divine design behavior.

We do not know the relevant objective function. We do not know how divine preferences should be distributed. We do not know whether familiar engineering tradeoffs apply. And classical omnipotence appears to remove many physical constraints that ordinarily help narrow a design space. As a result, the mapping:

$$
G
\rightarrow
A
$$

may be extraordinarily diffuse. This produces an important reversal of the usual design intuition. The design argument often treats the extraordinary capabilities of God as an explanatory advantage. A sufficiently powerful designer can explain any level of complexity. But from the perspective of model identification, unlimited capability can be a liability.

The more architectures a designer is capable of producing, the less any particular architecture follows merely from the designer's existence. Suppose a human engineer is limited to a relatively narrow feasible design space:

$$
\mathcal{A}_{H}
=
\{A_1,\ldots,A_{100}\}.
$$

Now suppose an omnipotent designer can instantiate an effectively unconstrained space:

$$
\mathcal{A}_{G}
=
\{A_1,A_2,\ldots\}.
$$

Capability alone does not tell us which element of $\mathcal{A}_{G}$ should receive high probability. To obtain predictive content, we need preferences or decision rules that concentrate probability mass. In probabilistic terms, omnipotence determines something like the support of the distribution:

$$
A\in\mathcal{A}_{G},
$$

but it does not determine the density:

$$
P(A\mid G).
$$

That distinction is crucial. The fact that God *could* create a life-permitting universe does not imply that such a universe is highly probable given God.

Possibility is not probability.

To move from one to the other, one needs a theory of divine choice.

### The strongest reply: constrain the designer independently

A defender of the design argument has an important response available at this point. The objectives attributed to God need not be inferred from the universe alone. They might instead be constrained by independent theological arguments about perfect goodness, rationality, love, simplicity, or other divine attributes. If those considerations genuinely narrow the class of worlds that a divine agent would choose, then they supply exactly the sort of forward-model structure the present critique says is required. The question is therefore not whether theology may add structure to the divine hypothesis; it must. The question is whether that structure is independently justified and sufficiently constraining to support the likelihood comparisons being claimed.

This is why appeals to divine attributes often enter design arguments. One might say that God is perfectly good, perfectly rational, or perfectly loving, and therefore would choose to create beings capable of consciousness, morality, or relationship. But this move does not eliminate the need for a forward model. It supplies additional structure to it. The hypothesis becomes something like:

$$
G^*
=
\{
\text{omnipotent},
\text{omniscient},
\text{perfectly good},
\text{values conscious moral beings},
\ldots
\}.
$$

Now the question is whether those added properties genuinely constrain the distribution over possible worlds. Does perfect goodness imply the creation of conscious beings? Does it imply biological life? Does it imply embodied agents? Does it imply a law-governed cosmos?

Does it imply this amount of suffering? Does it imply divine hiddenness? Does it imply one world rather than many? These are no longer peripheral theological questions. They determine the likelihood.

This is why the argument cannot indefinitely separate natural theology from theology proper. The moment one asks:

$$
P(E\mid G),
$$

one needs enough content in $G$ to produce expectations about $E$. A bare designer may be too weakly specified to do that. A richly specified designer may generate stronger predictions, but the additional theological commitments must then carry their own justificatory burden. And once those commitments generate predictions, they become vulnerable to evidence in both directions. This produces a useful tension. A highly flexible divine model can accommodate almost any possible world:

$$
P(E\mid G) > 0
$$

for an enormous range of $E$. Such a model is difficult to falsify. But precisely because it accommodates so much, it may provide weak discriminatory evidence for any particular observation. A more constrained divine model may produce stronger likelihood ratios. But then it also becomes easier for observations to count against it.

This is not a defect unique to theology. It is a general property of explanatory models. Predictive power comes from constraint. A model explains more strongly when it rules out more.

A related test is robustness. Exact numerical likelihoods may be unavailable, especially in philosophical applications, but the evidential ordering should not depend entirely on one fragile choice of divine objective, comparison class, prior weighting, or observation model. If modest and independently plausible changes to those assumptions reverse the claimed Bayes factor, then the evidential conclusion is correspondingly fragile. Sensitivity analysis, understood conceptually rather than necessarily computationally, is therefore another way to ask whether the result is being driven by the evidence or by discretionary modeling choices. That point can be expressed informally as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{The ability to accommodate an observation is not the same as predicting it.}
}
$$

The distinction is especially important in design arguments because retrospective interpretation is so easy. Given an observed architecture, one can often invent an objective that would make it appear sensible. Given a universe with stable laws, one can attribute a preference for order. Given one with evolution, one can attribute a preference for developmental processes. Given hiddenness, one can attribute a preference for epistemic distance.

Given suffering, one can attribute a preference for freedom, soul-making, or goods inaccessible to us. Again, these proposals may be true. But the evidential question is whether they were independently constrained enough to make the observations expected before they were used to explain them. The engineering and Bayesian formulations therefore converge on a common principle:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Inverse inference is only as strong as the forward model that constrains it.}
}
$$

This principle is familiar across many domains. In medical diagnosis, symptoms are informative because we possess models of how diseases generate symptoms. In astronomy, observations are informative because physical theories generate expectations about what telescopes should detect. In engineering failure analysis, damage patterns are informative because we understand how materials fail under different loads. In each case, the inverse inference depends on a body of forward causal knowledge.

The observed effect becomes evidence because competing causes predict it differently. Design arguments require the same structure. To infer a divine designer from the world, we need some principled account of how divine design differs from alternative world-generating processes. That account must do more than say that the observed world is compatible with God. It must show that the world is comparatively expected under the divine model.

And that expectation must come from somewhere. Systems engineering names the missing content in terms of objectives, requirements, stakeholders, constraints, and architecture. Bayesian analysis names it in terms of a generative likelihood. The terminology is different, but the epistemic demand is the same. Before we can reason backward from the universe to a designer, we need a sufficiently constrained account of what that designer would be expected to produce.

Without that, the inverse problem remains underdetermined. And the probability notation does not repair the underdetermination. It merely gives it a symbol.

## IX. Conclusion: The Problem Is Identification

The design argument is often presented as though the central question were whether the universe contains complexity, order, apparent purpose, or improbable structure. But those features are only the beginning of the evidential problem. The deeper issue is whether we possess a sufficiently constrained model connecting a proposed designer to the kind of world we actually observe.

From a systems-engineering perspective, the difficulty is straightforward. A design is ordinarily evaluated relative to needs, stakeholders, operational objectives, requirements, constraints, and tradeoffs. The architecture is the downstream result of that structure. The theological design argument attempts to reason in the opposite direction:

$$
\text{Observed universe}
\rightarrow
\text{Design intent}
\rightarrow
\text{Designer}.
$$

That is an inverse problem. And inverse problems become difficult when many different hidden causes are compatible with the same observed result.

In familiar human contexts, we manage this problem by relying on extensive background knowledge. We know what human beings need. We know what kinds of constraints they face. We know how artifacts are manufactured. We know what similar systems are used for. We can compare one design with many others whose origins and purposes are independently understood.

In the divine case, much of that information is missing. We do not have an independently established theory of divine preferences. We do not know the relevant design requirements. We do not know the stakeholder priorities. We do not know what tradeoffs apply.

And if the designer is assumed to be omnipotent, many of the constraints that ordinarily help us infer intent disappear altogether. This creates a substantial underdetermination problem. The observed universe may be compatible with divine design.

But compatibility is cheap.

A hypothesis becomes evidentially powerful when it makes some observations more expected than others. That is exactly why Bayesian reformulations do not automatically resolve the difficulty. The key quantity:

$$
P(E\mid G)
$$

does not derive itself from Bayes' theorem. It requires a model. To assign a high likelihood to an observed feature of the universe under God, one needs some account of what God would be disposed to create. That account will depend on assumptions about divine values, goals, intentions, decision procedures, and perhaps constraints. In other words, the likelihood depends on precisely the divine psychology that the design argument is often supposed to help establish.

This creates a methodological danger. If we observe life and then infer that God values life, we can make life probable under the resulting theological model. If we observe consciousness and then infer that God values consciousness, we can make consciousness probable. If we observe mathematical regularity, we can infer a preference for order. If we observe suffering, hiddenness, apparent waste, or long periods without life, additional divine purposes can be introduced to accommodate those observations as well.

None of these explanations is automatically false. But a model that derives its objectives retrospectively from whatever happens to be observed risks explaining too much. The difference between a strong model and a weak one is not merely that the strong model can accommodate the data. It is that the model constrains what data should be expected. This is the point at which systems engineering and Bayesian reasoning converge:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Inverse inference is only as strong as the forward model that supports it.}
}
$$

Systems engineering expresses that forward model in terms of objectives, requirements, constraints, and architecture. Bayesian reasoning expresses it as a likelihood:

$$
P(E\mid H).
$$

If the first is unspecified, the second is underdetermined. And the problem exists on both sides of the comparison. The proposition:

$$
\neg G
$$

is not itself a model either. It is merely the logical complement of $G$. To speak meaningfully of:

$$
P(E\mid\neg G)
$$

requires some distribution over the many possible non-theistic explanatory models contained within that complement. The appearance of a clean Bayes factor:

$$
\frac{P(E\mid G)}
{P(E\mid\neg G)}
$$

can therefore hide enormous uncertainty in both numerator and denominator. The evidence itself also requires care. Observations are not always epistemically equivalent merely because they can be summarized by the same proposition. Selection effects matter. Observation mechanisms matter.

Dependence among pieces of evidence matters. Historical transmission matters. Retrospective selection of favorable evidence matters. A collection of evidential propositions is not automatically a collection of independent likelihood factors. And a model should not be permitted to count favorable observations as confirmation while indefinitely neutralizing unfavorable observations through additional hidden purposes.

None of this entails that Bayesian epistemology is misguided. Nor does it imply that unknown designers can never be inferred. Unknown human agents can sometimes be inferred from artifacts. An unknown technological civilization could, in principle, be inferred from a sufficiently distinctive signal. In such cases, however, the inference is constrained by extensive independent knowledge of materials, physical processes, intelligent agency, and competing causal mechanisms. The relevant principle is therefore not:

$$
\text{You must already know the designer before inferring design.}
$$

It is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{You need a sufficiently constrained model of design before observations can strongly identify a designer.}
}
$$

That is a much narrower claim, but it creates a serious problem for natural-theological design arguments. If the divine hypothesis is kept extremely general, it may be too flexible to strongly predict the observed universe. If it is made sufficiently specific to generate meaningful expectations, then the additional theological assumptions must themselves be justified. And once those assumptions genuinely constrain the model, the evidence must be allowed to count both for and against it. The more predictive the theology becomes, the more vulnerable it becomes to the actual architecture of the world.

That is not an unfair demand. It is what gives evidential inference its force in the first place. The central problem with many probabilistic design arguments is therefore not probability. It is identification. Before asking whether an observation should raise our credence in God, we need to know what distinguishes worlds generated by the divine hypothesis from worlds generated by its competitors.

Before claiming that an architecture is well designed, we need to know the requirements against which it is being evaluated. Before assigning a high value to:

$$
P(E\mid G),
$$

we need to know what model of divine agency generates that likelihood. And before assigning a low value to:

$$
P(E\mid\neg G),
$$

we need to know what non-theistic model space that quantity summarizes. Without those steps, Bayesian notation risks giving mathematical form to an intuition without adding corresponding evidential structure. The result may be probabilistically coherent. It may even be philosophically suggestive. But coherence is not the same thing as identification, and compatibility is not the same thing as prediction.

A universe can always be interpreted after the fact as serving some purpose. The harder question is whether we had principled reason to expect that purpose—and this universe—before looking at the result. That is the question a serious design argument has to answer.
