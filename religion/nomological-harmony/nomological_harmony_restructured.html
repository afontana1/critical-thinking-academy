The Argument Seems Severely Confused

The argument from nomological harmony is a recent theistic argument. It claims that there is something in need of explanation about the way the laws of nature and the actual contents or states of the universe fit together, and that theism explains this fit better than brute naturalism does. I find the argument deeply unpersuasive, but the most useful way to evaluate it is not to dismiss it at the outset. It is to separate the different claims on which its force depends and ask what each one would actually require.

My central contention is that the argument trades on several distinctions that need to be kept apart: laws versus descriptions of laws, formal rules versus physical implementation, compatibility versus prediction, and a genuine unexplained feature of reality versus a mystery produced by a tendentious way of describing reality. Once those distinctions are made explicit, the alleged “harmony” becomes much less obviously surprising, and the further inference from harmony to God becomes much harder to justify.

Central thesis. Nomological harmony appears evidentially powerful only if we first treat laws and world-states as sufficiently independent metaphysical items, regard their apparent fit as improbable under naturalism, and then show that a divine mind makes that fit substantially more expected. Each of those steps requires argument; the familiar rulebook analogy tends to obscure those burdens rather than discharge them.

Part VII — What a Successful Argument Would Need

Conclusion

Part I What the Argument Claims Before criticizing the inference, it helps to state the proposed explanandum as clearly and charitably as possible.

The alleged harmony

The core idea is that the universe is not merely a pile of stuff plus a separate list of laws. The laws we actually formulate apply to the kinds of things and states that actually occur, and when applied to the universe’s states they generate or constrain further states. Cutter and Saad describe the striking fact as a “harmonious match between laws and states.” They also suggest that things might have been otherwise: a universe might have been “stillborn,” with laws that failed to engage its initial state in any productive way.

The argument therefore does two things. First, it identifies what is supposed to be a surprising fact: why do the actual laws “hook into” the actual furniture of the world so neatly? Second, it treats that fact as evidence for God. A divine mind or designer could, on this picture, intentionally match laws to a world, whereas on a non-theistic view the match might appear brute or coincidental. The standard intuition pump is a box containing game pieces and a rulebook. The surprising thing is allegedly not merely that there are pieces and rules, but that these rules are exactly the rules for these pieces. Intentional matching would make that pairing seem less accidental.

The argument in premise form

  1. There is a striking harmony between the actual laws of nature and the actual contents or initial states of the universe.
  2. This harmony calls for explanation.
  3. Theism provides a better explanation of that harmony than unguided naturalism or brute fact.
  4. Therefore, the harmony counts as evidence for God.

How it differs from ordinary fine-tuning

This is related to ordinary fine-tuning arguments, but it is not exactly the same argument. Fine-tuning arguments usually focus on laws, constants, or boundary conditions being life-permitting. Nomological harmony is more basic: it asks why the laws are even apt for the kind of universe that exists at all. The central philosophical pressure point therefore arrives earlier than the usual fine-tuning debate: why assume that a mismatch between laws and states was a genuine live possibility in the first place?

Critics can challenge that assumption by arguing that laws and the entities, powers, structures, or states they govern are not independently selectable in the way the argument requires. If so, the “surprise” may be overstated before theism ever enters the picture. A second and separate problem is that an appeal to God may merely relocate the contingency unless the defender can explain why a God would choose exactly this harmonious arrangement rather than countless alternatives.

My reaction to the argument is much more negative than that of some defenders, one of whom describes it as “ridiculously convincing.” I find that divergence interesting in its own right. At my most polemical, I am tempted to regard the persuasiveness of arguments like this as anthropological evidence disconfirming theism: evidence, at least, that human beings are extraordinarily capable of manufacturing explanatory pressure from conceptual framings that already favor the conclusion. But the essay does not need that polemical judgment to succeed. The argument can be evaluated on its own inferential structure.

Four hurdles the argument must clear

A useful way to organize the rest of the discussion is to separate four questions that are often run together. The argument needs favorable answers to all four.

HurdleQuestionWhy it matters
1. Coherence Are laws and states sufficiently separable for their “fit” to be an additional relation? If the fit is partly constitutive or representational, there may be no independent coincidence to explain.
2. Modality Were mismatched law/state combinations really possible? Surprise requires a relevant contrast class of alternatives, not merely an imagined recombination.
3. Probability If mismatch was possible, was harmony actually improbable on naturalism? Possibility alone does not establish low probability.
4. Comparison Does theism make this harmony substantially more expected? A hypothesis gains evidential support only by outperforming relevant alternatives, not merely by accommodating the observation.

The first two hurdles concern whether the alleged explanandum is even well formed. That is where the argument seems most conceptually unstable.

Part II The Hidden Metaphysics of “Harmony” The argument sounds neutral only if we overlook how much metaphysics is already packed into the idea of independently paired laws and states.

The independence assumption

The argument from nomological harmony tends to present laws and world-states as though they were two independently chosen ingredients that then happen to fit. That is already a substantive assumption. The relevant modal picture seems to be that we can hold the laws fixed while varying the states or entities more or less freely—or hold the states fixed while varying the laws—and then ask why we landed on one of the rare combinations in which the two successfully interact.

But why accept that recombination principle? On many views, laws are not an extra item laid alongside reality like a rulebook beside chess pieces. They are descriptions, systematizations, expressions of powers, modal constraints, or structural relations. On such views, the identities and admissible states of things may already be partly connected to the laws or structures in which they figure. Before one can assign surprise to a “match,” the defender must establish that enough independent recombination is metaphysically possible to generate a meaningful space of mismatches.

The key modal question is not merely: “Can I verbally describe laws L together with state S?” It is: “Does the relevant metaphysics actually license L and S as independently variable possibilities, and if so, with what measure or probability?”

What are laws of nature?

The argument often gains traction by quietly assuming a governing conception of laws: laws as something like metaphysical rules imposed on an otherwise independent reality. But that is only one family of views. Rival accounts alter the status of the supposed harmony dramatically.

ConceptionWhat laws areEffect on the harmony question
Humean General summaries of the mosaic of particular facts. The law is extracted from the world, so asking why it “fits” the world risks treating a summary as independent of what it summarizes.
Best-system Axioms of the simplest and strongest true systematization. Fit is partly built into what qualifies a system as a true and successful lawlike summary.
Dispositionalist / necessitarian Expressions of powers, essences, necessary connections, or modal structure in things. Law and entity may be internally related rather than externally matched.
Structuralist Mathematically expressible relations or structures instantiated by reality. The key question becomes one about structure and representation, not the pairing of a detached rulebook with independent pieces.
Governing / primitivist Ontologically basic modal governors or constraints. The harmony question has its strongest foothold here, but the governing ontology must itself be defended rather than smuggled in through analogy.

On Humean and best-system pictures especially, “why do the laws fit reality?” is close to malformed, because the laws are extracted from reality as part of our best description or systematization of it. They are not independently floating code that then needs to latch on. On dispositionalist or structuralist accounts, the problem may take a different form, but again the relation between law and world need not be a contingent external pairing. The argument therefore cannot present nomological harmony as a metaphysically neutral datum. Its force varies with one's prior theory of laws.

Map, model, and world

This is where the map/territory problem becomes useful. If laws are even partly representational, the relevant harmony is not like two separately existing objects that happen to coincide. It is more like the unsurprising fact that a well-made map tracks the terrain. The interesting question is not “why is there harmony between map and terrain?” but “how do creatures like us construct maps that capture enough stable structure to be useful?” That is an epistemic question about abstraction, idealization, model selection, measurement, and predictive success. It is not automatically a metaphysical pointer to God.

Scientific models are selectively built to capture stable structure, usually under restricted domains and idealizations. They do not mirror reality wholesale; they vary in fidelity, scope, and interpretation. If the nomological-harmony argument says, “Look how well the laws fit the world,” one answer is that successful laws and models are the polished outputs of a practice that retains representations that work and rejects or revises those that fail. That does not dissolve every realist question about why modeling succeeds at all, but it undercuts the idea that the success of a scientific representation should be re-described as an independent cosmic coincidence.

Semantics is not metaphysics

A Tarskian perspective makes the same point in a different vocabulary. We should distinguish object language from metalanguage, the world from the sentences or formal systems used to describe it, and a theory from the interpretation under which that theory is true of a domain. Once those distinctions are enforced, some formulations of nomological harmony begin to look like category mistakes. They talk as though formal descriptions stood over against the world in the way a legislator stands over citizens.

But from a semantic or formalist angle, a law statement is not a little metaphysical governor. It is a sentence or mathematical structure within a theory, interpreted in relation to a model or to the world. The “harmony” can therefore be, at least in part, an ordinary satisfaction relation between representation and domain inflated into a metaphysical relation between two independently existing things.

The first major objection is therefore not simply “laws are descriptions.” It is stronger: the nomological-harmony argument requires a particular relationship among laws, states, and modal possibility. It cannot assume that relationship and then count the resulting appearance of harmony as neutral evidence.

A defender can respond by abandoning the crude picture of two independently paired objects. The argument then becomes more interesting: perhaps the real mystery is the broader intelligibility of nature.

Part III The Stronger Intelligibility Argument The best reply shifts the discussion from “rules fitting pieces” to the deeper fact that reality admits unified, compressible, mathematically useful description.

A charitable reformulation

A defender might concede that laws are not detachable rulebooks and still argue that something striking remains. Reality is sufficiently unified, stable, compressible, mathematically tractable, and regular that elegant generalization is possible. Why should a world be so amenable to systematic understanding by finite minds? This is a more sophisticated argument than the crude rulebook-and-pieces formulation, and it deserves to be treated separately rather than as a mere diversion.

The difficulty is that intelligibility arguments often take the success conditions of theory and redescribe them as mysterious intrinsic features of reality. We call a theory intelligible, unified, compressible, tractable, elegant, or generalizable because those are precisely the features that make it useful as a theory for finite thinkers like us. When someone asks, “Isn’t it amazing that reality is describable in unified and compressible ways?”, part of the answer is that we deliberately search for, construct, retain, and praise descriptions possessing those virtues.

Epistemic selection

The direction of explanation is therefore easy to reverse. The theistic framing is often presented like this: the world is intelligible; that intelligibility is surprising; therefore intelligence is behind it. But a substantial part of what we call intelligibility is already a relation among the world, our cognitive capacities, and our methods of representation. Our theories are shaped around simplicity, scope, compression, tractability, and inferential manageability. It should not surprise us that the theories that survive our scientific practices disproportionately exhibit the very virtues for which those practices select.

A less mysterious ordering

  • We count as “intelligible” those aspects of the world our cognitive and theoretical practices can successfully regiment.
  • We deliberately build theories around simplicity, scope, compression, prediction, and inferential manageability.
  • We discard, revise, or marginalize representations that fail badly at those tasks.
  • So the intelligibility of successful theories is partly an output of epistemic selection, not simply a brute input supplied by reality.

That does not mean reality contributes nothing. The world must contain enough regularity for some modeling to work at all. But the theistic move often inflates that modest fact—some structures in reality are stable enough to support successful modeling—into the much stronger claim that reality is metaphysically pre-arranged for elegant cognition by minds like ours. The inflation does much of the argumentative work.

Embedded cognition

A natural-language analogy makes the point vivid. Imagine someone saying, “Isn’t it astounding that humans can communicate through implicature, pragmatics, and shared context? Surely this cries out for a transcendent explanation.” The right response need not deny that communication is remarkable. It can instead challenge the thin starting model that generates the mystery. If meanings are pictured as private mental atoms and sentences as bare literal encodings, successful communication can look miraculous. Once language is understood as a socially embedded, inferential, context-sensitive practice shaped by shared forms of life, much of the apparent miracle dissolves.

The intelligibility argument can make an analogous mistake. It begins with a picture on which reality is fundamentally opaque chaos, mind is somehow external to it, and theory is a detached mirror. Then it marvels that mirroring works at all. But we are organisms inside the world. Our cognitive capacities are constrained by the need to track enough structure for action, prediction, and coordination, while our conceptual tools and scientific methods have been historically refined to exploit recurring structure. Once that embeddedness is in view, intelligibility looks less like an inexplicable gift and more like an achievement of situated agents working within a structured world.

The world is not uniformly intelligible

The premise is also easy to overstate. “Reality is understandable” often means that we have managed to build some representations of some domains that are understandable enough for particular purposes. That is not the same as saying the world comes pre-labeled for ideal rational apprehension. Much of science is difficult, counterintuitive, computationally expensive, or resistant to elegant closed-form treatment. Quantum theory, turbulence, strongly nonlinear systems, consciousness, complex biological development, and macroeconomic dynamics are hardly paradigms of transparent intelligibility.

Science often advances precisely by using mathematics, instruments, approximation, simulation, and specialized formalisms to cope with what is not naturally intelligible to unaided human cognition. A world containing some highly compressible regularities alongside enormous local complexity is quite different from a world engineered for effortless comprehension.

What remains to be explained

Still, there is a legitimate residue that should not be waved away. Reality must have enough stable structure for inquiry, representation, and successful prediction to get traction at all. The fact that some domains admit powerful mathematics and generalization may remain philosophically interesting even after selection effects are acknowledged. The mistake is to jump from that modest residual question to the much larger conclusion that an intelligent creator is thereby strongly supported.

The strongest version of the intelligibility argument therefore does not disappear; it becomes more precise. The real question is how much objective regularity the world must possess for bounded, embedded agents to develop successful theories—and whether theism predicts that level and kind of regularity better than its competitors.

Even if we grant a genuine explanandum at this stage, a second major burden remains: why think “God” is a comparatively strong explanation rather than a flexible label that can be attached to almost any outcome?

Part IV From a Mystery to God An unexplained phenomenon is not yet evidence for theism. The comparative inference is where much of the argumentative burden actually lies.

The recurring argument template

Nomological harmony seems to instantiate a portable argumentative template that appears throughout philosophical apologetics. The target phenomenon changes, but the inferential pattern often remains remarkably stable.

  1. Isolate some phenomenon.
  2. Describe it as deeply surprising, brute, or resistant to naturalistic explanation.
  3. Introduce theism as a possible high-level explanation.
  4. Quietly slide from “possible” or “compatible with” to “best” or “actual.”
  5. Reinterpret any later natural explanation as merely the means by which God acted.
  6. When the original gap closes, relocate the mystery one level up.

The complaint is not merely the familiar accusation of “God of the gaps.” It is more specific. The gap can be manufactured by a tendentious metaphysics, exaggerated by an overstatement of what naturalism must predict, and then treated as resolved once theism is verbally associated with the phenomenon. If the original explanatory pressure depends on construing laws as quasi-Platonic prescriptions floating free of the world, for example, theism is not solving a neutral datum. It is cashing out a problem introduced by the argument's own framing.

Explanatory ratcheting

I see the same structure outside technical apologetics. I once heard a pastor call a person's healed bone a miracle. When the biological mechanisms of bone healing were acknowledged, the claim shifted: perhaps the miracle was that bones can heal at all. In another case, a cancer remission was described as miraculous; when medical explanation entered the picture, the miracle became the doctors' ability to know how to treat cancer. The explanatory target had moved upward rather than disappeared.

That migration is the important feature. If one gap closes, the argument can ratchet upward: not this healing, then healing as such; not this remission, then the existence of medical knowledge; not this design feature, then the laws; not the laws, then their intelligibility; not intelligibility, then the very possibility of rational inference. The explanandum is repeatedly redescribed at a higher level of abstraction whenever lower-level accounts improve.

This makes the strategy unusually resilient because it is not committed to any particular empirical gap. The price of that resilience is that the explanation becomes thinner and less discriminating. The more readily an account can be moved to cover whatever explanatory level remains, the harder it becomes to know what observation would count against it.

Are the arguments evidentially independent?

Fine-tuning, psychophysical harmony, moral knowledge, consciousness, rationality, nomological harmony, mathematical applicability, beauty, and related arguments can all be presented as separate evidential lines. But apparent multiplicity does not automatically produce cumulative force. If many arguments depend on the same questionable assumptions—manufactured surprise, an undefined naturalistic probability space, and a very permissive theistic likelihood—then their evidential failures may be correlated.

The precise point is not that “ten arguments are literally one argument.” It is that their evidential contributions cannot simply be multiplied as though they were conditionally independent. If the same inferential weakness recurs across multiple target domains, counting each target as an independent confirmation risks double-counting the shared premise. The multiplicity may therefore be more rhetorical than evidential.

This matters because theism can function as an explanatory universal solvent. If X happens, God intended it; if X is lawlike, God authored the laws; if X is random, God chose to work through indeterminacy; if X receives a natural explanation, God created the natural process; if X lacks a natural explanation, God explains the gap. A framework capable of absorbing every evidential state may still be true, but sheer flexibility is not itself a virtue. It can indicate low discriminatory power.

What “naturalism cannot explain X” may really mean

This is also why the phrase “naturalism cannot explain X” deserves much more scrutiny than it usually receives. Very often the claim actually being supported is weaker: naturalism has not yet fully explained X; there is no consensus explanation of X; current accounts leave residual philosophical questions; or the speaker finds available naturalistic explanations intellectually or existentially unsatisfying. Those are all importantly different from the modal claim that naturalism cannot explain X in principle.

And even the strongest version would still leave the comparative problem untouched. From “naturalism cannot explain X” it does not follow that “theism explains X.” The latter requires an account with its own constraints, expectations, explanatory costs, and reasons for preferring this outcome over relevant alternatives.

Compatibility is not prediction

The crucial distinction is between accommodation and prediction. A hypothesis can be compatible with an observation without making that observation especially likely. Saying “God could have wanted X” establishes, at most, that X can be fitted into a theistic story. It does not yet show that X is more expected on theism than on competing views.

Evidential support requires something like \(P(E\mid T) > P(E\mid N)\), not merely \(P(E\mid T) > 0\).

This point sharpens the objection. A sophisticated defender need not claim that theism predicts only one possible world. Broad theories can permit many outcomes while assigning them different probabilities. The problem is instead that bare theism often arrives without a sufficiently constrained account of divine goals to establish a meaningful likelihood for the observed harmony. The defender must show, rather than merely narrate after the fact, that a harmonious law-governed world of roughly this kind is substantially more expected given God.

The divine-choice problem

Fine-tuning makes the issue especially visible. Theism is often treated as though it reduces arbitrariness, when it may instead relocate or expand it. Replace “Why these physical constants?” with a divine agent and the space of questions does not disappear: Why did God choose these constants? Why this kind of universe? Why embodied biological life? Why create life through cosmological fine-tuning rather than directly? Why this level of suffering, waste, and indirection? Why a law-governed universe at all rather than direct divine sustenance?

Under a bare naturalistic setup, perhaps we are considering some fixed class of laws or constants and asking about their values. Under theism, by contrast, the apparent freedom can become much larger. God could create different laws, different constants, different dimensionalities, no stable matter, minds without biology, life under radically different physical regimes, or a world in which no fine-tuned intermediary is required. If explanatory flexibility is the issue, theism may enlarge the space of possibilities rather than narrow it.

That produces the neglected problem of predictive looseness. Why think this life-permitting, mathematically describable, law-governed structure is especially expected on theism rather than any of the countless other ways an unlimited deity might realize value, minds, order, or moral agency? A good explanation should not merely permit the data; it should make the data more expectable than relevant alternatives do.

In response, theistic explanations often accumulate auxiliary claims: God values embodied creatures, elegant laws, stable causation, discoverability, free processes, gradual emergence, hiddenness, or some particular balance between suffering and moral goods. Any one of these claims might be defensible, but the evidential argument cannot simply add them after the observation and then treat the resulting package as if bare theism had predicted the data all along. The more such preferences are introduced post hoc, the more the explanation risks becoming patchwork.

The actual inference is therefore much stronger than “naturalism struggles with X, and theism can explain X.” It must be: naturalism struggles with X, while a sufficiently specified theism makes X likely enough, specifically enough, and non-arbitrarily enough to count as a superior explanation. That stronger comparative claim is where the burden lies.

With those general distinctions in place, the chess analogy can now be evaluated as a concrete model of the argument rather than as an independent objection.

Part V Why the Chess Analogy Fails The analogy is rhetorically effective because it imports human-authored, prescriptive rules into the case of nature. That is also why it is philosophically dangerous.

Constitutive rules

Chess rules are constitutive norms: they partly create the game and help determine the functional identities of its pieces. A bishop is a bishop partly because of the role it occupies in the rule-governed practice. Physics is not obviously like that. To treat physical laws as analogous to game rules already builds in a heavyweight picture of laws as something like prescriptions imposed upon otherwise independent material.

FeatureChess rulesScientific laws

The chess dilemma

The analogy collapses in one of two directions. If we take chess pieces in their functional sense, then the “fit” between pieces and rules is not surprising at all, because the pieces are partly individuated through the rules. The physical token is incidental: a bishop can be wood, plastic, a mark on paper, a digital icon, or a purely imagined object. What matters is its role in the formal system. In that case, the analogy does not establish a contingent coordination requiring a designer.

If, instead, the analogy insists that the pieces are genuinely independent physical objects that happen to be paired with a rulebook, then the alleged “chess pieces” cease to be chess pieces in the relevant sense. They are merely bits of wood or plastic to which someone later assigns a role. The analogy therefore faces a dilemma: understand the pieces functionally and their fit is internal to the game; understand them independently and the case no longer resembles chess in the way needed by the argument.

The dilemma: if the rules partly constitute the pieces, their fit is not an extra coincidence. If the pieces are independent of the rules, they are not yet chess pieces in the sense required by the analogy.

Equivocation on “rule”

The analogy also trades on an equivocation. In ordinary life, “rule” often means a prescription or stipulation: game rules, legal rules, classroom rules. The analogy then quietly extends that image to laws of nature, as if physical laws were similarly imposed instructions that reality obeys. But in science, laws may instead be inferred from patterns, theory-laden, revisable, representational, or explanatory and predictive summaries. These are not remotely the same concepts simply because the English word “rule” can be used for both.

Once the meanings are blurred, the rhetorical inference becomes easy: just as chess pieces are paired with chess rules by a mind, physical entities are paired with physical laws by a mind. But that is not a discovery. It is a projection of the artifact model onto nature. The intentionality is already present in the source analogy and is then smuggled into the target case.

Scientific laws are also theory-dependent in an important sense. They are articulated within representational frameworks involving idealization, scale, background theory, measurement conventions, and explanatory aims. To ask why the “rules” fit the “entities” can therefore be backwards in something like the way it would be backwards to ask why the contours on a topographic map happen to match the mountain. The map was constructed to represent those contours. Likewise, if a scientific law is in significant part an inferred and model-relative representation, its fit with observed structure is not automatically an independent coincidence.

Even granting the analogy

Suppose, however, that we grant the theist the governing picture and say that God intentionally matched these entities with these rules. That still explains surprisingly little by itself. The explanatory burden is merely displaced into divine psychology and divine choice. Why these rules rather than others? Why this kind of universe? Why deterministic or probabilistic laws in these proportions? Why elegant and discoverable regularities if divine hiddenness matters? Why embodied, law-bound creatures rather than direct divine sustenance?

Because the answers are not supplied by bare theism, the account again tends to acquire auxiliary preferences: God values elegance, stable causation, creaturely freedom, discovery, gradual development, hiddenness, regularity, or some combination of them. That may eventually become a substantive theological model, but until those commitments are independently motivated, “God matched the rules to the entities” largely restates the pairing in intentional language rather than explaining why this particular pairing should have been expected.

Computation makes the same problem even clearer because it forces us to distinguish several very different senses of “rule,” “state,” “representation,” and “implementation.”

Part VI A Computational Perspective Formal systems show why “a rulebook matched to pieces” is too crude a model for the relationship among laws, states, semantics, and physical realization.

“Rule” names many different things

From the standpoint of computation, a rule is not automatically a little command imposed on passive stuff. “Rule” can refer to a transition function in an automaton, an update rule in a cellular automaton, an inference rule in a formal calculus, a production rule in a grammar, an algorithmic procedure, a constraint on admissible states, or simply a compact description of input-output behavior. These are not one unified metaphysical kind. The nomological argument becomes unstable when it slides among them while preserving the intuitive force of ordinary prescriptive rules.

Rules and states can be co-defined

In computation, the relation between rules and the things governed by them is often not one of external matching. Consider a finite automaton: the states, alphabet, and transition function are defined together as a formal system. Asking why the transition rules “happen to fit” those states is confused because the state identities are partly individuated by their place in the transition structure. The pairing is internal to the formalism.

The same point applies to a Turing machine. Its transition table is not an externally imposed lawbook accidentally matched to a pre-existing tape ontology. The machine description specifies a whole computational system. Change the transition table sufficiently and one does not have “the same machine with different rules”; one has a different machine description. Formal states and rules are frequently co-defined rather than independently selected and then marvelously paired.

Syntax is not semantics

A second distinction is between syntax and semantics. Computational rules are usually syntactic: they specify operations on symbols or states. Whether those symbols represent anything is a further question. A formal system can be perfectly well-defined without denoting a physical reality. A rulebook does not magically latch onto the world. Interpretation, encoding, semantics, and implementation all matter.

This is precisely where the analogy to scientific law becomes too simple. Scientific laws are not bare transition tables sitting in a void. They belong to theoretical models that require interpretation, idealization, parameterization, measurement conventions, and domain restrictions. The relevant relation is therefore closer to “formal structure used under an interpretation to model recurring patterns” than to “rules matched to pieces.” Once the relation is described accurately, it is much less obviously mysterious.

Multiple realizability

Computer science also makes the substrate issue explicit. The same abstract computation can be implemented in many different physical media. A chess program can run on silicon, be simulated with relays, be executed with marks on paper, or in principle be carried out by human clerks following a procedure. The abstract organization matters more than the material token.

That observation further weakens the rhetoric of “these rules fitting these pieces.” The material pieces are often irrelevant to the rule structure. What matters is a state space and a pattern of permitted transformations. But once the issue is recast in those terms, the wonder of a rulebook mysteriously matched to a particular set of objects loses much of its grip.

Complexity and tractability

Complexity theory also complicates intelligibility arguments. Not every formal rule system is equally tractable, compressible, predictable, or informative. Some systems are easy to simulate; others are computationally expensive, chaotic, universal, or effectively irreducible in the sense that there may be no useful shortcut to predicting their detailed behavior beyond stepping through the dynamics.

This matters because “Isn't it remarkable that the world has elegant, tractable rules?” is partly a selection-effect question. We preferentially understand, retain, and praise theories that compress phenomena and support feasible inference. A world may contain immense local complexity while still admitting some coarse-grained regularities that bounded agents can exploit. That is enough for science to begin. A maximally incompressible or computationally useless representation would not function well as a theory for us, so successful theories are predictably biased toward computational virtues.

Rules do not explain implementation

Formal rules also do not explain their own physical implementation. An abstract automaton description is not the same thing as a physical system that realizes it. Knowing the transition function of a cellular automaton does not explain why some physical medium instantiates that structure. Likewise, saying “God wrote the rules” does not by itself explain why there is a world instantiating them, why it instantiates these rather than others, or why the implementation has the specific features we observe. The move from formal description to metaphysical source is doing much more work than the analogy acknowledges.

Rules are not necessarily commands

Finally, computation undermines the prescriptive reading of rule. In many computational systems, a “rule” is simply a concise specification of state evolution. Nothing is being commanded, and the system does not “obey” in the normative sense. The rule captures a transition structure. That should make us especially suspicious of importing the everyday notion of prescriptions into nature merely because both contexts use the word “rule.”

The argument risks equivocating among at least four notions: rules as prescriptions, rules as constitutive stipulations, rules as syntactic transition functions, and laws as descriptive or explanatory summaries. The word is stable while the concept changes.

From a computational perspective, then, the chess analogy is deeply misleading. States and transition rules can be specified together, their “fit” can be internal to a formalism, syntax does not determine semantics, the same abstract structure can be multiply realized, and tractability is partly shaped by epistemic selection among bounded reasoners. None of this proves that nature is metaphysically Humean, computational, or merely descriptive. It does show that the simple picture of passive pieces being externally paired with commands is far too coarse to carry the theological inference.

The critique can now be stated constructively. What would a serious nomological-harmony argument have to establish in order to survive these objections?

Part VII What a Successful Argument Would Need The point is not that every possible harmony argument must fail. It is that the existing inference needs much more structure than the rulebook intuition provides.

A serious version of the argument would need to do at least four things. First, it would have to defend an ontology of laws and states on which their relationship is sufficiently external for “harmony” to constitute an additional fact. Second, it would have to defend a modal space containing genuine mismatched alternatives rather than merely verbally recombined descriptions. Third, it would need some principled way of saying that harmonious combinations are improbable under the relevant naturalistic hypothesis. And fourth, it would need a sufficiently constrained theology to show that harmony of the observed kind is appreciably more expected given God.

BurdenWhat must be establishedWhat is not enough
Ontology A defensible account on which laws and world-states can stand in the relevant contingent matching relation. A game analogy that simply presupposes independently authored rules and pieces.
Modality A principled class of genuinely possible disharmonious law/state combinations. The fact that we can describe arbitrary recombinations in language.
Naturalistic likelihood Why harmony is actually improbable on the naturalistic alternative being compared. Calling harmony “surprising” without a measure, mechanism, or principled probability model.
Theistic likelihood Why a sufficiently specified deity would have independent reason to produce a world with this kind of harmony. A post hoc story showing only that God could have wanted it.

Notice how different this is from merely identifying an interesting feature of science. Even if the universe possesses impressive regularity, mathematical structure, or compressibility, those facts do not become theistic evidence until a comparative probability claim has been earned. The defender must show both that the explanandum is real and that theism is appropriately constrained enough to predict it. Otherwise, explanatory flexibility is mistaken for explanatory success.

Conclusion The Surprise Has to Be Earned The deepest problem is not that nomological harmony asks a philosophical question; it is that it moves too quickly from a contested framing of that question to a theological answer.

The nomological-harmony argument begins with an arresting image: nature resembles a box of pieces whose rulebook happens to fit them perfectly. But the image is doing too much. Depending on one's metaphysics of laws, the alleged relation may be descriptive, constitutive, structural, dispositional, or genuinely governing. Until that issue is resolved, the “fit” cannot simply be treated as a neutral coincidence awaiting explanation.

Even after granting a genuine residual puzzle about the world's regularity or intelligibility, the comparative inference remains. An explanatory gap in naturalism is not automatically positive evidence for theism, and a story on which God could produce the observation is not the same thing as a model on which the observation was independently likely. Bare theism can generate an enormous space of possible divine choices, so the very flexibility that makes it easy to accommodate the data also makes its predictive advantage difficult to establish.

The chess and computational analogies clarify why the original intuition is unstable. Constitutive game rules, syntactic transition functions, scientific laws, semantic interpretations, and physical implementations are different things. Treating them all as instances of a single idea—an externally authored “rule” governing passive “pieces”—creates the appearance of harmony by collapsing distinctions that the argument actually needs to defend.

So the most important lesson is methodological. Before asking what explains a surprising harmony, we have to establish that there is an independent harmony to explain, that disharmony belongs to a principled possibility space, that the observed fit is genuinely improbable under the relevant naturalistic view, and that a specified theism predicts it substantially better. Until those burdens are met, the argument does not uncover a deep cosmic coincidence and then explain it with God. It risks manufacturing the coincidence through its metaphysics and then using theism to fill the gap it created.
