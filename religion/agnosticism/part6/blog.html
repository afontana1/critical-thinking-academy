I. The Wrong Question in the God Debate

The question “Does God exist?” is usually treated as though it presents a relatively ordinary dispute between competing hypotheses. One side offers cosmological arguments, arguments from fine-tuning, consciousness, morality, religious experience, or revelation. The other responds with arguments from evil, divine hiddenness, religious disagreement, naturalistic explanation, or the apparent dependence of religious belief on culture and geography. The debate then proceeds by comparing the relative force of these arguments and asking which side has accumulated the stronger case. This framing may begin too late.

Before asking which arguments favor theism or atheism, there are at least two more fundamental questions. First, are the competing hypotheses sufficiently well specified for the available evidence to discriminate among them in a reliable way? Second, if the evidence does not permit such discrimination, what degree of belief or practical commitment is rational under that uncertainty?

These questions are importantly different. The first concerns the epistemic structure of the problem itself. The second concerns rational decision-making given whatever epistemic position we actually occupy. This essay argues that both questions point toward a stronger form of agnosticism than the familiar admission that one personally does not know whether God exists. The argument will proceed in two stages. The first stage concerns epistemic underdetermination. The proposition

$$ G = \text{“God exists”} $$

is often treated as though it were a single explanatory hypothesis that can straightforwardly be compared with naturalism. But the term “God” encompasses an enormous and potentially open-ended class of models differing in divine nature, power, goodness, intentions, psychology, relationship to creation, willingness to intervene, methods of revelation, expectations of human beings, and much else. Once this is recognized, many familiar evidential arguments become considerably less straightforward. To claim that some observation favors theism requires more than the bare proposition that some divine reality exists. It requires additional assumptions concerning what sort of divine reality exists and what that being would be expected to do.

This difficulty is compounded by a series of familiar undercutting considerations: divine hiddenness, inconsistent revelation, the cultural predictability of religious affiliation, conflicting religious experiences, persistent disagreement among sophisticated inquirers, uncertainty concerning divine psychology, and the possibility that our present conceptual categories fail to exhaust the relevant forms of ultimate reality. Taken together, these do not straightforwardly establish atheism. Their more important effect is to undermine confidence that our existing arguments provide a reliable procedure for selecting among the relevant metaphysical possibilities. The conclusion of the first part will therefore not be that

$$ P(G)=0 $$

or even that

$$ P(G)=0.5. $$

The stronger and more interesting claim is that our present epistemic situation may not justify a determinate probability assignment of that kind at all. The problem is not simply that the evidence happens to be balanced. It is that the inference problem itself is poorly specified.

The second stage concerns what follows from this uncertainty. A theist might reasonably concede much of the first argument and respond: “Perhaps we cannot know. But we can still believe.” That response raises a different question. If rational inquiry leaves us under deep uncertainty concerning ultimate reality, should an agent nevertheless adopt a specific religious commitment? This essay will argue that the answer is generally no—not because nonbelief should be privileged, and not because inaction is always rational, but because rational decision-making under deep uncertainty should favor policies that are robust across plausible models, proportion commitment to evidential support, preserve access to further information, and avoid unnecessary exposure to assumptions that have not themselves been justified. The resulting position may be called strong agnosticism. It consists of two claims:

$$ \text{Deep epistemic underdetermination} $$

and

$$ \text{rational restraint under deep uncertainty}. $$

Together they support the conclusion that, under our present epistemic conditions, high-confidence commitment to a particular ultimist or theological model is not rationally warranted.

The argument at a glance

The essay is easiest to follow if its two stages are treated as a single chain of reasoning:

$$ \text{evidence} \rightarrow \text{model identification} \rightarrow \text{epistemic confidence} \rightarrow \text{decision under uncertainty} \rightarrow \text{degree of commitment}. $$
Stage Central question Main task Provisional result
Part I: Epistemic underdetermination Can the available evidence reliably identify a particular model of ultimate reality? Clarify the model space, likelihood problem, divine-psychology problem, and the reliability of proposed discriminators. Our present framework does not robustly identify a single ultimist model or a determinate probability distribution over the relevant space.
Part II: Rational commitment What should an agent do when the epistemic problem itself is deeply uncertain? Evaluate robustness, reversibility, optionality, value of information, and exposure to model error. High-confidence model-specific commitment should not outrun the robustness of its justification; agnostic inquiry is the defeasible default.

Several distinctions should remain fixed throughout. Agnosticism is not naturalism; suspension is not refusal; belief is not identical to practical commitment; private evidential force is not automatically public evidential force; and local evidence against or for one model is not automatically a global ranking of the entire ultimist model space. Later sections develop these distinctions rather than introducing different theses. This is not an argument that ultimate reality is forever unknowable. Nor is it an argument that religious belief is psychologically illegitimate, socially harmful, or necessarily irrational in every individual case. It is instead an argument about what our present evidence and our general principles of rational decision-making jointly permit us to conclude.

The central contention is therefore not that we have answered the question “Does God exist?” It is that we may have misunderstood what would be required to answer it, and what rationally follows when those requirements have not been met.

Part IEpistemic Underdetermination

The first part asks whether the available arguments and evidence can reliably identify a particular model of ultimate reality. It moves from the size and openness of the model space, through the Bayesian and divine-psychology problems, to the cumulative undercutters and the resulting condition of deep epistemic underdetermination.

II. What Exactly Is the Hypothesis? The Model-Space Problem

Any attempt to evaluate evidence for or against God depends upon what, exactly, is meant by “God.” This point can initially sound trivial. Obviously, different religions disagree about the divine. But the problem is considerably deeper than ordinary religious disagreement. Before evidence can discriminate among competing explanations, we need some account of what the relevant competing explanations actually are. In the case of ultimate reality, it is not clear that we possess any well-defined or exhaustive hypothesis space of this kind. Suppose we begin with the familiar proposition

$$ G=\text{“God exists.”} $$

Much contemporary philosophy of religion implicitly treats \(G\) as though it referred to a reasonably unified hypothesis: typically a single personal creator who is omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good, and metaphysically ultimate. The resulting debate then tends to compare this form of theism against naturalism. But this framing already makes an enormous theoretical choice.

Classical monotheism is only one possible conception of ultimate reality. Even the familiar taxonomy of religious metaphysics contains radically different alternatives. Pantheism, for example, generally identifies God with the cosmos rather than positing a creator ontologically distinct from it. Panentheism instead understands the world as existing within God while maintaining some distinction between God and the world. Polytheistic models posit multiple divine beings rather than a unique supreme personal creator. Deistic models may posit an intelligent creator while denying continuing supernatural intervention or special revelation. Other traditions and philosophical systems contain henotheistic, nonpersonal, monistic, process, finite-god, and other conceptions that fit uneasily into the usual binary between classical theism and naturalism. Even the boundaries among these categories are philosophically contested. This diversity is not merely anthropological. These are genuinely different metaphysical hypotheses. Consider only a small sample:

Model family Core claim Immediate evidential consequence
Classical monotheism One transcendent, omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good creator exists. Rich divine attributes can generate comparatively determinate expectations, but therefore also create clearer conditions for disconfirmation.
Deism A supreme intelligence is responsible for the universe but does not ordinarily intervene through miracles or special revelation. Evidence involving revelation or intervention may have little force against the model.
Pantheism God and the cosmos are, in some important sense, identical. Creator-style predictions may not apply because the model need not involve a separate choosing agent.
Panentheism The world exists in God and participates in divine reality, while God is not simply identical to the world. Its expectations can differ substantially from both classical creation and strict identity models.
Process or developing-God models God is affected by the world and may not possess the immutable, impassible nature associated with classical theism. Arguments that presuppose unlimited power, immutability, or impassibility may miss the target.
Polytheism Multiple divine beings exist, potentially with different domains, capacities, interests, or relationships to the cosmos. Polytheism itself need not be philosophically primitive; contemporary philosophers have explicitly explored sophisticated metaphysical forms of polytheism. Predictions depend upon how powers, aims, and causal roles are distributed among the gods.
Nonpersonal ultimate-reality models Reality has an ultimate metaphysical or axiological structure without that ultimate reality being a personal God in the familiar Western sense. Appeals to divine intention or psychology may be inapplicable.
Religious ultimism Some metaphysically and axiologically ultimate reality exists in relation to which an ultimate human good is possible, while leaving the specific nature of that reality substantially open. This is close to the deliberately general framework associated with J. L. Schellenberg. Breadth protects the hypothesis from some model-specific objections but weakens its predictive specificity.

Nor is this list plausibly exhaustive. There may be coherent possibilities that combine features of these positions, possibilities that fall outside our inherited religious classifications, and possibilities that have simply not yet been conceived. This last point matters. The philosophical problem cannot be reduced to comparing Christianity, Islam, Judaism, and naturalism, as though the metaphysical alternatives had conveniently been exhausted by historically prominent positions. Even within philosophy of religion there has been increasing criticism of the tendency to focus overwhelmingly upon personal theistic conceptions rather than the larger space of possible conceptions of God or ultimate reality. For the purposes of this essay, then, it is useful to introduce a broader symbol:

$$ U=\text{the space of ultimist models}. $$

We might represent that space schematically as

$$ U= \{ M^{\text{mono}}_1,\ldots, M^{\text{poly}}_1,\ldots, M^{\text{pant}}_1,\ldots, M^{\text{panen}}_1,\ldots, M^{\text{deist}}_1,\ldots, M^{\text{nonpersonal}}_1,\ldots \}. $$

The notation is intentionally incomplete. The ellipses are not merely shorthand for models we already know. They also represent the possibility that our current taxonomy fails to exhaust the relevant possibilities. This immediately changes the evidential problem. Suppose, for example, that we observe substantial divine hiddenness:

$$ H=\text{widespread sincere nonbelief concerning ultimate reality}. $$

This might be surprising under a model in which an all-loving personal God strongly desires explicit reciprocal relationships with every human being. But it may not be especially surprising under deism, under a God-model that values epistemic distance, under some forms of pantheism, or under an impersonal conception of ultimate reality. Conversely, suppose we observe apparent fine-tuning:

$$ F=\text{apparently life-permitting physical parameters}. $$

A proponent of classical theism might claim that \(F\) is expected because a good creator would value conscious life. But that expectation does not follow from the bare proposition that some ultimate or divine reality exists. A pantheistic ultimate does not obviously “choose” constants at all. A deistic creator might have intentions radically unlike those attributed to the God of classical theism. Multiple gods might have competing aims. An impersonal ultimate reality need not possess intentions of any kind.

The observation therefore cannot straightforwardly favor “God” in general. At most, it favors particular models relative to particular alternatives, conditional upon substantive assumptions about those models. This reveals a distinction that will be central throughout the essay:

$$ \text{compatibility with evidence} \neq \text{prediction of evidence}. $$

A hypothesis can be compatible with an observation while giving us little reason to expect that observation in advance. This distinction becomes especially important as the specification of a God-model changes. Consider a very general hypothesis:

$$ M_0=\text{“Some transcendent intelligence exists.”} $$

It is difficult to know what could disconfirm \(M_0\). Suppose the universe contains abundant conscious life. That is compatible with \(M_0\). Suppose it contains almost none. That is also compatible with \(M_0\). Suppose miracles occur constantly. Compatible. Suppose no unambiguous miracle has ever occurred. Also compatible. Suppose God appears universally to human beings. Compatible. Suppose God remains completely hidden. Again compatible. The hypothesis survives all of these possibilities because almost nothing has been specified about the motivations or behavior of the proposed intelligence. But that flexibility has an epistemic price. If both

$$ E $$

and

$$ \neg E $$

are equally easy to accommodate under a hypothesis, then observing \(E\) cannot ordinarily provide powerful confirmation of that hypothesis. In other words, increasing flexibility makes a hypothesis harder to falsify partly because it reduces what the hypothesis actually predicts. Now compare this with a more specific model:

$$ M_1= \text{“A perfectly loving personal God exists and strongly desires conscious reciprocal relationship with every capable person.”} $$

This hypothesis possesses much more content. It seems to generate at least some expectation concerning divine accessibility. Suppose we then discover extensive nonresistant nonbelief. The observation now becomes potentially troublesome precisely because \(M_1\) told us more about what kind of world to expect. The same phenomenon occurs with revelation. Compare:

$$ M_2=\text{“Some divine reality exists”} $$

with

$$ M_3= \text{“A perfectly good God intends to reveal a unique and universally important message to humanity.”} $$

The existence of numerous incompatible revelations may have little bearing on \(M_2\) because \(M_2\) says almost nothing about revelation. But it is much more relevant to \(M_3\) because \(M_3\) generates expectations concerning successful divine communication. This gives us a general relationship:

$$ \text{greater specification} \rightarrow \text{more determinate expectations} \rightarrow \text{greater potential confirmation} $$

but simultaneously,

$$ \text{greater specification} \rightarrow \text{more determinate expectations} \rightarrow \text{greater potential disconfirmation}. $$

The two consequences are inseparable. A model earns the possibility of strong confirmation by taking the corresponding risk of being wrong. Conversely,

$$ \text{greater flexibility} \rightarrow \text{compatibility with more possible worlds} $$

but therefore

$$ \text{greater flexibility} \rightarrow \text{less discriminatory evidential content}. $$

This creates a recurring tension for theological explanation. When a particular model appears to generate an unsuccessful prediction, it can often be protected by weakening or modifying its assumptions. If divine hiddenness is unexpected because God desires relationship, perhaps God also desires epistemic distance. If suffering is unexpected under perfect goodness, perhaps suffering serves unknown higher-order goods. If revelation is confusing, perhaps God values religious diversity or indirect discovery. Any one of these responses may be logically coherent. The problem is methodological. Each additional qualification changes the model being evaluated. A hypothesis of the form

$$ M $$

encounters unexpected evidence \(E\) and becomes

$$ M'=M+A, $$

where \(A\) is an additional auxiliary claim that makes \(E\) less surprising. Again, this does not by itself show that \(M'\) is false or irrational. Scientific theories also contain auxiliary assumptions and are sometimes legitimately revised. The important question is whether the revision is independently motivated, whether it generates new constraints, and whether there are observations that would count against it. Without such constraints, theological specification can become highly adaptive:

$$ \text{observation} \rightarrow \text{new account of divine motivation} \rightarrow \text{compatibility restored}. $$

At the limit, a sufficiently flexible God-model can become consistent with virtually any conceivable evidence. But such a model cannot then retain the evidential advantages of a highly predictive theory. It cannot simultaneously say, “Whatever we observed could be explained by God,” and “What we observed is strongly predicted by God,” unless some independent account tells us which observations were genuinely more probable beforehand. This is why specificity matters so much.

A model such as classical perfect-being theism can potentially acquire significant evidential content precisely because it says much more than merely “something divine exists.” Claims concerning perfect goodness, rationality, love, omniscience, creative agency, and providence constrain what proponents can plausibly say about divine action. Those constraints make arguments from fine-tuning, morality, consciousness, or religious experience possible—but they also make arguments from suffering, hiddenness, and religious confusion possible. A very weak ultimist hypothesis avoids many of those objections:

$$ U_0=\text{“There exists some ultimate reality of religious significance.”} $$

But it avoids them largely because it predicts much less. This yields what might be called the specificity dilemma:

$$ \boxed{ \text{A theological model sufficiently specific to predict evidence is sufficiently specific to risk disconfirmation.} } $$

while

$$ \boxed{ \text{A theological model sufficiently flexible to avoid disconfirmation risks becoming too weak to receive substantial confirmation.} } $$

Neither horn establishes that no divine reality exists. That is not the purpose of the argument. Instead, it forces us to identify exactly which model is doing the explanatory work whenever an evidential argument is offered.

This also exposes a problem with speaking loosely of “the evidence for theism.” Which theism? Classical monotheism? Deism? A finite creator? Panentheism? One member of a polytheistic system? A personal ultimate? A pantheistic absolute? Some much weaker form of ultimism?

The question becomes even more difficult once we recognize that these categories themselves contain large families of models. “Polytheism,” for example, does not tell us how many gods exist, how powerful they are, whether one is supreme, whether they created the universe, whether they are morally perfect, or whether they interact with human beings. “Pantheism” similarly admits importantly different accounts of what it means for the cosmos and the divine to be identical. The philosophical literature itself reflects substantial disagreement over how these categories should even be classified.

Thus the problem is recursive. We are uncertain not only over which broad family is correct but also over the enormous number of possible specifications within each family:

$$ U = \bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_j, $$

where each \(\mathcal{M}_j\) is itself a family of possible ultimist models. At this point the ordinary contrast between “theism” and “naturalism” begins to look artificially narrow. Naturalism may be one important competitor, and classical theism another, but there is no obvious reason to assume that these two exhaust the possibilities relevant to ultimate reality. Nor is there an obvious reason to assign classical theism a privileged position within the larger space simply because it has historically dominated Western philosophy of religion. This is the first major component of the argument for strong agnosticism.

Before asking whether some observation supports “God,” we first need to know which model the observation is being evaluated under, why that model should occupy a privileged position within the larger hypothesis space, and which assumptions generate its predictions. The more specific the model becomes, the more answerable these questions become—but the more empirical and philosophical risks the model must accept. The less specific it becomes, the more difficult it is to identify what evidence could genuinely distinguish it from its alternatives. The resulting difficulty is not merely that there are many religions.

It is that the space of possible ultimate realities is large, internally heterogeneous, and plausibly open-ended, while evidential arguments require precisely the kind of model specification that this diversity denies us at the generic level. The next question is therefore unavoidable: if “God” or “ultimate reality” represents a model space rather than a single model, what would it actually mean to say that some evidence makes it more probable?

III. The Bayesian Model-Space Problem

The model-space problem becomes sharper, not weaker, when expressed in Bayesian terms. Once “God” or “ultimate reality” is recognized as a heterogeneous class of possible models rather than a single hypothesis, the relevant likelihoods cannot simply be assumed. They must be constructed by specifying which models belong to the class, how probability is distributed across those models, and how each model assigns probabilities to possible observations. This means that an expression such as

$$ P(E\mid G) $$

or

$$ P(E\mid U) $$

can conceal substantially more uncertainty than it reveals. If \(G\) refers to a specific and sufficiently articulated God-model, then the quantity may at least be conceptually well-defined. But if \(G\) means something like “some God exists,” or \(U\) means “some ultimist account of reality is true,” then neither proposition determines a unique probability distribution over possible evidence. The central problem is therefore not Bayesian updating. It is Bayesian specification.

Before an observation can raise or lower the probability of ultimism, we must first possess a sufficiently justified probabilistic representation of ultimism itself. It is far from clear that we do.

Ultimism as a model class

Let

$$ U=\text{“some ultimist account of reality is true.”} $$

As argued in the previous section, \(U\) contains many substantially different possibilities. We can represent them schematically as

$$ U=\{M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_n,\ldots\}. $$

The members might include classical monotheistic models, deistic models, pantheistic and panentheistic models, polytheistic models, finite-God models, impersonal forms of ultimacy, and possibilities not captured by our current categories. If we want to ask how strongly some evidence \(E\) is predicted by ultimism in general, we therefore need something like

$$ P(E\mid U) = \sum_i P(E\mid M_i)P(M_i\mid U). $$

This equation reveals something that ordinary talk of “evidence for God” tends to suppress. The evidential significance of \(E\) for ultimism depends not only upon what the individual models predict, but upon how probability is distributed across the ultimist model space. Suppose some observation is highly probable under one particular form of classical theism:

$$ P(E\mid M_1)=0.9, $$

but unlikely under a deistic model:

$$ P(E\mid M_2)=0.1, $$

approximately neutral under some pantheistic model:

$$ P(E\mid M_3)=0.5, $$

and difficult even to interpret under an impersonal form of ultimism. Whether \(E\) is “expected under ultimism” cannot be answered simply by pointing to \(M_1\). We must know how much weight \(M_1\) receives relative to the alternatives:

$$ P(M_1\mid U),\quad P(M_2\mid U),\quad P(M_3\mid U),\ldots $$

Without those weights, there is no determinate marginal likelihood for ultimism as a whole. This is important because much religious argumentation moves implicitly from

$$ P(E\mid M_i)\text{ is high} $$

to

$$ P(E\mid U)\text{ is high}. $$

But the second claim does not follow from the first. An observation may be strongly predicted by one theological model while being irrelevant, surprising, or conceptually inapplicable under many others. The problem is therefore not simply that people disagree about how probable God is. It is that the proposition being assigned a probability often represents a model class whose internal distribution has never been specified.

The missing measure over the model space

To calculate

$$ P(E\mid U), $$

we need some principled measure over the members of \(U\). But what determines

$$ P(M_i\mid U)? $$

Why should classical monotheism receive more weight than deism? Why should a personal ultimate receive more weight than an impersonal one? How much probability should be assigned to polytheistic models? How should panentheistic or pantheistic possibilities be treated? What probability should be reserved for models that have not yet been conceived?

These questions cannot be answered merely by invoking Bayes' theorem. They concern the construction of the probability space to which Bayesian reasoning will subsequently be applied. This is a more fundamental problem than ordinary disagreement over priors. Suppose two investigators agree that the relevant possibilities are

$$ M_1,M_2,M_3. $$

They may disagree over whether

$$ P(M_1)=0.2 $$

or

$$ P(M_1)=0.4. $$

That is ordinary prior disagreement within a shared model space. Our problem is deeper. We may not know whether

$$ \{M_1,M_2,M_3\} $$

is remotely close to the correct model space in the first place. Perhaps it should instead be

$$ \{M_1,\ldots,M_{1000}\}. $$

Perhaps entire classes of possibilities are missing. Perhaps our way of dividing the possibilities into “models” is itself arbitrary. Thus there are really two prior questions:

$$ \text{What is the relevant hypothesis space?} $$

and

$$ \text{What probability measure should be placed over it?} $$

Only after answering both can the usual machinery of Bayesian model comparison begin.

There is no obvious neutral prior over possible Gods

One might try to avoid the problem by assigning equal prior probability to each possible ultimist model. But this immediately encounters a partition problem. Consider the broad hypothesis

$$ M_A=\text{“one personal creator exists.”} $$

Should this count as one model? Or should it be subdivided according to divine power? According to goodness? According to willingness to intervene? According to the importance assigned to human beings? According to preferences concerning revelation? According to whether an afterlife exists? According to what that creator desires from rational agents? A single broad model can be decomposed indefinitely:

$$ M_A = M_{A1}\lor M_{A2}\lor M_{A3}\lor\cdots. $$

If probability is assigned simply by counting models, then subdividing one region of the hypothesis space into more descriptions can artificially increase its probability mass. The same problem occurs across categories. “Polytheism” is not one model. It represents an enormous family of possible worlds involving different numbers of divine beings, different distributions of power, different moral characteristics, different relationships among the gods, and different relationships between those gods and the physical universe. Nor is “pantheism” a single fully specified probabilistic hypothesis. Nor is “deism.” Nor, for that matter, is “naturalism.” A neutral distribution therefore cannot be obtained simply by assigning equal probability to names on a list. Some independently justified measure over the underlying possibility space would be required. It is not obvious what such a measure would look like.

Uncertainty within each model

Even this understates the problem, because many comparatively specific theological models still fail to determine observational likelihoods. Suppose we restrict attention to

$$ M_C=\text{classical perfect-being theism}. $$

We have now substantially narrowed the model space. But \(M_C\) still does not uniquely tell us what sort of world God would create. To derive observational expectations, we may need additional variables describing divine purposes, priorities, and choices. Let these collectively be represented by

$$ \theta. $$

These might include variables corresponding to claims such as

$$ \theta_1=\text{the importance God assigns to creaturely freedom}, $$
$$ \theta_2=\text{the importance God assigns to explicit relationship}, $$
$$ \theta_3=\text{the value God places on embodied conscious life}, $$
$$ \theta_4=\text{God's willingness to intervene miraculously}, $$
$$ \theta_5=\text{God's reasons for permitting suffering}, $$

and so forth. The relevant likelihood then becomes something like

$$ P(E\mid M_C) = \int P(E\mid M_C,\theta) P(\theta\mid M_C) \,d\theta. $$

The evidential question has therefore acquired another level. We first need a distribution over broad metaphysical models:

$$ P(M_i\mid U), $$

and then, within a particular model, a distribution over the parameters governing its expected behavior:

$$ P(\theta\mid M_i). $$

Only after specifying both do we obtain

$$ P(E\mid U). $$

Schematically, the structure is

$$ U \rightarrow M_i \rightarrow \theta_i \rightarrow E. $$

This is why simply conditioning evidence on “God” can be misleading. Hidden beneath the symbol \(G\) may be an extensive hierarchy of theological assumptions.

Divine intention as a latent variable

This problem becomes particularly obvious whenever an argument contains a statement of the form:

“If God existed, we would expect...”

Complete the sentence in different ways:

If God existed, we would expect conscious life.

If God existed, we would expect objective morality.

If God existed, we would expect a mathematically elegant universe.

If God existed, we would expect religious experience.

If God existed, we would expect divine revelation.

None of these follows from the bare proposition

$$ G=\text{“God exists.”} $$

Each requires some substantive theory about the relevant God's preferences. The actual structure is closer to

$$ G + \text{God values }X + \text{God chooses means }Y \rightarrow E. $$

The same applies to arguments against particular forms of theism:

If God existed, we would not expect this much suffering.

If God existed, we would not expect widespread nonresistant nonbelief.

If God existed, we would not expect contradictory revelations.

Again, some theory of divine value or intention sits between the metaphysical hypothesis and the observation. This hidden variable is crucial because different accounts of divine psychology can reverse the evidential direction. Suppose

$$ E=\text{divine hiddenness}. $$

Under one parameterization,

$$ \theta_a=\text{God strongly values universally available explicit relationship}, $$

we might judge

$$ P(E\mid M_C,\theta_a) $$

to be quite low. But under another,

$$ \theta_b=\text{God strongly values epistemic distance}, $$

the same observation may become much less surprising. The dispute is therefore not necessarily about the observation. It is about

$$ P(\theta\mid M_C). $$

How likely is it that a perfectly good God would possess one set of aims rather than another? And what evidence could justify that probability assignment independently of the very world we are trying to explain? This last question is especially important.

Prediction and retrospective accommodation

Suppose we observe \(E\) and subsequently infer that God must value some goal \(X\) that would make \(E\) expected. We now obtain

$$ P(E\mid G,X)\text{ is high}. $$

But this does not establish that

$$ P(E\mid G) $$

was high before \(X\) was introduced. This distinction separates prediction from accommodation. If divine purposes are inferred retrospectively from whatever world happens to exist, then almost any observation can potentially be made probable:

$$ E_1 \rightarrow \text{God had reason }R_1, $$
$$ E_2 \rightarrow \text{God had reason }R_2, $$
$$ E_3 \rightarrow \text{God had reason }R_3. $$

If \(E_1\) and \(\neg E_1\) can each be assigned a plausible divine rationale after the fact, the claim that \(E_1\) was antecedently likely becomes much weaker. This does not mean that retrospective explanation is always illegitimate. Scientific explanations are also sometimes developed after unexpected evidence appears. The relevant issue is constraint. A successful explanatory revision should ideally be independently motivated, generate further expectations, and expose itself to the possibility of failure. If auxiliary theological assumptions can instead be introduced without an independently justified cost, then the model gains flexibility without paying the corresponding penalty in evidential credibility. That produces a form of theological overfitting. The observed world is accommodated increasingly well, but the ability of the model to distinguish the observed world from alternative possible worlds becomes weaker.

Bayesian notation can hide this problem

This is one reason apparently precise Bayesian presentations in philosophy of religion should be interpreted carefully. Suppose an argument assigns

$$ P(E\mid G)=0.9. $$

Nothing in probability theory guarantees that this number represents objective information supplied by the God hypothesis. Its justification may ultimately reduce to an intuition such as

“A perfectly good creator would probably want beings like us.”

The numerical representation does not remove the philosophical dependence upon that premise. A different investigator may instead believe

$$ P(E\mid G)=0.3 $$

because they possess a different conception of divine purposes. Both calculations can be internally valid. Their disagreement occurs upstream of Bayes' theorem. This is why the central issue is not whether Bayesian reasoning is legitimate. It is whether the theological models supply sufficiently constrained likelihood functions for Bayesian reasoning to discriminate among them. Put differently:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Bayesian formalism cannot rescue an unspecified likelihood.} } $$

And:

$$ \boxed{ \text{A posterior can be no better identified than the model that generates its likelihood.} } $$

The problem applies in both directions

This criticism is not selectively aimed at arguments for theism. Suppose an atheist argues that suffering is extremely improbable under God:

$$ P(S\mid G)\ll 1. $$

That judgment also depends upon a model of God. Perhaps the argument is strong against

$$ M_1= \text{a perfectly good and powerful God with certain independently motivated aims}. $$

But it may have little relevance to a finite God, morally indifferent creator, polytheistic system, deistic creator, pantheistic ultimate, or some other conception of ultimate reality. Likewise, divine hiddenness may strongly disconfirm some relational forms of perfect-being theism while saying comparatively little about ultimism generally. This is precisely why these arguments can be powerful conditional arguments without settling the larger metaphysical question. They may establish

$$ P(E\mid M_i)<P(E\mid M_j) $$

for particular models. They do not automatically establish

$$ P(E\mid U)<P(E\mid \neg U). $$

That latter comparison requires the entire structure discussed above. The symmetry matters because the argument for strong agnosticism is not an attempt to protect theism from disconfirmation. It is an argument that both positive and negative evidential claims become increasingly difficult to generalize as the target proposition expands from a particular theology toward ultimism as such. Against highly specified models, evidence can have considerable discriminatory force. Against a poorly specified model class, its force becomes correspondingly harder to determine.

Four levels of uncertainty

We can now distinguish at least four different kinds of uncertainty. They should not be collapsed, because each concerns a different part of the inferential structure.

Level What is uncertain? Typical form of the problem
Data uncertainty The observations themselves. Are the measurements, reports, or experiences accurate?
Parameter uncertainty The values of parameters within a known model. Given the model, which parameter values best describe the world?
Model uncertainty Which model within a specified model class is correct. Which of the recognized competing models should receive the greatest support?
Model-space uncertainty The structure of the hypothesis space itself. Which models belong in the space, how should they be individuated and weighted, and what important alternatives may remain unconceived?

Here we are uncertain about the structure of the hypothesis space itself. We may not know:

  • which models should be included;
  • how they should be individuated;
  • whether important alternatives remain unconceived;
  • how probability should be distributed across them; or
  • whether our conceptual categories carve the space appropriately at all.

The resulting epistemic state is therefore not adequately described by saying:

“We know the possibilities but do not know which one is true.”

It is closer to:

We do not possess a well-justified probabilistic representation of the possibilities over which the inference is supposed to range.

That is a materially stronger claim. If this diagnosis is correct, then assigning a precise posterior to generic theism or ultimism may convey more epistemic precision than the underlying problem warrants. The failure is not located in Bayes' theorem. The failure occurs before Bayes' theorem can do the work we are asking of it. We have not yet justified the probability model. This gives us a central principle for what follows:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Before evidence can update a metaphysical model, there must be a sufficiently specified model to update.} } $$

And because ultimism contains an open-ended family of competing models, many of which themselves require uncertain assumptions concerning divine purposes and behavior, it is far from obvious that this requirement has been satisfied. The next section concerns the deepest instance of this problem. For personal God-models in particular, observational likelihoods depend upon predictions about what a divine agent would choose to do. That requires some account of divine psychology. Yet our ability to construct such an account is precisely what many theological responses call into question. This creates a dilemma: the more confidently we claim to understand divine motivations, the more theism becomes exposed to contrary evidence; the less we claim to understand them, the less capable theism becomes of predicting favorable evidence in the first place.

IV. Divine Psychology and the Prediction Problem

The Bayesian model-space problem becomes especially acute whenever the hypothesis under consideration involves a personal divine agent. If a theory says that some agent created, sustains, or governs the world, then predicting what evidence that theory should produce depends upon some account of what that agent would want, value, permit, or choose. This is the problem of divine psychology.

The phrase should not be understood too literally. The issue is not whether God has a human-like psychology. The deeper point is that any probabilistic prediction from a personal God-model requires some theory of divine motivation. Suppose we are told that fine-tuning is evidence for theism because a good God would probably want conscious life. The structure of the argument is not merely

$$ G\rightarrow F. $$

It is closer to

$$ G + \text{God values conscious life} + \text{God prefers creating a life-permitting universe} \rightarrow F. $$

Similarly, an argument from moral awareness may depend upon something like

$$ G + \text{God wants rational creatures to recognize moral truths} \rightarrow M, $$

while an argument from religious experience may require

$$ G + \text{God wants creatures to experience the divine} \rightarrow R. $$

The point is not that these assumptions are necessarily implausible. It is that they are assumptions. The bare existence of a powerful or ultimate mind does not logically entail that it would create embodied conscious life, reveal moral truths, produce religious experiences, or instantiate any particular physical universe. The evidential force of the argument therefore depends upon how confidently we can move from claims about divine nature to claims about divine action. That same dependence appears in arguments against theism.

The argument from divine hiddenness often assumes that a perfectly loving God would make meaningful relationship available to all nonresistant persons. The evidential problem of suffering assumes that a perfectly good and sufficiently powerful God would have strong reasons to prevent at least some kinds or quantities of suffering. Arguments from religious confusion may assume that a God who intends to reveal important truths would communicate them in a sufficiently clear and reliable manner. So both sides rely upon inferences of the general form

$$ \text{God has property }X \rightarrow \text{God would probably choose action }Y. $$

This is where a deep tension appears.

The knowability dilemma

Suppose we are sufficiently confident about divine psychology to assign meaningful likelihoods to observations. We might then say:

$$ P(F\mid G)\text{ is high} $$

because God would probably value life. Or:

$$ P(R\mid G)\text{ is high} $$

because God would probably reveal itself. But once this sort of inference is permitted, negative evidence becomes equally available. If a perfectly loving God would probably make relationship widely accessible, then

$$ P(H\mid G) $$

may be low, where \(H\) represents widespread nonresistant nonbelief. If a perfectly good God would probably prevent certain forms of apparently gratuitous suffering, then

$$ P(S\mid G) $$

may likewise be low. If a revelatory God would probably communicate with substantial clarity, then widespread contradictory revelation may become evidentially relevant. The more confidently we claim to know what God would likely do, the more theological models become exposed to the world failing to look that way. This is not a defect. It is exactly what it means for a hypothesis to possess predictive content. A model that says more can be confirmed by more, but it can also be contradicted by more. Now consider the opposite strategy.

The theist may respond that human beings are in no position to confidently infer God's reasons. Divine intelligence may vastly exceed ours. Goods may exist that we cannot perceive. What appears to us pointless may play some role within a structure far beyond our understanding. This line of thought can weaken negative arguments. If we do not know what reasons God might have for permitting suffering, then the inference

$$ \text{I see no sufficient reason for }S \rightarrow \text{God has no sufficient reason for }S $$

becomes questionable. Likewise, perhaps there are reasons for divine hiddenness that human beings cannot appreciate. But this defense comes at a cost. If God's reasons are too inscrutable for us to infer that a loving God would probably prevent certain suffering, why are they sufficiently transparent for us to infer that a good God would probably create conscious life? If we cannot confidently predict that God would make divine reality evident, why can we confidently predict that God would fine-tune the universe?

If divine purposes may radically differ from our expectations when the evidence appears unfavorable, why should ordinary human intuitions about divine purposes be trusted when the evidence appears favorable? The same skeptical pressure applies in both directions. This produces what may be called the divine-psychology dilemma:

$$ \boxed{ \text{If divine motivations are sufficiently knowable to generate strong positive predictions, they are sufficiently knowable to generate potential negative predictions.} } $$

But:

$$ \boxed{ \text{If divine motivations are too inscrutable for negative predictions, they are also too inscrutable for many positive predictions.} } $$

The theological model cannot easily maximize both predictive confidence and defensive flexibility at the same time.

Skeptical theism and evidential symmetry

This tension is especially visible in skeptical theism. Skeptical theism, in broad form, questions whether human beings are cognitively positioned to infer that God lacks sufficient reasons for allowing apparently gratuitous evils. The strategy is understandable. If divine reasons may be far beyond our ken, then the fact that we cannot identify a justification for some event does not strongly imply that none exists. But once this skepticism is generalized, it threatens a large class of positive theological inferences. Suppose we observe an elegant physical law and say:

A rational God would value elegance.

Why assume that? Suppose we observe consciousness and say:

A good God would want conscious creatures.

Why assume that? Suppose we observe moral awareness and say:

A morally perfect God would want creatures to know moral truths.

Again, why should our limited moral and cognitive perspective provide reliable access to those divine preferences if it cannot reliably tell us what God would do in cases of suffering or hiddenness? The difficulty is not that one must choose total skepticism or total confidence. Degrees of confidence are possible. Some claims about divine action may be better justified than others. But then the burden becomes one of principled asymmetry. The theist must explain why our reasoning about divine motivations is trustworthy in the cases used to confirm theism but unreliable in the cases used to disconfirm it. Without an independent reason for that difference, the pattern risks becoming evidentially opportunistic:

$$ \text{favorable evidence} \rightarrow \text{we understand what God would want}, $$

while

$$ \text{unfavorable evidence} \rightarrow \text{God's reasons are beyond us}. $$

That is not a stable predictive methodology.

Divine properties do not uniquely determine divine actions

The problem goes deeper still. Even apparently rich divine attributes may fail to determine a unique policy of action. Take perfect goodness. From

$$ \text{God is perfectly good} $$

it does not immediately follow whether God should maximize happiness, maximize virtue, preserve freedom, create the greatest diversity of goods, prioritize relationship, permit substantial risk, minimize suffering, or pursue some combination of these. Different moral theories can generate different predictions about what a perfectly good being would do. Similarly, perfect rationality does not by itself determine which ends God possesses. Omnipotence specifies capacity, not preference. Omniscience specifies knowledge, not motivation. Even perfect love may underdetermine whether love is best expressed through unmistakable presence, respectful distance, developmental struggle, or some other relation. Thus the usual divine attributes do not by themselves solve the likelihood problem. There remains a gap between

$$ \text{what God is} $$

and

$$ \text{what God would choose}. $$

That gap is precisely where many evidential arguments locate their most important assumptions.

Specificity creates evidential liability

This returns us to the specificity dilemma from the previous section. Suppose a theological model adds the claim

$$ A=\text{“God strongly desires explicit relationship with every capable person.”} $$

The augmented model

$$ M'=M+A $$

now generates a stronger prediction concerning divine accessibility. That added specificity makes some evidence more probable under the model. But it also creates a new failure condition. If the world contains widespread sincere nonbelief, then \(M'\) faces a problem that the weaker model \(M\) may avoid. This relationship is general:

$$ \text{additional theological content} \rightarrow \text{additional evidential commitments}. $$

The model gains explanatory potential by accepting evidential risk. If the response to an apparent failure is always to weaken, reinterpret, or supplement the relevant divine motivation, then the theory may preserve logical consistency while gradually losing predictive content. For example:

$$ \text{God wants relationship} $$

may become

$$ \text{God wants relationship but also epistemic distance}. $$

If hiddenness remains extensive, the model might become

$$ \text{God wants relationship, values epistemic distance, and has unknown reasons for permitting persistent nonbelief}. $$

Again, none of these additions is automatically false. The methodological question is whether they were independently motivated before the problematic evidence appeared, and whether they create new expectations that could themselves fail. Otherwise the model risks becoming progressively insulated from disconfirmation.

The predictive-content tradeoff

The tension can therefore be summarized as follows:

$$ \text{greater confidence about divine psychology} \rightarrow \text{more determinate likelihoods} \rightarrow \text{greater evidential discrimination}, $$

but also

$$ \text{greater confidence about divine psychology} \rightarrow \text{more opportunities for prediction failure}. $$

Conversely,

$$ \text{greater skepticism about divine psychology} \rightarrow \text{greater resistance to negative evidence}, $$

but also

$$ \text{greater skepticism about divine psychology} \rightarrow \text{weaker positive confirmation}. $$

This does not prove that every personal God-model is evidentially empty. Some models may contain sufficiently well-motivated claims about divine action to generate meaningful predictions. The point is instead that the evidential force belongs to those specific claims. One cannot move freely between a highly specified God when deriving favorable predictions and an inscrutable, minimally specified God when confronting unfavorable evidence without changing the model under evaluation. This distinction will matter in the next section, where the familiar objections of divine hiddenness, religious disagreement, cultural conditioning, religious experience, and related considerations will be treated not merely as arguments against particular forms of theism, but as undercutters of the methods by which competing ultimist models are supposed to be identified in the first place.

V. The Cumulative Undercutters

The preceding sections identified a structural problem: the relevant metaphysical hypothesis space is large, and many theological models are too weakly specified to generate determinate likelihoods without additional assumptions about divine nature and motivation. That raises a natural question. Perhaps the correct model need not be identified through abstract probability assignments alone. Human beings have other purported routes to religious knowledge. We possess traditions, revelations, religious experiences, historical claims, philosophical arguments, and communities that claim to have encountered or understood ultimate reality. Perhaps these mechanisms can narrow the hypothesis space for us.

If they could do so reliably, much of the preceding difficulty would diminish. We might begin with an enormous model space but possess evidence that strongly concentrates rational credence within one region of it. The problem is that our principal methods of theological identification appear themselves to be subject to substantial undercutting evidence.

The following considerations should therefore not be understood merely as a collection of independent arguments against the existence of God. Some do function that way against particular theological models. Their more important cumulative role in the present argument is different: they weaken our confidence that we possess a reliable procedure for determining which ultimist model, if any, should receive epistemic privilege.

1. Divine hiddenness

Divine hiddenness is initially an argument against a particular kind of God. Schellenberg's influential formulation focuses on the existence of persons who are capable of relationship with God, are not resistant to such a relationship, and nevertheless fail to believe that God exists. The argument then asks whether this is compatible with the existence of an unsurpassably loving personal God who would remain open to relationship with every capable nonresistant person. For the present argument, however, hiddenness has a second significance.

If reality contains a personal God who wants human beings to know that God exists, then divine self-disclosure seems like one possible mechanism by which the enormous ultimist model space could be reduced. A sufficiently clear revelation of divine existence and nature would provide evidence unavailable from philosophical speculation alone. But the actual epistemic situation is strikingly different. There is widespread uncertainty not merely about God's purposes, but about whether any God exists at all. This produces two possibilities.

Under some theological models, hiddenness becomes evidence against the model itself. Under other models—deism, some forms of impersonal ultimism, or models on which God values epistemic distance—it may be far less surprising. But notice the consequence either way. Hiddenness does not help us identify generic ultimism. Instead, its evidential significance varies sharply with the theological model already under consideration. The purported solution therefore presupposes the very model-selection problem it was supposed to resolve.

Moreover, the philosophical literature on hiddenness explicitly includes arguments from the demographics of nonbelief, according to which patterns in the distribution of belief may themselves be more naturally explained under non-theistic hypotheses than under some forms of theism. Hiddenness is therefore not merely the absence of one piece of evidence. It is evidence that one of the most obvious possible mechanisms for resolving theological uncertainty—clear divine disclosure—has not operated in a way that produces widespread convergence.

2. Inconsistent revelation

The next problem is not simply that religions disagree. Disagreement occurs in every serious domain of inquiry. The epistemically important fact is that traditions claiming access to divine or ultimate revelation reach conclusions that are frequently incompatible at precisely the points where revelation is supposed to supply information unavailable through ordinary inquiry. Different traditions disagree about the number and nature of divine beings, incarnation, prophecy, salvation, scripture, reincarnation, judgment, revelation, ritual obligations, and the ultimate structure of reality. Even within traditions, sincere and intellectually sophisticated interpreters often disagree over the content of the same alleged revelation.

The philosophical literature on religious diversity emphasizes precisely this feature: individuals who appear sincere, informed, and truth-seeking can hold significantly different and mutually incompatible religious beliefs. This matters because revelation is not merely another proposition in the model. It is supposed to function as an epistemic bridge between otherwise inaccessible ultimate reality and human knowledge. If that bridge reliably produced convergence, it could solve much of the model-space problem. Instead, we encounter something closer to

$$ R_1\rightarrow M_1, $$
$$ R_2\rightarrow M_2, $$
$$ R_3\rightarrow M_3, $$

where the alleged revelations support mutually incompatible models. We must therefore ask which revelation is veridical. But any answer seems to require an independent criterion by which the revelations can be evaluated. If revelation \(R_1\) is judged authentic because it coheres with theological model \(M_1\), while \(M_1\) is justified because it is revealed by \(R_1\), the procedure risks circularity. The deeper problem is therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Revelation cannot resolve the model-selection problem unless we possess a reliable way to identify genuine revelation independently of the disputed model.} } $$

The existence of multiple incompatible revelatory traditions does not prove that all are false. It does, however, substantially weaken revelation as a generally available procedure for selecting among ultimist hypotheses.

3. The demographic and cultural contingency of religious belief

Religious belief is also strongly embedded within systems of cultural transmission. Individuals typically encounter religion through families, communities, languages, institutions, and geographically concentrated traditions. This fact alone does not imply that religious beliefs are false. Nearly every human belief is socially mediated to some extent. The relevant question is instead whether the mechanism producing religious confidence appears sufficiently truth-sensitive to warrant the confidence placed in its outputs.

Suppose religious affiliation were almost entirely independent of social environment. Suppose people raised in dramatically different cultures, studying ultimate reality independently, nevertheless converged upon highly specific theological conclusions. That would be epistemically striking. Instead, there is substantial predictability in what religious framework a person initially finds plausible based upon the religious environment into which that person is born. The concern is not:

$$ \text{socially transmitted}\rightarrow\text{false}. $$

That inference would plainly be invalid. The concern is:

$$ \text{strong social dependence} + \text{incompatible outputs} \rightarrow \text{reduced confidence in the mechanism as a discriminator}. $$

Imagine a belief-forming procedure that generates Catholicism in one population, Sunni Islam in another, Hindu traditions in another, and secular naturalism in another, with strong dependence upon local cultural inputs. Before treating any one output as reliable evidence of ultimate reality, we would want some reason to believe that the procedure tracks truth rather than merely reproducing the surrounding informational environment. This consideration becomes especially important when religious conviction itself is later presented as evidence:

“So many people sincerely believe they have encountered the truth of this tradition.”

Sincerity may tell us something about the phenomenology of belief. It does not by itself tell us whether the process generating the belief reliably discriminates metaphysical truth from culturally available interpretation.

4. Religious experience and the problem of calibration

Religious experience might appear to provide a more direct route. Someone experiences what seems to be God, an ultimate consciousness, divine presence, unity with reality, a saint, multiple gods, or some other religious object. To the individual undergoing such an experience, its evidential force can be profound. The difficulty emerges when religious experience is used as a general method of metaphysical identification.

Experiences interpreted within different traditions support significantly different conceptions of ultimate reality. Philosophical discussions of religious experience identify religious diversity and competing naturalistic explanations as major possible defeaters of claims that religious experience straightforwardly possesses the epistemic authority of ordinary perception. Reports can concern an infinitely perfect personal creator, an impersonal consciousness, or even forms of ultimate nothingness. This creates a problem of calibration.

With ordinary perception, we possess extensive mechanisms for determining when perception succeeds and fails. Observers can compare reports. Instruments can independently measure objects. Predictions can be tested. Perceptual systems can be studied under changing conditions. Religious experience does not obviously possess an equivalent external calibration procedure. Suppose a Christian reports an experience interpreted as Christ, while someone from another tradition reports an experience interpreted through an incompatible metaphysical framework. We could respond that both experiences contact the same underlying reality through different cultural interpretations. But that itself introduces another model. We might call it

$$ M_P=\text{religious experiences are culturally mediated encounters with a common ultimate reality}. $$

Why prefer \(M_P\)? The evidence of religious experience alone cannot answer, because \(M_P\) is being introduced precisely to explain the diversity within that evidence. Alternatively, we could claim that one tradition's experiences are veridical while the others are mistaken. But then we again require an independent discriminator. Thus:

$$ \text{experience} \rightarrow \text{interpretation} \rightarrow \text{theological model}. $$

The experience does not uniquely determine the final step. This distinction also explains why personal religious experience may possess a different epistemic status for the experiencer than for an outsider. Philosophical discussions have long recognized the possibility that an experience could rationally affect the person undergoing it without thereby supplying equally strong public justification to other people. Someone may therefore truthfully say:

“My experience makes belief psychologically unavoidable for me.”

That is importantly different from:

“My experience gives you sufficient reason to adopt my metaphysical interpretation.”

For purposes of model selection, the latter is what would be required.

5. Draper's jelly beans and the limits of cumulative evidence

Paul Draper's cumulative case for naturalism illustrates an important response to the demand for certainty. Imagine two jars containing different distributions of colored jelly beans. A bean drawn from either jar might be compatible with both hypotheses, but its color may be much more probable under one jar than the other. Repeated draws can therefore accumulate into strong evidence even though no individual draw logically proves which jar was selected. Draper uses the analogy to motivate a cumulative comparison between naturalism and theism. This is exactly right as a principle of evidential reasoning. The problem lies in transferring the analogy to the full ultimist question. In the jelly-bean case, we know the composition of the jars. That is why the observation has a determinate evidential direction. If

$$ P(R\mid J_1)=0.9 $$

and

$$ P(R\mid J_2)=0.1, $$

then drawing a red bean clearly favors \(J_1\). But with ultimist models, the analogous probabilities are often precisely what we do not know. What proportion of universes generated by a perfectly good personal God should contain extensive evolutionary suffering? What proportion should contain widespread religious disagreement? What proportion should contain embodied consciousness? How frequently would a deistic creator produce fine-tuned universes? What should we expect from pantheism? What is the relevant distribution under polytheistic models? The jelly-bean framework therefore demonstrates how cumulative evidence works once the distributions are sufficiently specified. It does not supply those distributions. Draper's individual comparisons may still have considerable force against specific forms of theism. But the move from

$$ \text{the evidence favors naturalism over }M_i $$

to

$$ \text{the evidence settles naturalism over the larger ultimist space} $$

requires precisely the model-space structure developed in the previous sections. The analogy therefore helps reveal both the strength and the limit of cumulative probabilistic reasoning. If we do not know the composition of the jars, counting beans cannot by itself tell us which jar is favored.

6. Schellenberg and our possible epistemic immaturity

Schellenberg's broader religious skepticism pushes the problem beyond any individual argument. His position concerning ultimism emphasizes the possibility that human beings occupy an extremely early stage in the history of serious inquiry into ultimate reality. In The Wisdom to Doubt, he argues that our cognitive limitations and species-level immaturity support withholding judgment regarding an ultimate salvific reality, rather than treating either religious belief or religious disbelief as established. This possibility has an important consequence for the present argument. Our existing conceptual options may not merely contain uncertainty. They may be historically immature. We routinely assume that the metaphysical alternatives worth taking seriously are those currently available to us:

$$ \{\text{classical theism, naturalism, pantheism, deism},\ldots\}. $$

But why assume that a species at our point in intellectual history has already generated the most important possible conceptions of ultimate reality? Schellenberg's developmental skepticism explicitly leaves open the possibility that more adequate conceptions of the divine or ultimate could emerge only after substantial future intellectual development. If that is right, then unconceived alternatives are not merely a technical possibility. They become a substantive reason for epistemic modesty. The relevant model space contains not only

$$ M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_n $$

but potentially

$$ M_{n+1},M_{n+2},\ldots $$

where some future models may reorganize the problem in ways we currently cannot anticipate. This consideration cuts in both directions. It weakens confident traditional theism. But it also weakens confidence that metaphysical naturalism exhausts the serious alternative. The appropriate response is therefore not straightforward atheism but greater uncertainty about the adequacy of the entire presently conceived hypothesis space.

7. Persistent disagreement as higher-order evidence

Finally, there is the persistence of sophisticated disagreement itself. This argument must be formulated carefully. The fact that intelligent people disagree about a proposition does not entail that the proposition has no correct answer. Nor does disagreement imply that every position is equally reasonable. The relevant point is higher-order.

After centuries of philosophical and theological inquiry, individuals with substantial familiarity with essentially the same major arguments continue to reach radically different conclusions concerning theism, atheism, religious traditions, divine attributes, revelation, and the evidential significance of the same observations. Contemporary epistemology of religion continues to treat disagreement as one of its central problems. Persistent disagreement therefore gives us some information about the reliability of our current methods for resolving the question. Suppose a decision procedure repeatedly produced convergence among competent investigators. That would provide some reason to trust the procedure. Suppose instead that equally informed investigators apply apparently reasonable standards and obtain dramatically different conclusions:

$$ P(G)\approx0.95, $$
$$ P(G)\approx0.50, $$
$$ P(G)\approx0.05. $$

This does not tell us which estimate is correct. But it should reduce our confidence that the available argumentative machinery determines one of them with anything approaching the precision often claimed. The disagreement is therefore not direct evidence for atheism. It is higher-order evidence about our epistemic position.

The cumulative point

Each consideration can be answered individually. A theology can propose reasons for hiddenness. A religious tradition can explain why other revelations are mistaken. The dependence of religion on culture can be compared with the social transmission of true scientific beliefs. Religious experience can be defended as prima facie trustworthy. Draper's naturalistic comparisons can be disputed. Schellenberg's developmental skepticism can be challenged. Persistent disagreement can coexist with truth. The argument for strong agnosticism does not require denying any of this. The question is what happens when the considerations are evaluated together.

We begin with a large and open-ended metaphysical model space. The considerations surveyed above then add a second layer of difficulty: the principal mechanisms that might have narrowed that space are themselves contested. Their cumulative role can be summarized as follows.

Consideration What it undercuts
Models often lack determinate likelihoods Confidence that generic ultimism has a well-defined predictive profile.
Divine psychology is poorly constrained Confidence in inferences from divine attributes to what a divine agent would probably choose.
Revelation generates incompatible outputs Revelation as a generally available, model-independent selector among rival theologies.
Religious affiliation is strongly culturally mediated Confidence that religious belief formation is sufficiently truth-sensitive to serve as an independent discriminator.
Religious experience lacks a clear external calibration procedure The move from powerful experience to a uniquely warranted metaphysical interpretation.
Cumulative evidence depends upon disputed model distributions The assumption that many local evidential comparisons automatically yield a global ranking of ultimist models.
Important alternatives may remain unconceived Confidence that the present taxonomy exhausts the serious possibilities.
Competent investigators continue to disagree about how the evidence should be interpreted Confidence in the resolving power of our current argumentative methods.

None of these propositions individually entails agnosticism. Their cumulative significance is that the ordinary mechanisms by which we might hope to collapse the hypothesis space onto one privileged model are themselves uncertain. This is what makes them undercutters.

They do not merely place evidence in the atheistic column of a ledger. They call into question whether the ledger has been constructed correctly, whether its categories are exhaustive, and whether the evidential weights assigned to its entries are independently justified. The resulting problem is therefore deeper than a contest between arguments for and against God. It is a problem concerning our ability to identify the correct metaphysical model in the first place.

And once that problem is recognized, the appropriate epistemic state may no longer be ordinary uncertainty between two familiar competitors. It begins to resemble something much more severe: deep epistemic underdetermination.

VI. From Ordinary Uncertainty to Deep Epistemic Underdetermination

The cumulative effect of the previous sections is not simply that the evidence for and against ultimism is mixed. That would still leave us within an ordinary probabilistic framework: several well-defined hypotheses, each with uncertain support, competing for posterior probability. The situation appears more difficult than that.

In ordinary uncertainty, the basic structure of the problem is already known. We know what the relevant models are, we know how they are parameterized, and we possess some justified way of assigning probabilities over them. The uncertainty concerns which model is true. Schematically,

$$ \{M_1,M_2,M_3\} $$

may be fixed while we remain uncertain about

$$ P(M_1),\quad P(M_2),\quad P(M_3). $$

The problem concerning ultimism is more fundamental. We are uncertain not merely about which model is true, but about:

  • which models belong in the relevant space;
  • whether important models remain unconceived;
  • how those models should be individuated;
  • how probability should be distributed across them;
  • which parameters govern their predictions; and
  • whether our principal sources of religious evidence reliably discriminate among them.

This is better described as deep epistemic underdetermination. The distinction matters because it changes what our uncertainty represents. Ordinary uncertainty might justify a claim such as

$$ P(G)=0.5. $$

Deep underdetermination does not necessarily justify even that. To assign

$$ P(G)=0.5 $$

is already to claim a considerable amount of epistemic structure. It suggests that we possess a meaningful probability space in which the proposition \(G\) occupies half of the relevant probability mass. But if the boundaries of \(G\) are themselves unclear, if the space of alternatives is open-ended, if the internal weighting of theological models is unspecified, and if their likelihood functions depend upon contested assumptions about divine psychology, then assigning a precise probability may express more confidence in the representation of the problem than the evidence warrants. The appropriate conclusion is therefore not

$$ P(U\mid E)=0.5, $$

but something closer to:

$$ \boxed{ P(U\mid E)\text{ is poorly identified by our present epistemic framework.} } $$

This is an important distinction. A poorly identified proposition is not necessarily one whose probability is near the middle. It is one for which substantially different reasonable model specifications can generate substantially different assessments without the available evidence clearly determining which specification should govern. One investigator may construct the problem in a way that yields

$$ P(U\mid E)\approx0.8. $$

Another may reasonably obtain

$$ P(U\mid E)\approx0.2. $$

A third may reject the attempt to assign a precise value altogether. One formal way to represent this kind of situation is to use imprecise probabilities rather than force a single exact credence. Instead of pretending that one uniquely justified probability function has been identified, we may represent the admissible epistemic states by a set

$$ \mathcal{K}=\{P_1,P_2,\ldots,P_k\}, $$

where each member reflects a defensible specification of the model space, priors, or likelihoods. The important question then becomes not merely what one selected probability function says, but which conclusions remain stable across the admissible set. This does not abandon probabilistic reasoning. It makes the uncertainty about the probability model itself explicit and provides a natural bridge to the robustness analysis in Part II.

The mere existence of disagreement is not what establishes underdetermination. The important point is that the disagreement can arise from unresolved choices about the structure of the inference itself: which models count, how they are weighted, which likelihoods they generate, and which forms of evidence are trustworthy. This is the distinction between local evidential force and global model identification. Evidence can discriminate meaningfully between two specified models without thereby determining how the entire open-ended model space should be partitioned or weighted. This also explains why individual arguments can remain locally significant while failing to settle the larger question. Divine hiddenness may strongly disconfirm a particular relational God-model. Fine-tuning may raise the plausibility of some creator models relative to some naturalistic alternatives. Draper's cumulative evidence may favor naturalism over a sufficiently specified form of perfect-being theism. Religious experience may increase an individual's confidence in some form of ultimism.

None of this requires us to deny that evidence exists. The stronger claim is that these evidential movements do not automatically aggregate into a determinate answer to the much broader question:

$$ \text{What, if anything, is ultimately real?} $$

The available arguments operate conditionally within particular model specifications. They do not currently provide a universally compelling procedure for selecting the specification itself. This is why strong agnosticism should not be confused with mere indecision. Indecision might mean that the evidence is finely balanced. Strong agnosticism begins from a different diagnosis:

$$ \boxed{ \text{the evidential problem is underdetermined at the level of model construction itself.} } $$

That diagnosis also explains why additional argumentation does not necessarily resolve the problem. More arguments can shift credences within a particular framework while leaving the framework itself contested. If one philosopher evaluates suffering under classical perfect-being theism while another regards divine purposes as inscrutable, additional examples of suffering may not settle their disagreement. If one person treats religious experience as prima facie veridical while another regards cultural variation as a major defeater, additional reports may simply be absorbed into their respective models. The disagreement persists because the contested assumptions are upstream of the evidence being accumulated. This gives us the first major conclusion of the essay:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Our present evidence does not provide a sufficiently robust and model-independent procedure for deciding among the relevant ultimist possibilities.} } $$

This conclusion does not entail atheism. It does not entail theism. It does not entail that ultimate reality is unknowable in principle. It establishes a claim about our present epistemic position: we currently lack sufficient grounds for high-confidence metaphysical identification. At this point, however, an important challenge arises. Someone may accept everything said so far and respond:

“Perhaps we cannot know. Perhaps the evidence does not decide the issue. But knowledge is not required for faith. We still have to choose how to live.”

That response changes the nature of the problem. The question is no longer merely what the evidence establishes. It becomes:

$$ \boxed{ \text{How should a rational agent act when the epistemic problem itself is deeply underdetermined?} } $$

It is at this point that the argument for strong agnosticism must move from epistemology to decision theory.

Part IIRational Commitment Under Deep Uncertainty

The second part asks what rational action requires once deep epistemic underdetermination is granted. It shifts from the truth of a metaphysical proposition to the rationality of commitment, develops a robust decision procedure, and evaluates religious commitment through optionality, reversibility, value of information, and exposure to model error.

VII. “We Don’t Know, But We Believe”

At this stage, a theist might accept much of the preceding argument without abandoning religious commitment. The response may be something like:

“Perhaps the evidence does not decide the question. Perhaps no one can prove that God exists. But faith was never supposed to be certainty. We do not know; we believe.”

As a description of a person’s psychological state, there may be nothing incoherent about this response. Human beings routinely hold beliefs under uncertainty. Indeed, very few of our beliefs are supported by anything approaching certainty. But as a normative reply, the response is incomplete. The moment the claim becomes

“Even if the evidence is underdetermined, one should still believe,”

or

“One should still commit to a religious life,”

the problem has changed. We are no longer asking only what the evidence supports. We are asking what a rational agent should do given a particular epistemic state. That is a decision problem. The distinction is important because evidence and action are related but not identical. Suppose two agents possess exactly the same evidence concerning some uncertain proposition \(H\). They therefore assign the same degree of confidence to it. Yet they face radically different consequences depending on how they act. For one agent, mistakenly acting as though \(H\) were true carries almost no cost. For the other, the same mistake carries catastrophic consequences. It would be entirely possible for both agents to share the same epistemic state while rationally choosing different actions. In abstract form:

$$ \text{same evidence} \not\Rightarrow \text{same rational action}. $$

Rational action depends not only upon what one believes about the world, but upon the consequences attached to acting under those beliefs. This is usually taken for granted in ordinary reasoning. An investor does not ask only:

$$ \text{“Which asset do I think will perform best?”} $$

but also:

$$ \text{“How uncertain am I, and what happens if I am wrong?”} $$

A military planner does not ask only which scenario seems most likely. The planner also considers robustness across alternative scenarios, asymmetric downside, reversibility, and exposure to catastrophic failure. An engineer does not merely identify the most probable stress level a structure will encounter. Safety margins exist because decision-making must account for the consequences of uncertainty. The same principle applies here. If Part I is correct, then our epistemic situation concerning ultimism is characterized not merely by uncertainty, but by deep uncertainty about the relevant models, their likelihoods, and the mechanisms by which they might be identified. The practical question is therefore not simply:

$$ \text{“Which theological proposition seems most plausible?”} $$

It is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{“What degree and form of commitment is rational under this particular structure of uncertainty?”} } $$

That question cannot be answered by repeating the original arguments for and against God. It requires an additional theory connecting epistemic uncertainty to rational action.

Belief and commitment must be distinguished

This distinction becomes especially important because belief itself may not be directly voluntary. If doxastic voluntarism is false, then a person cannot simply decide:

$$ \text{“I will now believe Christianity is true.”} $$

in the same direct sense that one can decide to attend church, donate money, marry within a religious community, or structure one’s life around a religious moral code. Belief may instead arise from a complex interaction of evidence, personal experience, temperament, social environment, upbringing, repeated exposure, and interpretive habits. That possibility matters because it changes the object of decision theory. The relevant decision need not be

$$ A=\{\text{believe},\text{disbelieve}\}. $$

It can instead be represented as

$$ A= \{ \text{commit to }R_1, \text{commit to }R_2, \ldots, \text{maintain agnostic inquiry} \}. $$

Here, “commitment” includes the practical and social consequences of treating a religious model as action-guiding. These may include:

  • worship and ritual practice;
  • community membership and acceptance of religious authority;
  • moral obligations and financial contributions;
  • marriage, family, and child-rearing decisions; and
  • deliberate cultivation of faith.

These are decisions even if belief itself is not directly chosen. This allows the argument to remain neutral on the philosophical controversy over doxastic voluntarism. If belief is not voluntary, then the demand that someone simply “choose belief” is already psychologically questionable. If belief can be cultivated through voluntary practices, then those practices themselves become appropriate objects of decision-theoretic evaluation. Either way, the practical problem remains.

Psychological explanation is not normative justification

This also clarifies the status of socially conditioned or experiential belief. A person may say:

“I believe because of the experiences I have had.”

Or:

“I simply find belief compelling.”

Or:

“My religious upbringing made this worldview seem natural to me.”

All of these may be accurate explanations of why the person believes. But explanation and justification are different. From

$$ \text{“This process caused me to believe }M\text{”} $$

it does not follow that

$$ \text{“A rational outsider should therefore adopt }M\text{.”} $$

This is especially important for religious experience. An experience may have overwhelming subjective force for the person undergoing it. It may even make disbelief psychologically difficult or impossible for that individual. But if the experience cannot reliably discriminate among competing metaphysical interpretations, then its personal persuasiveness does not automatically translate into public evidential force. The same distinction applies more broadly:

$$ \text{cause of belief} \neq \text{evidence for belief}, $$

and

$$ \text{evidence for belief} \neq \text{sufficient reason for costly commitment}. $$

These three levels should not be collapsed.

The missing step in religious argument

Religious discussions often move rapidly from evidence to commitment. An argument may establish, at most, something like:

$$ E\rightarrow \text{some increase in support for }M. $$

But the practical conclusion is often much stronger:

$$ \text{therefore commit to }M. $$

The intermediate reasoning is rarely made explicit. Yet a rational transition from evidential support to commitment should depend upon a set of further questions:

  1. How strong is the evidence?
  2. How robust is it across competing models?
  3. How sensitive is the conclusion to disputed assumptions?
  4. What is the cost of false commitment?
  5. What is the cost of withholding commitment?
  6. How reversible is the decision?
  7. Can further information be acquired?
  8. Does commitment itself alter future evidence evaluation?

These questions are standard in serious decision-making under uncertainty. They should not disappear simply because the subject matter is religious. In fact, if the epistemic uncertainty is as deep as Part I suggests, they become more important.

Uncertainty does not automatically license commitment

There is a temptation to move from

$$ \text{“Reason cannot decide”} $$

to

$$ \text{“Therefore faith is permissible.”} $$

But this inference is not automatic. The failure of evidence to uniquely determine an answer does not by itself tell us what practical response is rational. Sometimes uncertainty justifies action. Sometimes it justifies delay. Sometimes it justifies precaution. Sometimes it justifies diversification. Sometimes it justifies gathering more information. Sometimes it justifies accepting risk. The appropriate response depends upon the structure of the decision problem. Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{epistemic underdetermination does not itself determine a practical policy.} } $$

This point is crucial. Part I alone does not establish strong agnosticism. It establishes an epistemic condition. A second argument is needed to show what form of commitment is rational given that condition.

The decision-theoretic question

The central problem can now be stated more precisely. Suppose an agent confronts the ultimist model space

$$ U=\{M_1,M_2,M_3,\ldots\} $$

under the conditions established previously:

  • the model space may be incomplete;
  • probability weights over the models are poorly justified;
  • many likelihoods depend upon uncertain theories of divine psychology;
  • revelation and religious experience do not uniquely identify one model;
  • culturally mediated belief formation produces incompatible outcomes;
  • and additional unconceived alternatives may remain possible.

The agent nevertheless has to decide how much practical commitment to place upon one region of that space. What rule should govern that decision? A familiar answer is Pascalian: under uncertainty, the possible consequences of religious belief may be so large that commitment is rational even when the evidence is weak. But that proposal immediately encounters the very model-space problem developed in Part I. Claims such as

“What if you are wrong and go to hell?”

do not follow from generic ultimism. They follow from highly specific theological models involving particular doctrines of revelation, judgment, salvation, punishment, and the role of belief. To treat those consequences as decision-relevant without first justifying the model that generates them risks importing a theology into the decision matrix before the decision has even been made. The next stage of the argument therefore begins with a more basic question:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Can ordinary expected-utility reasoning select a religious commitment when the theological state space itself is deeply uncertain?} } $$

The answer, I will argue, is no. Once the model-space problem is carried into the decision-theoretic domain, Pascal-style reasoning does not solve the uncertainty. It reproduces it inside the utility calculation.

VIII. Why Pascal-Style Reasoning Fails Under Model Uncertainty

Pascal-style arguments are attractive because they appear to bypass the evidential impasse. Even if the existence of God cannot be established with confidence, perhaps the consequences of being wrong are sufficiently large that religious commitment remains rational. The familiar form of the wager can be represented with a simple decision matrix:

God exists God does not exist
Commit to belief enormous or infinite gain finite cost
Do not commit enormous or infinite loss finite gain

If the potential reward associated with belief is infinite, then even a very small positive probability of God may appear sufficient to make belief rational. The appeal of this argument is understandable. It recognizes something the purely evidential debate often neglects: action depends upon consequences as well as probabilities. But the simplicity of the wager is purchased by assuming almost the entire theological structure that the decision was supposed to help us navigate. The proposition

$$ G=\text{“God exists”} $$

does not imply

$$ \text{believers receive eternal reward}. $$

Nor does it imply

$$ \text{nonbelievers receive eternal punishment}. $$

Those conclusions require a much more specific hypothesis. Something closer to

$$ M_C= \{ \text{a particular God exists}, \text{a particular revelation is authentic}, \text{a particular soteriology is correct}, \text{belief is salvifically relevant}, \text{nonbelief risks punishment} \}. $$

The traditional wager therefore does not compare belief in generic ultimism with disbelief in generic ultimism. It compares actions under a highly specific theological model with actions under a comparatively undifferentiated alternative. That asymmetry does most of the work.

The state space has already been chosen

Decision theory requires a set of possible states of the world. Let

$$ \Theta=\{\theta_1,\theta_2,\ldots,\theta_n\}. $$

An action \(a\) receives some utility in each possible state:

$$ U(a,\theta). $$

Under ordinary expected utility, the agent then evaluates

$$ EU(a) = \sum_{\theta\in\Theta} P(\theta)U(a,\theta). $$

The crucial point is that both

$$ \Theta $$

and

$$ P(\theta) $$

must be specified before the expected utilities can be calculated. But Part I argued that the relevant ultimist state space is precisely what remains uncertain. Instead of

$$ \Theta= \{\text{Christian God},\text{no God}\}, $$

the relevant space is more plausibly something like

$$ \Theta= \{ M_1,M_2,M_3,\ldots,M_n,\ldots,N_1,N_2,\ldots \}, $$

where the \(M_i\) represent different ultimist models and the \(N_i\) represent different non-ultimist or naturalistic models. Among the ultimist possibilities might be:

$$ M_1=\text{a Christian exclusivist God}, $$
$$ M_2=\text{a Muslim exclusivist God}, $$
$$ M_3=\text{a universalist God}, $$
$$ M_4=\text{a deistic creator indifferent to worship}, $$
$$ M_5=\text{a God who values sincere inquiry over profession of faith}, $$
$$ M_6=\text{an ultimist reality for which propositional belief is irrelevant}, $$
$$ M_7=\text{a polytheistic order}, $$
$$ M_8=\text{an unknown divine model not represented by existing religions}. $$

This list could be extended indefinitely. Once these possibilities are admitted, the wager changes dramatically. The action

$$ a_C=\text{commit to Christianity} $$

may receive enormous utility under \(M_1\). But it may receive lower utility under \(M_2\). It may make almost no difference under \(M_3\). It may be completely irrelevant under \(M_4\). And it may even be negatively evaluated under a model in which ultimate reality values intellectual restraint or condemns false religious certainty. The same is true of every other specific commitment. The decision problem is therefore not:

$$ \text{belief versus nonbelief}. $$

It is:

$$ \text{which policy performs best across a radically uncertain space of possible ultimate realities?} $$

“What if you go to hell?” is itself a model-dependent claim

This becomes particularly clear in the familiar challenge:

“But what if you are wrong and go to hell?”

The rhetorical force of the question comes from treating hell as though it were a generic consequence of being mistaken about God. It is not. For hell to enter the decision matrix, several further propositions must be true. For example:

$$ H_1=\text{there exists an afterlife}, $$
$$ H_2=\text{some persons are subject to postmortem punishment}, $$
$$ H_3=\text{the punishment is severe or eternal}, $$
$$ H_4=\text{religious belief or commitment affects one's fate}, $$
$$ H_5=\text{the relevant doctrine of salvation is correctly identified}, $$

and perhaps

$$ H_6=\text{sincere agnosticism does not exempt the agent}. $$

The warning therefore has the structure

$$ M_C+H_1+\cdots+H_6 \rightarrow \text{agnosticism risks hell}. $$

But why should this particular conjunction receive sufficient probability to determine the decision? That is exactly the theological question still under dispute. The wager cannot answer by saying:

“Because the consequences are enormous.”

Magnitude of consequence and probability of the generating model are different variables. A sufficiently enormous payoff can dominate expected value only after the state producing that payoff has been assigned an appropriate probability. The payoff itself cannot establish that probability. Otherwise arbitrary imagined catastrophes would dominate every decision.

The many-gods problem is really a model-space problem

The traditional “many gods” objection points out that Pascal's Wager seems to favor one religious commitment only because it neglects competing religions. But the deeper issue is not merely that there are several historically existing religions. The deeper issue is that the space of possible reward structures is unconstrained. Suppose one proposes:

$$ M_A=\text{God infinitely rewards Christian commitment}. $$

If bare possibility is sufficient to enter the matrix, then we can equally formulate

$$ M_B=\text{God infinitely rewards sincere agnosticism}, $$

or

$$ M_C=\text{God infinitely punishes belief adopted for prudential reasons}, $$

or

$$ M_D=\text{God rewards only persons who refuse to claim knowledge without sufficient evidence}. $$

Nothing in generic ultimism selects among these possibilities. This does not mean that all such models should receive equal probability. That would be an unnecessary and implausible claim. The point is instead methodological:

$$ \boxed{ \text{a decision procedure cannot privilege one theological payoff structure unless there are independent reasons to privilege the model generating it.} } $$

If those independent reasons exist, then they—not the wager—are doing the decisive work. If they do not exist, then the wager cannot manufacture them.

Infinite utilities make the problem worse

The problem becomes even more severe when infinite rewards or punishments are introduced. Suppose the agent assigns some nonzero probability to

$$ M_1=\text{Christian commitment receives }+\infty. $$

The Pascalian response is that any positive probability multiplied by infinite utility dominates finite alternatives. But now suppose the agent must also admit some nonzero probability for

$$ M_2=\text{Christian commitment receives }-\infty, $$

because another possible divine model infinitely punishes precisely that commitment. Then the expected utility of Christianity contains both

$$ +\infty $$

and

$$ -\infty. $$

The ordering of the action becomes unstable or undefined. The same construction can be applied to agnosticism, Islam, atheism, Hindu practice, or almost any other policy. If the model space is sufficiently unconstrained, then for almost every action \(a\) we can formulate states such that

$$ U(a,\theta_i)=+\infty $$

and other states such that

$$ U(a,\theta_j)=-\infty. $$

Infinite utility therefore does not rescue the wager. It amplifies the consequences of model uncertainty until the ordinary expected-value ordering may collapse altogether. A threat of infinite punishment is decision-theoretically powerful only if we already possess reason to privilege the theology that contains it. Without that prior work,

$$ \boxed{ \text{infinite stakes do not compensate for an unidentified state space.} } $$

The problem affects both probabilities and utilities

Part I focused primarily on uncertainty about

$$ P(\theta). $$

The decision-theoretic problem is deeper because the utility function is also model-dependent. In ordinary decision analysis, we might know roughly what counts as a gain or loss while remaining uncertain about which state will occur. Religious wagers complicate this because the theological model may determine the payoff itself. Under one model:

$$ U(\text{faithful commitment},M_1)=+\infty. $$

Under another:

$$ U(\text{faithful commitment},M_2)=0. $$

Under another:

$$ U(\text{faithful commitment},M_3)<0. $$

Thus both principal ingredients of expected utility are unstable:

$$ \underbrace{P(\theta)}_{\text{deeply uncertain}} $$

and

$$ \underbrace{U(a,\theta)}_{\text{theology-dependent}}. $$

This is not ordinary risk. It is uncertainty about the probability distribution and uncertainty about the payoff-generating structure.

Expected utility does not fail; its inputs fail

The conclusion should not be that expected-utility theory is incoherent. The problem is more specific. Expected utility is extremely useful when the relevant states, probabilities, and utilities can be specified with sufficient confidence. But in the ultimist case, the very conditions required for that calculation are what remain contested. The problem can therefore be summarized as:

$$ \boxed{ \text{The wager does not solve theological uncertainty because its decision matrix presupposes a theological model.} } $$

Or more formally:

$$ \text{expected utility} = f(\Theta,P,U), $$

but Part I gives us substantial uncertainty concerning

$$ \Theta, $$
$$ P, $$

and, in the religious case,

$$ U $$

itself. The resulting decision problem cannot be solved merely by inserting one culturally familiar theology into the state space and treating all alternatives as “no God.”

What follows from the failure of the wager?

Importantly, none of this yet proves that agnostic commitment is rationally preferable. It only blocks one familiar route from uncertainty to religious commitment. The failure of ordinary expected utility under an inadequately specified model does not automatically imply:

$$ \text{therefore do nothing}. $$

That would repeat the very mistake criticized in the previous section. We instead need a decision rule suited to circumstances in which the relevant probabilities are not uniquely known and the model structure itself is uncertain. The appropriate framework must ask questions different from

$$ \text{“Which action maximizes expected utility under my preferred theological model?”} $$

It must instead ask something closer to:

$$ \text{“Which policy remains defensible across a wide range of plausible models without depending excessively upon unsupported assumptions?”} $$

This is the transition from optimization under risk to robust decision-making under deep uncertainty. And it is here that the positive case for strong agnosticism begins.

IX. The Robust Commitment Principle

The failure of Pascal-style reasoning leaves an important question unresolved. If ordinary expected-utility maximization cannot be cleanly applied because the relevant theological state space, probabilities, and payoff structures are themselves deeply uncertain, what principle should guide action instead? The answer cannot simply be:

$$ \text{“when uncertain, do nothing.”} $$

That would be far too crude. In many contexts, uncertainty gives us reasons to act quickly. In others, it gives us reasons to hedge, diversify, delay, gather information, or preserve flexibility. The rational response depends upon the structure of the uncertainty and the costs attached to the available options. What is needed, then, is not a rule favoring inaction, but a rule governing commitment under deep uncertainty. I will call this the Robust Commitment Principle:

When an agent faces deep uncertainty concerning the relevant models, probabilities, and consequences, and no option robustly dominates the alternatives, rational commitment should be proportionate to the robustness of the available justification. All else equal, agents should prefer policies that reduce unsupported model-specific exposure, preserve reversibility, maintain access to further information, and remain responsive to future evidence.

The principle is intentionally general: it does not mention religion or atheism, and it does not privilege disbelief. It is meant to describe a broader feature of rational decision-making whenever the agent lacks confidence not merely in which outcome will occur, but in the model used to represent the decision itself.

Robustness rather than optimization

Under ordinary risk, an agent may be able to optimize relative to a reasonably well-specified probability distribution. If the states are

$$ \theta_1,\theta_2,\ldots,\theta_n $$

and their probabilities are known well enough, then maximizing

$$ EU(a) = \sum_i P(\theta_i)U(a,\theta_i) $$

may be appropriate. But suppose several substantially different probability models are defensible. Let

$$ \mathcal{P} = \{ P_1,P_2,\ldots,P_k \} $$

represent a set of admissible probability assignments. Then the ranking of actions may vary across the set:

$$ EU_{P_1}(a)>EU_{P_1}(b), $$

while

$$ EU_{P_2}(b)>EU_{P_2}(a). $$

An action that appears optimal under one specification may become unattractive under another. This is exactly the kind of sensitivity that should matter to a rational decision-maker. If the recommendation

$$ a^* $$

depends upon selecting one highly contestable model from a much larger set of admissible alternatives, then confidence in

$$ a^* $$

should be correspondingly reduced. Robust decision-making therefore asks a different question from ordinary optimization. Instead of asking only

$$ \text{“Which action is best under my chosen model?”} $$

it asks

$$ \boxed{ \text{“Which action remains defensible across a broad range of reasonable model specifications?”} } $$

That difference is fundamental. An action can have a very high expected payoff under one model while being extremely fragile to uncertainty about the model itself. A more robust action may sacrifice some upside under the favored model while performing acceptably across many alternatives. This is familiar in strategic reasoning. A military plan that succeeds spectacularly only if one uncertain intelligence estimate is exactly correct may be less rational than a plan that performs adequately under several plausible enemy responses.

An investment strategy concentrated entirely in one uncertain asset may have the highest expected return under one forecast but expose the investor to enormous model error. An engineering design optimized precisely for one estimated stress distribution may be less rational than a design with margins sufficient to tolerate uncertainty in those estimates. In each case, the rational virtue is not maximum confidence. It is robustness to being wrong about the model.

Model-specific exposure

This suggests the first important component of the principle. Call the dependence of an action upon one particular model its model-specific exposure. Suppose action \(a\) is highly attractive under

$$ M_1 $$

but unattractive under

$$ M_2,M_3,M_4,\ldots. $$

Then choosing \(a\) amounts to concentrating one's practical commitments on the assumption that \(M_1\) is approximately correct. We can represent this informally as

$$ \text{exposure}(a) \propto \text{dependence of }a\text{'s justification on a narrow region of model space}. $$

High model-specific exposure is not automatically irrational. If the evidence strongly favors \(M_1\), concentrated commitment may be entirely appropriate. But when the evidence does not robustly distinguish \(M_1\) from alternatives, concentrated exposure becomes harder to justify. This yields a general principle:

$$ \boxed{ \text{The weaker the model identification, the stronger the case against highly model-dependent commitment.} } $$

Or, conversely,

$$ \boxed{ \text{The stronger the commitment, the stronger the evidential burden required to justify its model dependence.} } $$

This is the principle of proportional commitment.

Proportionality between evidence and commitment

Rational agents routinely vary the magnitude of commitment according to the strength of their information. Suppose an investor has weak evidence that an unfamiliar company will outperform the market. A small experimental position may be reasonable. Investing one's entire savings would require far stronger justification. The distinction is not between belief and disbelief. It is between degrees of practical exposure. Similarly, suppose a physician has moderate evidence that a low-risk diagnostic test may be useful. Ordering the test may be rational even when the diagnosis remains uncertain. A highly invasive and irreversible treatment would require a much higher evidential threshold. The structure is:

$$ \text{greater cost or irreversibility} \rightarrow \text{higher required justification}. $$

This idea should apply equally to worldview commitments. If a metaphysical proposition has little practical consequence, modest evidence may be enough to make tentative acceptance harmless. But if accepting that proposition structures large parts of one's life, relationships, morality, finances, identity, and future decisions, then the relevant commitment is larger. A larger commitment reasonably demands more robust justification. Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{commitment should scale with evidential robustness, not merely with psychological conviction.} } $$

This is especially important because subjective confidence and evidential robustness can come apart. A person may feel extremely certain because of upbringing, community reinforcement, or personal experience while the underlying model remains poorly discriminated from alternatives. Decision theory should track the latter, not merely the former.

Reversibility

A second major virtue is reversibility. Suppose two actions have similar expected value under current information, but one can easily be reversed later while the other cannot. If future evidence may improve, the reversible option possesses a structural advantage. Let

$$ a_R=\text{reversible action} $$

and

$$ a_I=\text{irreversible action}. $$

If both perform comparably now, but future information may distinguish the relevant states, then

$$ a_R $$

preserves the ability to update while

$$ a_I $$

forecloses it. This matters because rational action occurs through time. The agent is not making one isolated choice from a timeless perspective. The agent expects to encounter additional information, experiences, arguments, and evidence. A policy that preserves responsiveness to those future inputs has value. In simplified form:

$$ \text{uncertainty} + \text{possible future learning} + \text{irreversibility} \rightarrow \text{value of delay or flexibility}. $$

This is the logic behind option value. The option to revise later is itself valuable when current information is poor.

Information preservation

Closely related is the value of maintaining access to information. Suppose commitment to some action makes it more difficult to acquire or impartially evaluate future evidence. Then the action has an additional epistemic cost. Consider two policies. Under the first,

$$ a_1, $$

the agent remains exposed to multiple competing perspectives and can revise beliefs as new evidence appears. Under the second,

$$ a_2, $$

the agent enters a social or institutional environment in which alternative interpretations become increasingly costly to entertain. Even if the two policies initially appear similar, they differ in their effect on future inquiry. This suggests another principle:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Under deep uncertainty, policies that preserve epistemic flexibility have prima facie value.} } $$

Again, this is not an absolute rule. Sometimes circumstances require commitment before all information is available. But when urgency is low and continued learning is possible, preserving access to information is rationally attractive.

Sensitivity to assumptions

A fourth virtue is low sensitivity to arbitrary modeling choices. Suppose an action is recommended only if we choose

$$ P(M_1)=0.31, $$

but the recommendation reverses if we instead choose

$$ P(M_1)=0.29. $$

If there is no strong reason to privilege one assignment over the other, then the action recommendation is fragile. Likewise, suppose a theological commitment appears optimal only if we assume

$$ P(\text{nonbelief punished}\mid M)=0.8, $$

but becomes suboptimal under several equally plausible assumptions about divine judgment. The sensitivity of the recommendation is itself epistemically relevant. We can express the ideal roughly as:

$$ \text{small changes in weakly justified assumptions} \not\Rightarrow \text{large changes in recommended policy}. $$

A robust policy should survive reasonable perturbations in the uncertain parts of the model. This is especially important in the present argument because Part I identified numerous such uncertain quantities:

$$ P(M_i\mid U), $$
$$ P(\theta\mid M_i), $$
$$ P(E\mid M_i,\theta), $$

and even the boundaries of the model space itself. If a religious commitment can be justified only by selecting one narrow configuration of these assumptions, then its decision-theoretic support is correspondingly weak.

Avoiding false precision

The principle also guards against a common error: acting as though poorly justified probability assignments were more precise than they really are. Suppose our information supports only the claim

$$ P(M)\in[0.2,0.7]. $$

Replacing that interval with

$$ P(M)=0.46 $$

does not improve the information. It merely hides the uncertainty. A rational decision procedure should preserve uncertainty when the evidence does not eliminate it. The same applies at the level of models. If several model specifications remain admissible, the decision should not silently collapse them into one merely because optimization is easier after doing so. This is one reason strong agnosticism should not be represented as

$$ P(U)=0.5. $$

The point is not to replace one unjustified precise credence with another. It is to allow the structure of uncertainty itself to constrain the degree of commitment.

Universal applicability

An important test of the Robust Commitment Principle is whether it generalizes beyond religion. If the principle had been constructed solely to produce agnosticism, then the objection that it privileges nonbelief would be serious. But the same reasoning appears across ordinary and strategic decision-making. Under severe financial uncertainty, an agent may diversify rather than concentrate. Under uncertainty about future demand, a firm may delay an irreversible investment. Under uncertainty about structural loads, an engineer may build in safety margins. Under uncertainty about an emerging threat, a policymaker may choose a precautionary measure whose cost is low relative to the possible downside. Under uncertainty about which explanation is correct, a scientist may preserve multiple hypotheses rather than prematurely collapsing the inquiry onto one model. The outputs differ. Sometimes the principle recommends action. Sometimes restraint. Sometimes diversification. Sometimes information gathering. Sometimes immediate precaution. The common feature is not inactivity. The common feature is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{do not expose yourself more heavily to model error than your information justifies.} } $$

That is why the principle is neutral with respect to its conclusions.

Consistency and relabeling

Another virtue is consistency. Suppose we confront two structurally identical decision problems. In the first, the favored hypothesis is culturally familiar. In the second, it is unfamiliar. A rational decision procedure should not recommend stronger commitment in the first case merely because the hypothesis has a familiar name. Call this relabeling invariance. If

$$ M_1 $$

and

$$ M_2 $$

occupy structurally equivalent positions in a decision problem, merely replacing the label “Christianity” with “Model 1” should not change the reasoning. This matters because cultural familiarity can easily masquerade as prior plausibility. A theological model may feel more natural not because the evidence uniquely identifies it, but because the agent has been exposed to it repeatedly since childhood. A robust decision principle should attempt to separate such psychological salience from structural justification. Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{rational commitment should depend on evidential and decision-relevant structure, not on cultural familiarity.} } $$

The principle does not require extreme pessimism

A possible concern is that robust decision-making collapses into extreme conservatism. If we always worry about unknown models, perhaps no substantial action is ever justified. But the Robust Commitment Principle does not say that. It is conditional upon the structure of the problem. If one action robustly dominates across the plausible model space, then strong action may be justified even under uncertainty. If delay carries severe and asymmetric costs, immediate precaution may be rational. If information cannot improve, preserving optionality may offer little advantage. If the evidence strongly concentrates probability within one region of the model space, then model-specific commitment may become appropriate. The principle therefore does not demand certainty. It demands proportionality. As uncertainty decreases,

$$ \text{permissible model-specific commitment} $$

can increase. As uncertainty increases,

$$ \text{justification for concentrated commitment} $$

should decrease. The relationship is gradual rather than absolute.

A provisional decision rule

The preceding considerations can be summarized in a more operational form. When confronting a deeply uncertain decision, ask:

  1. How well identified is the relevant model space?
  2. How sensitive is the recommendation to disputed probability assignments?
  3. How model-specific is the proposed commitment?
  4. What costs follow if the favored model is wrong?
  5. How reversible is the commitment?
  6. Is further information likely to become available?
  7. Does the commitment preserve or impair future inquiry?
  8. Does the proposed action remain defensible across a broad range of reasonable models?

The purpose of these questions is not to eliminate risk. No decision procedure can do that. Their purpose is to ensure that the strength of the action does not exceed the strength of the justification. This yields the deeper normative claim behind the second half of the argument:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Where epistemic confidence is structurally weak, rational commitment should remain correspondingly cautious and revisable.} } $$

The next question is whether this principle secretly privileges nonbelief. A critic may argue that “agnostic restraint” is itself a substantive choice and that treating it as the default simply builds the desired conclusion into the decision rule. That objection deserves to be taken seriously. The next section will argue that it fails because the Robust Commitment Principle does not systematically favor non-action or nonbelief. It favors whichever policy is most robust under the actual structure of uncertainty—and in different domains, that can mean acting immediately, delaying, diversifying, hedging, or gathering more information.

X. Optionality, Robustness, and the Charge of Privileging Nonbelief

A natural objection to the argument developed so far is that the Robust Commitment Principle simply privileges nonbelief. The objection might be put as follows:

If the evidence does not determine whether some religious model is true, why should withholding commitment receive any special status? Agnosticism is itself a choice. To treat it as the “default” seems to build the desired conclusion into the decision procedure.

This objection would be serious if the principle were simply:

$$ \text{when uncertain, choose nonbelief}. $$

But that is not the principle being defended. The claim is instead that, under deep uncertainty, rational agents should care about features such as robustness, reversibility, optionality, information acquisition, sensitivity to model error, and the magnitude of irreversible exposure. Depending upon the structure of a decision, these considerations can recommend immediate action, delay, diversification, hedging, experimentation, or some combination of them.

These are not concepts invented for religious epistemology. They already play important roles in economics, finance, strategic planning, engineering, public policy, and formal decision analysis. The argument for agnostic restraint is therefore better understood as an application of a general theory of rational commitment under uncertainty.

Deep uncertainty is already a recognized decision problem

Decision analysis commonly distinguishes situations of ordinary risk from situations in which the uncertainty itself is difficult to characterize. Under ordinary risk, the possible outcomes and their probability distributions may be reasonably well understood. Under deeper uncertainty, decision-makers may be unable to identify a unique probability distribution, may disagree about the relevant causal model, or may not know which model adequately represents the system at all.

Work on decision-making under deep uncertainty explicitly addresses cases in which uncertainties cannot be satisfactorily represented by agreed probability distributions or in which analysts cannot agree upon the models connecting decisions to outcomes. Rather than optimizing against a single supposedly correct forecast, such approaches often evaluate strategies across many plausible futures and look for policies that remain satisfactory despite model error. This distinction maps closely onto the ultimist problem developed in Part I. The uncertainty concerns several nested questions:

  • Which theological model is true?
  • Which theological models should be considered in the first place?
  • How should those models be weighted?
  • What consequences do they predict?
  • Which allegedly decision-relevant theological consequences should be treated as genuine possibilities rather than merely stipulated payoffs?

The situation therefore resembles the very circumstances for which robust decision methods are designed.

Optionality and the value of preserving future choice

One of the most important ideas in decision-making under uncertainty is optionality. An option is valuable because possessing it allows an agent to act later without requiring that the action be taken now. The simplest example comes from financial options. The holder of an option possesses a right without an obligation to exercise it. The value lies partly in the ability to wait while uncertainty resolves. The same logic has been extended to real-world decisions through the theory of real options.

Suppose a firm is considering a large irreversible investment. Standard reasoning might compare the expected return of investing now with the expected return of not investing. But if the investment cannot easily be reversed and additional information about future demand will become available later, investing immediately destroys something valuable:

$$ \text{the option to wait}. $$

Real-options analysis treats this lost flexibility as an opportunity cost. Research on irreversible investment under uncertainty explicitly emphasizes that undertaking an investment can extinguish the value of waiting, and that greater uncertainty can therefore raise the threshold at which irreversible investment becomes rational. The decision is consequently not merely

$$ \text{invest} \quad\text{versus}\quad \text{do not invest}. $$

It is

$$ \text{invest now} $$

versus

$$ \text{retain the option to invest later after learning more}. $$

These are importantly different. Waiting is not equivalent to rejecting the investment. Indeed, the entire value of waiting depends upon preserving the possibility of later investment. This is closely analogous to the conception of agnosticism being defended here. Agnostic inquiry is not:

$$ \text{“ultimate reality does not exist.”} $$

It is closer to:

$$ \text{“do not make an unnecessarily strong model-specific commitment while preserving the ability to do so later if the evidence changes.”} $$

The structure is therefore:

$$ \text{agnostic inquiry} \neq \text{metaphysical rejection}. $$

Instead,

$$ \text{agnostic inquiry} = \text{preserved epistemic optionality}. $$

If stronger evidence for some religious or ultimist model subsequently appears, the agnostic remains capable of updating. Thus:

$$ A_0 \rightarrow M_i $$

remains available. The option has not been destroyed. This is why describing agnosticism merely as “nonbelief” can obscure the decision-theoretic structure of the position. What matters is not simply what proposition is absent from the agent's beliefs. What matters is that the agent has avoided making a high-cost commitment to a model that remains poorly identified.

The value of information

Closely related is the formal concept of the value of information. Information has decision value when acquiring it before committing to an action allows the decision-maker to choose more effectively. Decision analysis explicitly treats the value of information in terms of how much resolving epistemic uncertainty can improve the outcome of a decision. This creates a general reason to distinguish decisions that must be made immediately from those that can rationally remain open. Suppose an agent must choose between

$$ a_1 $$

and

$$ a_2, $$

but expects evidence \(E\) to become available tomorrow. If

$$ E $$

could substantially alter which action is preferable, and waiting until tomorrow carries little cost, then immediate irreversible commitment throws away the potential value of that information. The relevant comparison is therefore not simply:

$$ U(a_1)\quad\text{versus}\quad U(a_2). $$

It also includes

$$ U(\text{wait, observe }E,\text{ then choose}). $$

That third strategy may dominate both immediate commitments. This becomes particularly relevant to metaphysical inquiry because there is no obvious deadline forcing most individuals to finalize their entire theory of ultimate reality today. Further arguments may be encountered. Additional philosophical work may be done. One's understanding of science, history, theology, and religious traditions may improve. Novel forms of evidence may arise. Our civilization itself may develop better conceptual frameworks. If the epistemic problem is deeply underdetermined and continued inquiry remains possible, then maintaining the capacity to benefit from future information has genuine rational value. The relevant policy becomes:

$$ \text{observe} \rightarrow \text{update} \rightarrow \text{commit when warranted}. $$

Rather than:

$$ \text{commit} \rightarrow \text{reinterpret future information through the commitment}. $$

This distinction will become especially important when we consider the path-dependent effects of religious adoption.

Robustness: performing acceptably across models

A second family of decision methods emphasizes robustness. Suppose a planner possesses several plausible models:

$$ M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_k. $$

Action \(a_1\) performs extremely well under \(M_1\) but badly under every other model. Action \(a_2\) is not optimal under any one model, but performs acceptably across almost all of them. If there is strong evidence that \(M_1\) is correct, choosing \(a_1\) may be rational. But if the planner cannot determine which model is reliable, \(a_2\) may be preferable because its performance is less dependent upon resolving the model uncertainty correctly. This is the central motivation behind robust decision-making under deep uncertainty: rather than optimizing policy for one forecast, analysts examine performance over many plausible futures and seek strategies that are less vulnerable to getting the model wrong. The analogy with religious commitment is straightforward. A policy such as

$$ a_C=\text{organize one's life around a highly specific theological model} $$

may perform extremely well if that theological model is correct. But its justification may deteriorate rapidly across alternative models. A policy such as

$$ a_A=\text{maintain open inquiry while avoiding unnecessary model-specific commitments} $$

may sacrifice some possible benefits under \(M_C\) while remaining defensible across a much broader class of possibilities. This does not establish that \(a_A\) is always preferable. It identifies the relevant question:

$$ \boxed{ \text{How sensitive is the rationality of the policy to our being wrong about the model?} } $$

A policy whose justification collapses whenever one disputed theological assumption changes has poor robustness.

Minimax regret and the danger of premature concentration

Another way to approach uncertain decisions is through regret. Instead of asking which action has the greatest expected payoff under a disputed probability distribution, one can ask how badly one would regret an action relative to the best action that would have been available if the true state had been known. For an action \(a\) in state \(\theta\), define regret schematically as

$$ R(a,\theta) = U(a^*_\theta,\theta)-U(a,\theta), $$

where \(a^*_\theta\) is the action that would have been optimal had \(\theta\) been known in advance. A minimax-regret strategy seeks to reduce the largest avoidable loss from having chosen badly under uncertainty. The point here is not that minimax regret should be adopted as the uniquely correct decision rule for religion. It should not. The importance of regret-based reasoning is methodological: rational decision-making need not always consist in maximizing expected utility under one precise probability model. When probabilities are poorly grounded, alternative criteria can evaluate how vulnerable a decision is to being badly wrong. This again shifts attention toward concentrated commitments. Suppose one commits strongly to

$$ M_1 $$

when

$$ M_2,M_3,\ldots,M_n $$

remain substantial possibilities. The concern is not merely that \(M_1\) might turn out false. It is that the action may create large losses that could have been avoided by a more flexible policy while the uncertainty remained unresolved. This is closely related to the idea of model-specific exposure developed earlier.

Diversification and concentration risk

Portfolio reasoning provides another useful analogy. Suppose an investor believes that one asset has the highest expected return but has substantial uncertainty about the model producing that estimate. Investing everything in that asset creates concentration risk. Diversification sacrifices the maximum possible payoff if the favored model happens to be exactly correct, but reduces dependence upon any one uncertain forecast. The relevant insight is not that diversification always maximizes returns. It is that

$$ \text{uncertainty about which model is right} $$

can itself create a reason to avoid

$$ \text{maximal exposure to one model}. $$

Decision analysis is applied explicitly to portfolio problems involving allocation across projects of uncertain value, including questions about the value of obtaining additional information before allocating resources. Again, the application to ultimism should not be pushed too literally. One cannot simply hold 30 percent Christianity, 20 percent pantheism, and 15 percent deism in the same way one owns financial assets. But the structural insight remains useful. Where model uncertainty is high, concentrated exposure demands stronger justification. A person who restructures every major dimension of life around one highly specific theology is taking something analogous to a concentrated position. That may be rational if the evidence is sufficiently strong. But the concentration itself is not justified merely because the theory offers a large upside if true.

Adaptivity

Another important concept is adaptive strategy. A robust policy does not always specify one fixed action for all future time. It may specify a sequence:

$$ a_0 \rightarrow \text{observe} \rightarrow a_1 \rightarrow \text{observe} \rightarrow a_2. $$

The strategy changes as information changes. This is particularly attractive under deep uncertainty because the agent avoids pretending to know in advance what cannot presently be known. The rational policy can therefore be conditional:

$$ \text{If evidence }E_1\text{ emerges, increase commitment to }M_1. $$
$$ \text{If evidence }E_2\text{ emerges, reduce commitment to }M_1. $$
$$ \text{If uncertainty remains high, preserve inquiry}. $$

Agnosticism, understood in this way, is not a frozen midpoint between belief and disbelief. It is an adaptive epistemic policy. Its central disposition is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{remain responsive to information rather than resolving uncertainty by fiat.} } $$

Why this does not privilege inaction

We can now see why the charge of privileging nonbelief mistakes the structure of the argument. The Robust Commitment Principle does not always recommend waiting; its recommendation depends upon the structure of the decision.

Decision structure Rational response the same principles may support
A fire alarm where evacuation is cheap and reversible but remaining inside could be catastrophic Evacuate immediately.
An irreversible billion-dollar infrastructure investment under substantial demand uncertainty, with additional information expected next year Delay.
An uncertain investment environment with avoidable concentration risk Diversify.
An unknown technological process that can initially be tested cheaply Run a pilot.
A decision in which further information is impossible and delay itself produces severe losses Commit despite uncertainty.

There is no universal preference for non-action. Instead:

$$ \boxed{ \text{the rational response varies with reversibility, information value, downside exposure, robustness, and urgency.} } $$

This is precisely what we should expect from a genuinely general decision principle.

Religious agnosticism emerges from the structure of the problem

The important question is therefore not whether the principle privileges agnosticism. It is why, when applied to the particular structure of ultimist uncertainty, it tends to recommend agnostic restraint. The answer is that the relevant decision currently appears to combine:

  • high model uncertainty;
  • poorly identified probabilities;
  • large differences among possible theological payoff structures;
  • continued opportunities for inquiry;
  • no generally established deadline for commitment; and
  • potentially substantial costs from model-specific commitments.

Under those conditions, optionality matters. Information preservation matters. Reversibility matters. Model robustness matters. The policy

$$ A_0=\text{continued agnostic inquiry} $$

therefore has properties analogous to an option-preserving strategy. It retains the ability to move toward

$$ M_1 $$

if evidence for \(M_1\) becomes stronger. It retains the ability to move toward

$$ M_2 $$

if evidence instead favors \(M_2\). It can also remain uncertain if the evidence never becomes sufficiently discriminating. In this sense,

$$ A_0 $$

occupies a structurally different position from a highly specific theological commitment. It makes fewer bets on unresolved model-specific propositions.

Relabeling the problem

A useful test is to strip away the religious vocabulary. Imagine an agent faces one hundred possible models of an unknown system. The agent does not know whether the list is exhaustive. The models make radically different predictions. The probability weights over the models are poorly justified. Some of the models contain enormous rewards for selecting them and enormous punishments for selecting their rivals, but those reward structures are themselves consequences of the disputed models. The agent can acquire additional information over time. Selecting one model now would significantly influence future decisions and may make later revision costly. Would a rational decision scientist normally say:

“Choose one of the models now because one of them might contain an enormous payoff”?

Probably not. The natural questions would instead concern robustness, sensitivity, option value, value of information, reversibility, and exposure to model error.

Changing the labels back to “Christianity,” “Islam,” “pantheism,” “naturalism,” or “ultimism” should not alter the rational structure of the problem. This is a form of relabeling invariance:

$$ \boxed{ \text{If two decision problems are structurally equivalent, culturally familiar labels should not change the decision rule.} } $$

That principle is important because the religious model most psychologically available to an agent will often be determined by social and cultural history. The model's familiarity does not by itself give it greater decision-theoretic standing.

Agnosticism as an option-preserving policy

The resulting position can therefore be expressed more precisely. Strong agnosticism does not say:

$$ \text{“Choose nothing.”} $$

It says:

$$ \boxed{ \text{“Where the ultimist model space remains deeply underdetermined, preserve epistemic optionality and avoid commitments whose strength exceeds the robustness of their justification.”} } $$

This is not an exotic rule. It belongs to the same broad family of reasoning that gives value to flexibility in irreversible investment, information in decision analysis, diversification under model uncertainty, adaptive strategies under deep uncertainty, and robustness against forecasting error. The application is unusual only because the object of uncertainty is metaphysical rather than financial, strategic, or technological. And this matters for the accusation that agnosticism is being privileged. The procedure does not begin with

$$ \text{agnosticism is preferable}. $$

It begins with general properties of rational choice:

  • preserve valuable options;
  • do not discard useful future information unnecessarily;
  • avoid excessive concentration when model identification is weak;
  • prefer policies robust to reasonable model error; and
  • increase irreversible commitment as the justification for it becomes stronger.

Only after those principles are applied to the epistemic conditions established in Part I does agnostic restraint emerge. That is the relevant asymmetry. The rule is symmetric. The decision problem is not. The next question is therefore how large the relevant exposure actually is. If adopting a theology were merely a costless intellectual preference that could be reversed instantly, the value of preserving optionality might be small. But religious commitments often influence behavior, identity, relationships, morality, authority, finances, and even the mechanisms through which future evidence is interpreted. The next section therefore examines the risk exposure and path dependence created by religious commitment itself.

XI. Religious Commitment, Risk Exposure, and Epistemic Path Dependence

The argument from optionality becomes significantly stronger once we examine what religious commitment actually involves. If adopting a theological model were merely equivalent to placing a temporary mark next to a proposition on a questionnaire, then the costs of premature commitment might be minimal. One could affirm the proposition today, retract it tomorrow, and lose almost nothing in the process. But serious religious commitment is rarely so thin. For many people, adopting a religious worldview changes behavior, identity, social relationships, moral obligations, financial choices, family structure, ritual practice, political judgments, and the authorities regarded as epistemically trustworthy. A decision to commit to some model

$$ M_R $$

may therefore generate a bundle of downstream commitments:

$$ M_R \rightarrow \{ C_1,C_2,\ldots,C_n \}. $$

These commitments can include substantial benefits as well as costs. Religious communities can provide friendship, meaning, ritual structure, charitable institutions, emotional support, intergenerational continuity, and powerful forms of communal identity. Nothing in the present argument requires denying any of those benefits. The relevant issue is different. When a model is deeply uncertain, adopting it can create exposure to the consequences of having selected the wrong model, and that exposure may increase over time. This is precisely the kind of feature that matters in serious decision-making under uncertainty.

Commitment is not merely propositional

Suppose an agent moves from

$$ A_0=\text{agnostic inquiry} $$

to

$$ A_R=\text{high commitment to religious model }M_R. $$

That transition may involve decisions across several domains:

Domain of commitment Illustrative consequences
Resources Time, money, attention, and career choices.
Relationships Community affiliation, marriage, family structure, and child-rearing.
Practice Ritual, dietary, sexual, and other behavioral obligations.
Authority and morality Which institutions, texts, or persons are treated as morally or epistemically authoritative.
Future interpretation How later experiences, doubts, testimony, and counterevidence are understood.

The magnitude of these consequences varies enormously between traditions and between individuals. Some religious commitments are comparatively light; others are highly demanding. The point is therefore not:

$$ \text{religion is costly}. $$

The more precise point is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{the practical exposure associated with religious commitment can be substantially larger than the mere act of entertaining a metaphysical proposition.} } $$

This matters because a general principle of proportional commitment should care about the magnitude of downstream consequences. A weakly supported proposition that changes almost nothing may rationally be treated differently from a weakly supported proposition that restructures one's life.

False commitment has opportunity costs

Consider a simple decision under uncertainty. Suppose an agent has limited resources:

$$ R=\{\text{time, attention, money, social capital, life opportunities}\}. $$

Committing these resources to one model necessarily prevents their allocation elsewhere. Thus, even if religious commitment produces real benefits, there remains an opportunity-cost question:

$$ C_{\text{opportunity}} = \text{value of foregone alternatives}. $$

Years spent inside one institution cannot simultaneously be spent inside another. Money donated to one project cannot be donated elsewhere. A marriage structured around one religious expectation may foreclose other forms of life. Children raised within one comprehensive worldview cannot simultaneously be raised within every alternative. Again, none of this implies that the commitment is mistaken. Every serious life decision generates opportunity costs. But under deep model uncertainty, those costs become relevant because they are incurred before the underlying model has been well identified. The stronger the commitment, the more important it becomes that the evidential basis for selecting that model be correspondingly robust.

Some costs are partially irreversible

This leads to a second feature: irreversibility. Many religious decisions can of course be reversed. One can stop attending a religious institution. One can revise beliefs. One can leave a community. One can reinterpret earlier experiences. But reversibility is often incomplete. Time cannot be recovered. Some financial transfers cannot be undone. Certain marriage or family choices may have permanent consequences. Educational paths, social networks, and identity formation can constrain later possibilities. The decision therefore has a temporal structure. Let

$$ C_t $$

represent commitment at time \(t\). A simplified trajectory might look like

$$ C_0 \rightarrow C_1 \rightarrow C_2 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow C_n, $$

where each stage creates additional dependencies on the preceding choice. The cost of revision may therefore increase with time:

$$ \text{Cost of exit at }t_n > \text{Cost of exit at }t_0. $$

This is a classic feature of path-dependent decisions. The choice made today alters the set of choices available tomorrow. That is precisely why optionality matters.

A concrete asymmetric case

Consider a hypothetical person, Alex, who is twenty years old and uncertain between continued agnostic inquiry and commitment to a highly demanding religious tradition. Suppose Alex's present evidence does not strongly discriminate the religious model from its alternatives. Alex nevertheless faces two policies. The first is

$$ A_0=\text{remain agnostic while continuing serious inquiry}. $$

Under this policy, Alex reads theology, attends religious services when useful, speaks with believers and nonbelievers, experiments with spiritual practices, and remains open to future commitment. Alex does not organize major irreversible life decisions around the assumption that any one theology is already known to be true. The second policy is

$$ A_R=\text{commit now and treat religious model }M_R\text{ as authoritative}. $$

Suppose that commitment leads Alex over the next ten years to:

  • choose a spouse partly according to the requirements of the tradition;
  • abandon a desired career opportunity because it conflicts with religious obligations;
  • donate a significant fraction of income;
  • raise children within the religious framework;
  • restrict certain relationships and behaviors;
  • build nearly the entire social network around the religious community;
  • and interpret important personal experiences through the theology's conceptual framework.

Now suppose that at age thirty Alex encounters evidence that substantially weakens confidence in \(M_R\). The two original policies were not symmetric. Had Alex remained agnostic at twenty and become convinced of \(M_R\) at thirty, the transition

$$ A_0\rightarrow A_R $$

would still have been available. Alex could begin attending, practicing, donating, identifying with the tradition, and restructuring life around it. There would certainly have been opportunity costs from delaying. Perhaps Alex would regret not having participated earlier. Perhaps some religious experiences or community benefits would have been missed. But compare the reverse transition. If Alex committed at twenty and ceased believing at thirty, then

$$ A_R\rightarrow A_0 $$

does not restore the original decision state. The ten years cannot be replayed. The abandoned career opportunity may no longer exist. The marriage and family structure cannot simply be reset. The children's upbringing has already occurred. The social network may make departure costly. Past donations cannot be recovered. And the psychological process through which Alex evaluated evidence over the decade may itself have been shaped by the original commitment. Thus:

$$ \text{Cost}(A_R\rightarrow A_0) > \text{Cost}(A_0\rightarrow A_R) $$

under the stipulated conditions. That is the relevant asymmetry. It does not arise because agnosticism is assumed to be true. It arises because one policy preserves a larger future choice set while the other creates commitments that are costly or impossible to reverse. The same structure appears in real-options problems. If two strategies remain available tomorrow but choosing one today permanently eliminates the other, then the ability to delay has positive value when additional information is expected. The relevant comparison is therefore not:

$$ \text{religion} \quad\text{versus}\quad \text{nothing}. $$

It is:

$$ \text{commit now under deep uncertainty} $$

versus

$$ \text{retain the option to commit later after additional information}. $$

Of course, the asymmetry could reverse under different assumptions. Suppose a particular religious model were already extremely well supported, or suppose commitment were genuinely urgent and delay imposed large irreversible losses. In that case, waiting might destroy valuable options instead. This concession is important because it shows that the argument is not structured to produce agnosticism regardless of the facts. The principle is conditional:

$$ \boxed{ \text{when commitment is more irreversible than delay, uncertainty increases the value of preserving the option to delay.} } $$

If the structure of the problem changed, the recommendation could change with it.

The decision can change the future decision-maker

Religious commitment introduces an even deeper form of path dependence. It does not merely change the external opportunities available to the agent. It may change the agent's future epistemic environment. Suppose an individual commits to model \(M_R\) and then:

$$ M_R \rightarrow \text{membership in community }C, $$
$$ C \rightarrow \text{greater exposure to testimony supporting }M_R, $$
$$ C \rightarrow \text{greater emotional investment in }M_R, $$
$$ M_R \rightarrow \text{interpretation of ambiguous experiences through }M_R, $$

and

$$ M_R \rightarrow \text{selection of authorities who themselves affirm }M_R. $$

The future evidence encountered by the agent is no longer independent of the earlier decision. We can represent this as

$$ A_t \rightarrow I_{t+1}, $$

where \(A_t\) is today's commitment and \(I_{t+1}\) is tomorrow's informational environment. That informational environment then affects future belief:

$$ I_{t+1} \rightarrow B_{t+1}. $$

The process may therefore become recursive:

$$ B_t \rightarrow A_t \rightarrow I_{t+1} \rightarrow B_{t+1} \rightarrow A_{t+1}. $$

This is epistemically important. The agent is not merely making a bet on a fixed external state of the world. The bet partially changes the mechanism through which the agent will later evaluate whether the bet was correct.

Belief perseverance

There are well-established reasons to take this possibility seriously in human decision-making generally. Psychological research on belief perseverance concerns the tendency for beliefs to persist even after the evidential basis originally supporting them has been undermined. It is not unique to religion and has been studied across many domains of judgment and decision-making. This matters because early commitment can generate interpretations and auxiliary beliefs that survive later challenges. Suppose an agent initially accepts

$$ M_R $$

because of evidence

$$ E_1. $$

Later, \(E_1\) is weakened. It does not necessarily follow psychologically that

$$ P(M_R) $$

returns to its previous level. By then the agent may have accumulated

$$ E_2,E_3,\ldots,E_n, $$

some of which were interpreted within the conceptual framework supplied by \(M_R\) itself. Thus the process can become path-dependent even when the original reason for entering the path disappears. This is not evidence that religious believers are uniquely irrational. Atheists, political partisans, investors, scientists, professionals, and virtually every other class of human beings are vulnerable to similar effects. That universality is precisely why the phenomenon is decision-relevant.

Escalation of commitment

A related concept from decision science is escalation of commitment. Decision-makers sometimes continue investing resources in a chosen course of action despite negative feedback, in part because prior investment, personal responsibility, self-justification, and cognitive dissonance make reversal psychologically costly. The basic structure is:

$$ \text{initial commitment} \rightarrow \text{sunk investment} \rightarrow \text{greater psychological cost of reversal} \rightarrow \text{additional commitment}. $$

The rational rule should instead be:

$$ \text{future action depends upon future evidence}, $$

not

$$ \text{future action depends upon defending past investment}. $$

Yet human beings do not always behave this way. The possibility of escalation therefore creates another reason to value early-stage flexibility when the underlying model is deeply uncertain. A commitment that begins small may become increasingly difficult to evaluate independently because later decisions are partly motivated by preserving coherence with earlier ones.

Identity makes updating more costly

The problem becomes more severe when a proposition ceases to be merely something one believes and becomes part of one's identity. Suppose

$$ M_R $$

becomes associated with

$$ \text{“who I am.”} $$

Then evidence against \(M_R\) may threaten not merely one proposition but an interconnected network:

$$ \{ \text{family}, \text{community}, \text{morality}, \text{meaning}, \text{status}, \text{relationships}, \text{self-conception} \}. $$

The effective cost of updating therefore increases. This possibility is familiar from research on motivated reasoning and identity-protective cognition more broadly. People may evaluate information partly through social identities, prior commitments, and judgments concerning which sources are trustworthy. Again, this is not uniquely religious. The same mechanism can operate in politics, nationalism, professional identity, ideological movements, and anti-religious communities. The general lesson is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{when a belief becomes identity-bearing, revision can acquire costs unrelated to the truth of the belief itself.} } $$

That fact is directly relevant to decision-making under deep uncertainty.

Endogenous evidence

This creates a particularly important distinction between exogenous and endogenous evidence. Exogenous evidence is generated relatively independently of one's commitment. For example, an astronomical observation does not ordinarily change because an astronomer prefers one cosmological theory. But some evidence relevant to religious belief is partly endogenous to participation. A person joins a tradition. The tradition teaches the person how to pray. Prayer produces powerful experiences. Those experiences are interpreted using the concepts supplied by the tradition. They then become evidence for the tradition. Schematically:

$$ M_R \rightarrow P_R \rightarrow E_R \rightarrow M_R, $$

where \(P_R\) is a religious practice and \(E_R\) is an experience produced or interpreted through that practice. This does not make the evidence false. A practice can generate genuine information. Scientific experiments are deliberately constructed to generate evidence as well. The relevant question is whether the process is independently calibrated. If incompatible religious systems can each produce experiences that strongly reinforce their own metaphysical interpretations, then the evidential feedback loop requires additional scrutiny. The decision to enter the system may partly determine which future evidence becomes psychologically salient.

Commitment therefore changes the value of waiting

This brings us back to real-options reasoning. The value of preserving an option is especially high when three conditions hold:

$$ \text{uncertainty is substantial}, $$
$$ \text{future information may arrive}, $$

and

$$ \text{commitment is costly to reverse}. $$

The ultimist problem plausibly contains all three. First:

$$ \text{model uncertainty is substantial}. $$

Second:

$$ \text{continued philosophical, scientific, historical, and personal inquiry remains possible}. $$

Third:

$$ \text{some forms of religious commitment generate partially irreversible social and epistemic consequences}. $$

Under such conditions, the option to remain less committed has positive decision value. The policy

$$ A_0=\text{continued agnostic inquiry} $$

preserves the option

$$ A_0\rightarrow A_R $$

if evidence later favors \(M_R\). But strong commitment may reduce the practical ease of returning:

$$ A_R\rightarrow A_0. $$

The asymmetry need not be absolute for it to matter. If

$$ \text{Cost}(A_R\rightarrow A_0) > \text{Cost}(A_0\rightarrow A_R), $$

then agnostic inquiry possesses additional option value.

Religious commitment can also produce information

This point is stronger than the observation that participation supplies additional data. Some choices are transformative: entering the experience may reveal values or forms of understanding that were difficult to access beforehand, while also changing the preferences, identity, or interpretive standpoint of the person who must later evaluate the choice. Religious commitment can plausibly have this dual character. That gives participation genuine epistemic value, but it also means that the experiment can modify the investigator. The decision-theoretic problem therefore concerns both information gained and the extent to which the process changes the conditions of later updating. A fair analysis must acknowledge an important response. Some kinds of information may be available only through participation. One cannot know what meditation is like without meditating. One cannot fully evaluate a religious community without entering it. One cannot know whether certain spiritual practices produce transformative experiences merely by reading descriptions of them.

This means that the value-of-information argument does not imply total disengagement. In fact, it may support exploratory participation. The relevant decision may include policies such as

$$ A_E=\text{low-cost exploratory engagement}. $$

For example:

$$ \text{attend}, $$
$$ \text{read}, $$
$$ \text{practice experimentally}, $$
$$ \text{compare traditions}, $$
$$ \text{observe results}, $$

while withholding the stronger proposition

$$ \text{“this metaphysical interpretation is therefore true.”} $$

This distinction is important. A robust agnostic policy need not avoid religious life. It can actively investigate it. Indeed, if participation generates useful information at low cost, then the Robust Commitment Principle may positively recommend such exploration. What it resists is the transition from

$$ \text{exploration} $$

to

$$ \text{high-confidence metaphysical commitment} $$

before the resulting evidence has been adequately discriminated from alternative explanations.

The asymmetry is about exposure, not religion

The argument can now be stated more generally. Suppose action \(a\) has the following properties:

  1. it depends strongly upon model \(M\) being approximately correct;
  2. \(M\) is poorly distinguished from alternatives;
  3. adopting \(a\) creates substantial downstream costs;
  4. some of those costs are difficult to reverse;
  5. adoption changes the future informational environment;
  6. further evidence could become available later.

Under those conditions, rational decision-making has reason to demand stronger justification before adopting \(a\). Nothing about this structure is specifically religious. If the same conditions applied to an investment, political ideology, medical intervention, business strategy, or technological commitment, similar caution would be appropriate. The relevant principle is therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Greater path dependence and irreversibility increase the evidential threshold for commitment under model uncertainty.} } $$

This is why practical consequences matter

It is sometimes suggested that if a person finds religious belief meaningful or comforting, then the epistemic uncertainty need not matter very much. But comfort is only one component of the utility function. A full decision analysis must include both benefits and exposures. Schematically:

$$ U(A_R) = B_{\text{community}} + B_{\text{meaning}} + B_{\text{ritual}} + B_{\text{support}} - C_{\text{time}} - C_{\text{financial}} - C_{\text{opportunity}} - C_{\text{behavioral}} - C_{\text{exit}} - C_{\text{epistemic}}. $$

No universal numerical values can reasonably be assigned to these quantities. They vary dramatically across people and religious traditions. The point is not to calculate them precisely. The point is that they exist. Religious adoption is therefore not a costless response to metaphysical uncertainty. It is a policy with real consequences. And the more comprehensive the religious system, the larger the portion of one's life that may become conditional upon the truth or practical wisdom of that model.

The epistemic risk may be the most important

Among these forms of exposure, epistemic exposure deserves particular emphasis. Material costs can often be recognized explicitly. Epistemic costs are harder to see because the process generating them changes the perspective from which they are evaluated. If commitment to \(M\) causes future evidence to be interpreted increasingly through \(M\), then the agent may become progressively more confident precisely because of having committed. Thus:

$$ \text{commitment} \rightarrow \text{model-shaped evidence environment} \rightarrow \text{greater confidence} \rightarrow \text{greater commitment}. $$

This feedback loop can occur whether \(M\) is true or false. That is the problem. A mechanism that produces greater confidence under both truth and error is not, by itself, a reliable discriminator. This links the decision-theoretic half of the argument back to the epistemic undercutters of Part I. If religious belief is already substantially mediated by culture, community, personal experience, and interpretation, then entering a highly model-specific religious environment may amplify precisely those mechanisms whose independent reliability was previously placed in question.

The rational response is proportional engagement

None of this supports avoiding religion altogether. That would overstate the argument. The more defensible conclusion is a principle of proportional engagement:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Engage in proportion to the informativeness, reversibility, and evidential robustness of the commitment.} } $$

Low-cost exploration may be reasonable. Comparative study may be reasonable. Participation without dogmatic commitment may be reasonable. Practices that produce independent benefits may be reasonable even when their metaphysical interpretation remains uncertain. What requires increasingly strong justification is the transition toward commitments that are:

$$ \text{more exclusive}, $$
$$ \text{more costly}, $$
$$ \text{more identity-forming}, $$
$$ \text{less reversible}, $$

and

$$ \text{more dependent upon one disputed theological model}. $$

The decision-theoretic conclusion is therefore not hostility toward religion. It is that the magnitude of commitment should track the robustness of the case supporting the model on which that commitment depends. Once this is recognized, agnostic inquiry acquires a further structural advantage. It is not riskless, and it does not maximize every possible good. But it generally preserves greater optionality, imposes fewer model-specific commitments, and leaves the agent comparatively free to update as the evidential environment changes.

The next section will make that positive case explicit: why agnostic inquiry, understood not as metaphysical denial but as a low-model-specific and adaptive policy, occupies a distinctive position under deep epistemic uncertainty.

XII. Agnostic Inquiry as a Low-Model-Specific Policy

The previous sections have mostly been negative. They have argued that the ultimist model space is deeply underdetermined, that ordinary Bayesian comparison depends upon poorly specified theological likelihoods, that Pascal-style reasoning imports disputed theology into the decision matrix, and that strong religious commitment can create substantial practical and epistemic exposure. What remains is the positive question:

$$ \text{Why should agnostic inquiry be preferred?} $$

The answer is not that agnosticism is costless, metaphysically neutral in every sense, or guaranteed to maximize utility under every possible theology. It is that agnostic inquiry has a distinctive structural advantage under the particular form of uncertainty established in Part I. It is comparatively low in model-specific commitments. It preserves optionality. It remains responsive to future information. It avoids treating poorly identified theological consequences as though they were established features of the decision problem. And it allows degrees of practical engagement without requiring premature metaphysical closure. For these reasons, agnostic inquiry functions not merely as an absence of belief, but as a robust adaptive policy.

Agnosticism is not atheism with weaker confidence

The first point must be made clearly. The agnosticism defended here is not:

$$ A=\text{“God probably does not exist.”} $$

Nor is it:

$$ A=\text{“naturalism is the default metaphysics.”} $$

Nor does it require:

$$ P(U)<0.5. $$

The argument has deliberately resisted such precise assignments. Instead, the relevant policy is closer to

$$ A_0= \text{withhold high-confidence model-specific commitment while continuing inquiry}. $$

This is an epistemic and practical stance toward uncertainty. It is therefore compatible with substantial openness to ultimism. An agnostic following this policy might believe that some form of ultimate reality is entirely possible. They might regard classical theism as worth serious investigation. They might participate in religious practices, read theology, study mystical traditions, compare metaphysical systems, and take religious experience seriously. What they refrain from doing is treating one particular model as sufficiently established to justify a level of commitment that outruns the robustness of the evidence. That difference is crucial.

Model specificity

Consider again the ultimist model space

$$ U=\{M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_n,\ldots\}. $$

Suppose action

$$ A_1 $$

requires the truth of a highly specific conjunction:

$$ M_1 = G + R + S + J + P, $$

where

$$ G=\text{a particular God exists}, $$
$$ R=\text{a particular revelation is authentic}, $$
$$ S=\text{a particular soteriology is correct}, $$
$$ J=\text{a particular theory of divine judgment is correct}, $$

and

$$ P=\text{a particular religious practice is required}. $$

The action's justification is therefore conditional upon a relatively narrow region of model space. By contrast, suppose action

$$ A_0 $$

requires only assumptions such as:

$$ \text{our present information is incomplete}, $$
$$ \text{further inquiry is possible}, $$

and

$$ \text{strong commitment can be delayed without prohibitive known cost}. $$

The second policy is less model-specific. This matters because uncertainty accumulates through conjunction. If a decision requires several disputed propositions to be correct simultaneously, then its justification depends upon the entire conjunction. The relevant comparison is not:

$$ \text{Does God exist?} $$

but something closer to:

$$ P(G\land R\land S\land J\land P\mid E). $$

Even if an agent assigns substantial credence to \(G\), the more specific religious commitment may require confidence in much more than \(G\) alone. This is another reason why generic arguments for theism cannot automatically justify specific religious adoption. The distance between

$$ \text{“some divine reality probably exists”} $$

and

$$ \text{“therefore I should organize my life around this particular tradition”} $$

may contain numerous additional uncertain steps. Agnostic inquiry avoids pretending those steps have already been resolved.

Robustness across ultimist models

A policy is robust when its justification survives across a reasonably broad range of models. Consider several possibilities. Under a deistic model, honest agnostic inquiry may be irrelevant to ultimate reward or punishment. Under a universalist God-model, it may likewise carry little transcendent downside. Under a model in which God values sincerity and responsible inquiry, agnostic restraint may perform well. Under an impersonal ultimist model, propositional religious commitment may be largely beside the point. Under naturalism, agnostic inquiry avoids unnecessary metaphysical commitments while preserving whatever naturalistic benefits religious practices may independently produce. Under an exclusivist model, however, delayed religious commitment may carry substantial cost. The point is not that agnosticism wins in every row of the matrix. It does not. The point is that agnosticism does not depend upon one narrow theology being correct in order to remain intelligible as a policy. Compare this with a specific exclusivist commitment.

Its strongest decision-theoretic justification may depend heavily upon the truth of exactly the theology being selected. Thus:

$$ \text{religious commitment} \rightarrow \text{potentially high payoff under a narrow model region}, $$

while

$$ \text{agnostic inquiry} \rightarrow \text{moderate defensibility across a broader region}. $$

Under deep model uncertainty, that difference has rational significance.

Agnosticism preserves the capacity to update

The strongest structural feature of agnostic inquiry may be its relationship to future evidence. Suppose at time \(t_0\) the agent remains uncertain:

$$ A(t_0)=A_0. $$

At time \(t_1\) compelling new evidence appears for model \(M_1\). The agnostic policy contains no prohibition against updating:

$$ A_0 \xrightarrow{E_1} A_{M_1}. $$

Suppose instead evidence favors \(M_2\):

$$ A_0 \xrightarrow{E_2} A_{M_2}. $$

And if the evidence remains underdetermined:

$$ A_0 \xrightarrow{E_{\text{ambiguous}}} A_0. $$

This makes agnostic inquiry naturally compatible with an adaptive policy. Its defining commitment is not to remain agnostic forever. Its defining commitment is to let the degree of commitment remain conditional upon the evidence. This can be expressed as

$$ C_{t+1} = f(E_{t+1},M_{t+1},C_t), $$

where future commitment changes as the evidential situation changes. The ideal is not indecision. It is responsiveness.

The distinction between suspension and refusal

This also clarifies an ambiguity in ordinary talk of “nonbelief.” There is an important difference between

$$ \text{suspending judgment} $$

and

$$ \text{refusing to consider}. $$

A person who dogmatically refuses all theological possibilities is not practicing the form of agnosticism defended here. That position would itself exhibit model rigidity. Strong agnosticism requires openness in both directions. It should be possible for new evidence to increase confidence in ultimism. It should also be possible for evidence to strengthen naturalism or another non-ultimist view. The stance can therefore be represented as:

$$ \boxed{ \text{high openness} + \text{low premature commitment}. } $$

This combination is what distinguishes agnostic inquiry from either dogmatic theism or dogmatic atheism.

Agnosticism can include action

Another important misconception is that agnosticism must be behaviorally empty. It need not be. An agent may remain metaphysically agnostic while adopting practices that have independent justification. Suppose meditation produces psychological benefits. An agnostic may meditate. Suppose participation in a religious community provides friendship and meaning. An agnostic may participate. Suppose a moral tradition contains valuable ethical insights. An agnostic may adopt them. Suppose religious ritual creates a useful structure for reflection. An agnostic may engage in ritual. The distinction is between

$$ \text{using a practice} $$

and

$$ \text{endorsing the full metaphysical model associated with the practice}. $$

This is decision-theoretically important because many benefits normally treated as advantages of religious commitment may be partially separable from the truth of the underlying theology. Let

$$ B_P $$

represent the practical benefit of some practice \(P\). If

$$ B_P>0 $$

under several competing models, then the practice may have a robust justification independently of whether the theology is true. This allows an agent to capture some of the upside of religious traditions without necessarily accepting their full model-specific exposure. In decision-science terms, this resembles modular commitment. The agent need not purchase the entire bundle when some components have independent value.

Decomposing the religious bundle

A religious worldview often arrives as a package:

$$ R= \{ M, P, C, E, A, I \}, $$

where

$$ M=\text{metaphysical claims}, $$
$$ P=\text{practices}, $$
$$ C=\text{community}, $$
$$ E=\text{ethical commitments}, $$
$$ A=\text{authority structure}, $$

and

$$ I=\text{identity}. $$

But rational evaluation need not treat the package as indivisible. An agent may judge:

$$ P\text{ is valuable}, $$
$$ C\text{ is valuable}, $$
$$ E\text{ contains useful insights}, $$

while remaining uncertain about

$$ M $$

and cautious about

$$ A. $$

This decomposition is important because otherwise the debate can become artificially binary:

$$ \text{accept religion} \quad\text{or}\quad \text{reject religion}. $$

The actual decision space is much richer. A robust agnostic policy can selectively adopt components whose value survives metaphysical uncertainty while withholding commitment to components whose justification depends heavily upon one disputed model. This is another sense in which agnosticism preserves optionality.

What about the cost of waiting?

A critic may object that delay is not free. This is correct. If some true religious model rewards early commitment, then waiting may have costs. An agent who remains agnostic may lose years of community participation, spiritual development, ritual practice, or other benefits. If an exclusivist theology is true, the stakes could be much larger. A serious decision analysis must therefore include

$$ C_{\text{delay}}. $$

The argument for strong agnosticism does not require

$$ C_{\text{delay}}=0. $$

It requires something more modest. Under the conditions of deep model uncertainty established previously, we lack adequate grounds for treating the highly model-specific transcendent components of \(C_{\text{delay}}\) as established. For example,

$$ C_{\text{delay}} = \text{eternal punishment} $$

is not a generic cost of agnosticism. It is a consequence of particular theological models. The known costs of delay should certainly be counted. But the unknown model-specific costs cannot simply be inserted into the decision matrix at full strength. This is symmetrical with the treatment of religious benefits. A religion cannot receive infinite decision-theoretic credit for a promised heaven unless the model generating that promise is independently justified. And agnosticism cannot be assigned infinite decision-theoretic liability for a threatened hell on the same basis.

Robustness does not mean zero risk

This distinction is worth emphasizing. The claim is not:

$$ \text{agnosticism is safe}. $$

No policy is safe across an unconstrained model space. For any action, one can imagine a possible reality in which that action performs terribly. If the criterion were

$$ \text{avoid every conceivable downside}, $$

no decision would be possible. The relevant criterion is instead robustness across admissible and epistemically live models without pretending that we possess a uniquely justified precise distribution over them. The decision rule therefore operates between two extremes:

$$ \text{precise optimization under unjustified probabilities} $$

and

$$ \text{paralysis before every conceivable possibility}. $$

Strong agnosticism occupies that middle ground. It recognizes uncertainty without turning mere imagination into equal evidential weight.

The burden of specificity

This produces an important burden-shifting result. Suppose someone recommends

$$ A_R=\text{commit to }M_R. $$

The recommendation contains more information than

$$ A_0=\text{continue inquiry}. $$

It selects a particular region of model space and treats that region as sufficiently reliable to guide action. The appropriate question is therefore:

$$ \text{What justifies the additional specificity?} $$

This is not an illicit presumption against religion. It is a general feature of reasoning. A more specific claim excludes more possibilities. A more specific policy depends upon more conditions being correct. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{greater specificity creates a greater justificatory burden.} } $$

If the burden is met, commitment becomes rational. If it is not, remaining less specific is not arbitrary favoritism. It is simply declining to claim more than the evidence presently supports.

Strong agnosticism as a default, not a dogma

The word default should therefore be interpreted carefully. A default is not a permanently privileged truth. It is the policy followed in the absence of sufficient reason to depart from it. For example, one might represent the structure as:

$$ A_0 \xrightarrow{\text{sufficient discriminating evidence}} A_i. $$

The default remains in place until the informational threshold for a more model-specific action is crossed. This makes strong agnosticism inherently defeasible. If tomorrow we acquired extraordinary and independently verifiable evidence for one particular ultimist model, then the same decision procedure defended in this essay could recommend abandoning agnosticism. Indeed, if it could not do so, the procedure would be suspect. Its willingness to recommend commitment under different evidential conditions is precisely what distinguishes it from a disguised commitment to atheism.

The positive case

We can now summarize the structural advantages of agnostic inquiry. Under present conditions, it tends to:

$$ \text{require fewer disputed metaphysical assumptions}, $$
$$ \text{preserve a larger future choice set}, $$
$$ \text{retain the value of future information}, $$
$$ \text{reduce exposure to model-specific error}, $$
$$ \text{remain compatible with exploratory engagement}, $$
$$ \text{permit selective adoption of independently valuable practices}, $$

and

$$ \text{remain defeasible in response to stronger evidence}. $$

None of these establishes that agnosticism is metaphysically true. That would misunderstand the argument. They establish that, given deep epistemic underdetermination, agnostic inquiry possesses important decision-theoretic virtues. This gives us the central transition from Part I to Part II:

$$ \text{deep epistemic underdetermination} $$

combined with

$$ \text{robust commitment principles} $$

supports

$$ \boxed{ \text{agnostic inquiry as the rational default policy}. } $$

The conclusion is still defeasible. A sufficiently strong argument, revelation, experience, or other form of evidence could alter the decision. But until the relevant model is identified with substantially greater robustness, the burden lies with the more model-specific commitment.

The remaining task is to consider the strongest objections to this conclusion. In particular: Is this standard so demanding that it would undermine science as well as religion? Can religious commitment itself generate evidence that agnosticism cannot access? Does agnosticism carry its own serious risks? And does robust decision-making become excessively conservative when applied to questions that may require existential commitment? Those objections determine whether strong agnosticism is genuinely a general principle of rational restraint or merely an attractive description of intellectual caution.

The final two sections test the argument against its strongest objections and then state the complete two-part case for strong agnosticism.

XIII. Major Objections to Strong Agnosticism

The argument for strong agnosticism now has two components. Part I argued that our present epistemic situation regarding ultimism is characterized by deep underdetermination. The relevant model space is large and potentially open-ended, model weights are poorly justified, many likelihoods depend upon uncertain accounts of divine psychology, and the principal mechanisms proposed for identifying the correct model are themselves subject to substantial undercutting evidence.

Part II then argued that, under these conditions, rational commitment should be evaluated using principles of robustness, optionality, reversibility, value of information, and proportional exposure. Agnostic inquiry emerged as the default not because disbelief was privileged, but because it currently appears to be comparatively low in model-specific commitment while preserving the ability to update. Several substantial objections remain. The most important are not that the argument is obviously hostile to religion, but that its standards may be too demanding, too conservative, or insufficiently attentive to the possibility that commitment itself can be epistemically valuable.

Objection 1: Science is underdetermined too

Perhaps the most important objection is that nothing about underdetermination is unique to religion. Scientific theories are also evaluated against finite evidence. Observations do not normally entail theories. Auxiliary assumptions intervene between theory and prediction. Multiple models can fit the same data. Historically, scientists have repeatedly failed to conceive of alternatives that later became serious competitors.

The philosophy of science contains an extensive literature on precisely these problems. Duhem-Quine forms of underdetermination emphasize that hypotheses are rarely tested in isolation, while contrastive underdetermination concerns the possibility that multiple theories can receive support from the same evidence. The problem of unconceived alternatives goes further: the history of science gives us reason to suspect that presently accepted theories may have serious competitors that have not yet occurred to us. If underdetermination warrants strong agnosticism about religion, why does it not warrant equivalent agnosticism about physics, biology, or cosmology? This objection succeeds against an overly strong version of the argument. If the principle were

$$ \text{underdetermination} \rightarrow \text{withhold commitment}, $$

then it would be far too skeptical. Virtually every nontrivial empirical theory is underdetermined to some degree. The argument therefore cannot depend upon the mere existence of underdetermination. What matters is its structure and severity. Consider the difference between two cases. In the first, we possess several models

$$ M_1,M_2,M_3, $$

each of which specifies measurable quantities, generates relatively precise expectations, interacts with other independently successful theories, and can be tested against future observations. The models may still be underdetermined by current evidence. But the inquiry possesses substantial structure. In the second case, we do not know whether our model space is remotely exhaustive, do not possess an agreed measure over it, do not know how to derive many of its likelihoods, and can often modify the models' latent motivational parameters after observing the evidence. These are not equivalent epistemic situations. The distinction is therefore one of degree of constraint, not a sharp boundary between science and metaphysics. Scientific theories can suffer from underdetermination while still possessing properties such as

$$ \text{independent measurement}, $$
$$ \text{parameter estimation}, $$
$$ \text{novel prediction}, $$
$$ \text{cross-domain constraint}, $$
$$ \text{replication}, $$

and

$$ \text{systematic predictive failure}. $$

Those mechanisms can progressively reduce the admissible model space. A scientific model that repeatedly predicts the wrong observations cannot ordinarily reply merely by introducing an unconstrained new preference into the laws of nature. Theological models involving purposive agents have greater flexibility precisely because intentions can often be retrospectively varied. Thus the strong agnostic argument does not rely on:

$$ \text{science is certain} $$

while

$$ \text{religion is uncertain}. $$

That contrast would be indefensible. It relies upon the more modest claim that different domains can exhibit different levels and kinds of underdetermination, and rational commitment should be sensitive to those differences. Indeed, the existence of scientific underdetermination actually supports the general methodology developed here. Scientists frequently respond to model uncertainty by preserving competing hypotheses, designing experiments with high discriminatory value, reporting uncertainty ranges, performing sensitivity analyses, and resisting stronger conclusions than the evidence supports. Strong agnosticism asks that metaphysical inquiry adopt the same intellectual discipline. The principle is not:

$$ \text{“Religion must satisfy an impossible scientific standard.”} $$

It is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{“The confidence of the conclusion should reflect the actual discriminatory power of the evidence.”} } $$

Objection 2: Some evidence is available only through commitment

A more specifically religious objection is that agnostic distance may itself prevent access to relevant evidence. William James famously argued that there are cases in which a choice is live, forced, and momentous, yet cannot be settled on intellectual grounds alone. In such circumstances, waiting for conclusive evidence can itself amount to losing an opportunity. Religious commitment may be such a case. Perhaps one cannot know whether prayer produces an encounter with God without praying. Perhaps one cannot understand a religious form of life without inhabiting it. Perhaps trust, love, spiritual transformation, or divine disclosure are relational goods that become available only after some degree of commitment. The critic can therefore argue:

Your demand for evidence before commitment creates a circular barrier. Some of the evidence capable of resolving the uncertainty becomes available only after commitment.

This objection should be granted considerable force. There are many domains in which participation generates information unavailable from detached observation. One cannot determine whether one enjoys playing the piano merely by studying acoustics. One cannot fully evaluate a career without experiencing aspects of the work. One cannot know whether a relationship can develop without accepting some degree of vulnerability. Religious inquiry may have similar features. But this does not establish the rationality of high-confidence metaphysical commitment. It establishes the possible rationality of exploratory engagement. The distinction introduced earlier becomes critical:

$$ A_E=\text{exploratory participation} $$

need not equal

$$ A_C=\text{full metaphysical commitment}. $$

An agent can pray experimentally. Attend services. Meditate. Read scripture devotionally as well as critically. Spend time within religious communities. Attempt spiritual disciplines. Take religious testimony seriously. The agent can therefore acquire participation-dependent evidence without first deciding that the tradition's metaphysical interpretation of that evidence is correct. Indeed, this resembles an experimental strategy:

$$ \text{low-cost intervention} \rightarrow \text{observation} \rightarrow \text{updating}. $$

The key question becomes whether the resulting evidence discriminates among models. Suppose Christian prayer produces a profound experience. That may increase the agent's confidence that something significant has occurred. But the interpretive question remains:

$$ \text{Christian revelation?} $$
$$ \text{generic theistic experience?} $$
$$ \text{culturally mediated ultimist experience?} $$
$$ \text{psychological state generated by contemplative practice?} $$

The experience does not necessarily adjudicate among these possibilities by itself. Moreover, if participation in incompatible traditions can generate similarly powerful internal confirmation, then commitment-dependent evidence faces the calibration problem discussed earlier. So the appropriate answer to the Jamesian objection is not:

$$ \text{never commit enough to experience anything}. $$

It is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{distinguish exploratory commitment needed to generate information from stronger metaphysical commitment that interprets the information.} } $$

Strong agnosticism can permit—and sometimes positively recommend—the former.

Objection 3: Agnosticism also has costs

A third objection attacks the apparent asymmetry in favor of waiting. Agnostic inquiry is not costless. A person who remains uncommitted for twenty years may lose twenty years of religious community, ritual, spiritual development, meaning, or relationships. If some religion is true, the person may lose opportunities that cannot later be recovered. Perhaps certain forms of character are cultivated only through long practice. Perhaps the religious life itself has cumulative value. And if some exclusivist theology is true, delay could have consequences vastly greater than any ordinary opportunity cost. This objection is correct. The strong agnostic argument should not claim

$$ C_{\text{agnosticism}}=0. $$

Nor should it claim that the transition

$$ A_0\rightarrow A_R $$

is always costless. The real-options analogy can be misleading if it is interpreted as though waiting were free. In investment theory, an option to wait has value only after accounting for the benefits that may be lost by delaying investment. The same is true here. The relevant decision compares

$$ \text{value of preserved optionality} $$

against

$$ \text{cost of delayed commitment}. $$

This means that the strong agnostic conclusion is conditional. If there were robust evidence that delay imposed large irreversible costs, the recommendation could change. But notice the difficulty for the religious critic. The strongest alleged costs of agnosticism are often themselves highly model-specific. For example:

$$ \text{agnosticism} \rightarrow \text{eternal punishment} $$

requires a very particular theology. Likewise:

$$ \text{failure to accept religion }R\text{ before time }t \rightarrow \text{irreversible supernatural loss} $$

is not an independently established feature of the decision environment. It belongs to the model being evaluated. The known secular costs of agnosticism should certainly be counted. The possible loss of community, ritual, psychological support, or years of practice may be genuine. But many of these goods can also be pursued through partial participation or alternative institutions. More importantly, they must be weighed against the opportunity costs and path dependence of premature commitment. So the objection corrects the argument without defeating it:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Agnosticism possesses option value, but option value must be weighed against the real cost of waiting.} } $$

The claim is comparative, not absolute.

Objection 4: Robust reasoning may be excessively conservative

Another challenge concerns the Robust Commitment Principle itself. A strategy that performs reasonably across many models may be attractive precisely because it avoids catastrophic failure. But excessive concern with robustness can also produce timid decisions.

Suppose one strategy has enormous upside under the best-supported model and only moderate downside under less plausible alternatives. A robustness criterion might nevertheless favor a mediocre strategy that performs acceptably everywhere. Applied generally, this could discourage entrepreneurship, experimentation, scientific boldness, personal relationships, or any action requiring uncertainty. The problem becomes especially obvious with very conservative rules such as maximin. If an agent evaluates each action only by its worst conceivable outcome,

$$ V(a) = \min_{\theta}U(a,\theta), $$

then sufficiently extreme possibilities can paralyze decision-making. In the ultimist case, the problem is even worse. For virtually any action \(a\), we can imagine some bizarre theological model under which

$$ U(a,\theta)=-\infty. $$

Then maximin gives us

$$ V(a)=-\infty $$

for almost every action. The rule becomes useless. This is why the Robust Commitment Principle should not be identified with pure maximin. Nor should it require optimal performance under every conceivable model. The relevant standard is robustness across epistemically admissible models and reasonable variations in uncertain assumptions. This matters enormously. Mere logical possibility does not equal decision relevance. We do not ordinarily alter our lives because we can imagine a deity who punishes people for wearing blue shirts. A possibility must possess some epistemic standing before it deserves significant influence over rational policy. Thus the argument operates between two errors:

$$ \text{arbitrary precision} $$

and

$$ \text{possibilistic paralysis}. $$

The first pretends we possess a precise probability distribution when we do not. The second treats every conceivable possibility as equally decision-relevant. Neither is rational. Robust decision-making under deep uncertainty is better understood as searching for policies that perform acceptably across a structured range of plausible futures, rather than optimizing against one assumed future. That is also how robust decision methods are used in policy analysis: robustness is contrasted with optimization for one particular assumed state of the world, and strategies are tested across many plausible futures. The strong agnostic argument therefore does not say:

$$ \text{choose the safest imaginable action}. $$

It says:

$$ \boxed{ \text{do not make the justification of a major commitment depend excessively upon unresolved and weakly grounded modeling choices.} } $$

That is considerably less conservative.

Objection 5: Life itself may force a choice

A related Jamesian objection is that agnosticism may falsely represent the religious question as optional. Perhaps one cannot simply remain outside the decision. If Christianity is true, failing to commit is itself a response to Christianity. If God desires relationship, refusing to enter that relationship has consequences. More broadly, every way of living embodies assumptions about what matters. There may therefore be no genuinely neutral stance. This objection identifies an important truth:

$$ \text{not choosing one policy} $$

is often itself

$$ \text{choosing another policy}. $$

Strong agnosticism should not deny this. An agnostic still lives. They spend time. Form relationships. Make moral decisions. Raise children. Allocate resources. No one can place life on hold until metaphysics is solved. But this does not imply that every practical policy requires a settled ultimist interpretation. One can make first-order decisions while remaining uncertain about their ultimate metaphysical grounding. For example, an agnostic can value friendship without knowing whether friendship has transcendent significance. They can oppose cruelty without first settling divine command theory. They can raise children with intellectual openness without possessing a completed theory of ultimate reality. Thus:

$$ \text{practical action} \not\Rightarrow \text{metaphysical closure}. $$

The relevant distinction is between being forced to live and being forced to adopt one highly specific explanation of reality. The former is unavoidable. The latter is not obviously so. Moreover, where life does force a decision, the Robust Commitment Principle remains applicable. It may recommend selecting the best available action while keeping one's metaphysical interpretation provisional. The fact that action cannot be delayed does not entail that belief must become more confident.

Objection 6: Perhaps faith is intrinsically valuable

A different response does not treat faith merely as a means to salvation. Perhaps faith itself is a virtue. Trust may be valuable precisely because it exceeds what evidence compels. Love, friendship, and interpersonal relationships often require vulnerability and commitment in advance of certainty. Perhaps the demand for robust evidence misunderstands the kind of relationship religious faith is supposed to be. This objection is strongest when religious belief is modeled analogously to interpersonal trust. But the analogy has limits. Ordinary interpersonal trust normally presupposes that the other person has already been identified. The uncertainty concerns:

$$ \text{whether this person is trustworthy}. $$

The ultimist problem occurs earlier:

$$ \text{which entity, if any, is there to trust?} $$

and

$$ \text{which alleged communications genuinely originate from it?} $$

Faith might rationally exceed evidence concerning the reliability of an identified agent. It is harder to see how faith can itself solve uncertainty concerning which agent or model is real. Otherwise trust becomes model-generating rather than responsive to a previously identified object. The distinction is:

$$ \text{trust under uncertainty about an identified person} $$

versus

$$ \text{selecting which possible person exists through trust itself}. $$

These are not the same problem. Strong agnosticism does not deny that trust can possess intrinsic value. It questions whether the value of trust provides a model-independent reason to select one disputed theology as its object.

Objection 7: Strong agnosticism may be self-undermining

Finally, perhaps strong agnosticism applies its skepticism selectively. The argument itself relies upon decision principles, claims about rationality, models of human cognition, and judgments about evidence. Why should those escape the underdetermination imposed upon theology? This is an important methodological challenge. The answer is that they should not escape it. The Robust Commitment Principle is itself defeasible. Its justification depends upon arguments concerning consistency, reversibility, information value, robustness, and proportionality. If a superior theory of rational action were developed, the argument should be revised. Strong agnosticism is therefore not based upon an infallible meta-theory. Its claim is comparative:

$$ \text{given our current understanding of rational choice under deep uncertainty}, $$

the policy of proportional and reversible commitment appears better justified than arbitrary high-confidence model selection. Moreover, the principle is deliberately constructed to be self-applicable. Because it recommends proportional confidence, we should hold the principle itself with a degree of confidence proportionate to its justification. Thus the position need not say:

$$ \text{“Strong agnosticism is certainly the uniquely rational position.”} $$

It can say:

$$ \boxed{ \text{“The case for strong agnosticism is presently more robust than the case for model-specific metaphysical commitment.”} } $$

That conclusion is appropriately defeasible.

What survives the objections

These objections significantly qualify the argument. Their effect is easiest to see when the concessions are placed side by side.

Objection What the argument should concede
Scientific underdetermination Uncertainty alone cannot justify suspension.
Jamesian / participation-dependent evidence Participation can generate evidence that detached inquiry cannot access.
Cost of delay Agnosticism is not free; waiting can sacrifice goods and opportunities.
Excessive conservatism Robustness cannot mean maximizing against worst-case fantasies or treating every imaginable model as decision-relevant.
Forced choice Practical life cannot be indefinitely suspended, even while metaphysical judgment remains provisional.
The value of faith Some forms of commitment may possess non-evidential value.
Self-undermining concern The argument must remain modest about its own decision theory and treat the Robust Commitment Principle as defeasible rather than certain.

But none of these defeats the central claim. Instead, they refine it. Strong agnosticism should not be formulated as:

$$ \text{“Whenever uncertainty exists, withhold commitment.”} $$

It should be formulated as:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &\text{When model uncertainty is deep,}\\ &\text{when no model is robustly identified,}\\ &\text{when commitment is substantially model-specific,}\\ &\text{when commitment creates meaningful exposure or path dependence,}\\ &\text{and when additional information can be acquired,}\\ &\text{rational agents have prima facie reason to preserve optionality}\\ &\text{and keep commitment proportional to evidential robustness.} \end{aligned} } $$

Under different conditions, the recommendation could change. If one theology became strongly discriminated from its competitors, commitment could become rational. If delaying commitment imposed independently established catastrophic costs, immediate action could become rational. If participation were necessary to acquire highly discriminatory evidence, exploratory participation could become rational. The argument is therefore responsive to precisely the variables a serious decision procedure should track. Its conclusion is conditional, defeasible, and adaptive. But given the epistemic conditions concerning ultimism argued for in Part I, those conditions currently appear to favor agnostic inquiry over high-confidence model-specific religious commitment. That leaves only the final task: to state the argument in its complete form and clarify exactly how strong a conclusion it supports.

XIV. The Argument for Strong Agnosticism

The argument developed in this essay can now be stated in full. Its conclusion is not that God does not exist. It is not that naturalism is true. It is not that all religious belief is irrational, or that ultimate reality is unknowable in principle. The conclusion is narrower, but stronger than ordinary agnosticism:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Given our present epistemic condition, high-confidence model-specific commitment concerning ultimate reality is not rationally warranted.} } $$

And because practical commitment must be made under that same epistemic condition,

$$ \boxed{ \text{agnostic inquiry is the rational default policy under deep ultimist uncertainty.} } $$

The argument reaches this conclusion in two stages.

Part I: Epistemic underdetermination

The first stage begins with the structure of the hypothesis space. The proposition

$$ G=\text{“God exists”} $$

is not itself a single predictive model. Nor is the broader proposition

$$ U=\text{“some ultimist account of reality is true.”} $$

Instead, ultimism contains a large and plausibly open-ended family of models:

$$ U=\{M_1,M_2,M_3,\ldots\}, $$

including classical monotheism, deism, pantheism, panentheism, polytheistic models, finite-God views, nonpersonal forms of ultimacy, and perhaps possibilities not yet conceived. These models make very different claims about the structure of reality. More importantly, they generate very different expectations about evidence. To say that some observation \(E\) favors ultimism therefore requires something like

$$ P(E\mid U) = \sum_i P(E\mid M_i)P(M_i\mid U). $$

But this requires a probability distribution over the ultimist model space. No such distribution follows from the bare concept of ultimism. The problem becomes deeper still because many individual God-models do not independently determine their own observational likelihoods. A personal God-model typically requires further assumptions about divine purposes, preferences, and behavior:

$$ M_i \rightarrow \theta_i \rightarrow E. $$

Thus even

$$ P(E\mid M_i) $$

may depend upon poorly constrained variables describing divine psychology. The likelihood problem is therefore hierarchical. We are uncertain about which models exist, which models are epistemically admissible, how they should be weighted, how their internal parameters should be specified, and what likelihoods those specifications generate.

At the same time, the principal mechanisms by which one might hope to resolve this uncertainty are themselves undercut. Divine hiddenness raises questions about models predicting relational availability. Conflicting revelations weaken revelation as a unique discriminator. The demographic and cultural contingency of religious belief raises questions about the truth-sensitivity of religious belief formation. Religious experience is powerful but difficult to calibrate across incompatible interpretations. Draper-style cumulative reasoning requires sufficiently specified comparative likelihoods. Schellenberg's broader skepticism raises the possibility that our current ultimist concepts are historically immature. Persistent sophisticated disagreement supplies higher-order evidence about the limited resolving power of our current argumentative methods. The cumulative result is not simply:

$$ \text{some evidence favors theism} $$

and

$$ \text{some evidence favors naturalism}. $$

It is more fundamental:

$$ \boxed{ \text{the inference problem itself remains substantially underdetermined at the level of model construction.} } $$

This is why the resulting epistemic state should not be represented simply as

$$ P(U)=0.5. $$

That would imply more precision than has been justified. The more defensible conclusion is:

$$ \boxed{ P(U\mid E)\text{ is poorly identified by our present epistemic framework.} } $$

This establishes epistemic agnosticism. But epistemic agnosticism alone does not yet determine how anyone should live. That requires the second half of the argument.

Part II: Rational commitment under deep uncertainty

Once deep underdetermination is admitted, a familiar response becomes available:

“Perhaps we do not know. But we can still believe.”

As a psychological description, this may be entirely accurate. As a normative claim, however, it requires a theory of rational action under uncertainty. The relevant decision is not necessarily whether one can voluntarily choose belief. Even if direct doxastic voluntarism is false, agents can choose degrees of religious commitment, participation, authority, practice, financial investment, community affiliation, and deliberate cultivation of belief. The decision space is therefore something like

$$ A= \{ A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_n,A_0 \}, $$

where \(A_i\) represents commitment to some model \(M_i\) and

$$ A_0=\text{continued agnostic inquiry}. $$

Ordinary Pascalian expected-utility reasoning cannot resolve this problem without importing precisely the theological structure that Part I placed in question. Expected utility requires

$$ EU(a) = \sum_{\theta}P(\theta)U(a,\theta). $$

But in the ultimist case, both terms are unstable. We do not possess a uniquely justified

$$ P(\theta), $$

and many of the relevant utilities are generated by the disputed theological models themselves:

$$ U(a,\theta). $$

Claims such as

$$ \text{“nonbelief risks eternal punishment”} $$

cannot enter the matrix as neutral facts. They are consequences of particular theories of God, revelation, judgment, and salvation. To use those consequences to justify the theology that generated them is therefore circular unless independent reasons already privilege that theology. The many-gods problem is, in this sense, a special case of the larger model-space problem.

The decision principle

The positive decision-theoretic argument therefore begins from a different rule. The Robust Commitment Principle states:

When model uncertainty is deep, when no option robustly dominates, and when the relevant probabilities and consequences are poorly identified, rational commitment should be proportionate to evidential robustness. All else equal, agents should prefer policies that reduce unsupported model-specific exposure, preserve reversibility, retain access to information, and remain responsive to future evidence.

This principle does not say:

$$ \text{always wait}. $$

It does not say:

$$ \text{always choose the safest option}. $$

And it does not say:

$$ \text{nonbelief is inherently preferable}. $$

Its recommendation varies according to the structure of the decision. Under asymmetric danger, it may recommend immediate precaution. Under investment uncertainty, it may recommend diversification. Under irreversible uncertainty, it may recommend delay. Under cheap experimentation, it may recommend exploration. Under improved information, it may recommend stronger commitment. The relevant invariant is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{do not expose yourself more strongly to model error than your information justifies.} } $$

Why agnostic inquiry emerges

When this principle is applied to the current ultimist problem, agnostic inquiry acquires several structural advantages. It is comparatively low in model specificity. It does not require committing to one particular account of God, revelation, salvation, divine psychology, or ultimate reality. It preserves the possibility of future updating. It retains the value of information. It reduces the cost of having committed prematurely to the wrong model. It can include exploratory religious engagement. And it can selectively adopt practices whose value survives metaphysical uncertainty. In simplified form:

$$ A_0 = \text{low model dependence} + \text{high revisability} + \text{high information preservation}. $$

Specific religious commitment may offer greater benefit under some models. But it may also depend upon a longer conjunction of disputed claims:

$$ G\land R\land S\land J\land P. $$

The stronger and more exclusive the commitment, the more of that conjunction must be sufficiently justified. This creates a general burden of specificity:

$$ \boxed{ \text{greater model-specific commitment requires greater evidential discrimination.} } $$

Agnostic inquiry is therefore not privileged by stipulation. It emerges because the decision problem contains asymmetric features. The uncertainty is deep. The state space is poorly identified. Future information remains possible. Some commitments are partially irreversible. Commitment can alter the future informational environment. And no specific ultimist model presently appears robustly identified enough to eliminate these concerns.

The complete argument

The argument can now be stated compactly.

Argument I: Epistemic Underdetermination

  1. Rational evidential discrimination requires sufficiently specified competing models.

  2. Ultimism encompasses a large, heterogeneous, and plausibly open-ended space of models.

  3. Generic ultimism does not itself determine observational likelihoods.

  4. More specific ultimist models require additional assumptions concerning divine nature, psychology, intentions, revelation, and behavior.

  5. These assumptions are themselves disputed and often weakly constrained.

  6. The principal proposed mechanisms for identifying the correct ultimist model—revelation, religious experience, philosophical argument, and cumulative evidence—are subject to substantial undercutting considerations.

  7. Therefore our present evidence does not provide a sufficiently robust and model-independent procedure for determining which ultimist model, if any, is true.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{epistemic suspension concerning ultimism is presently warranted.} } $$
Argument II: Robust Commitment

  1. Rational commitment should be sensitive not merely to evidential support, but also to uncertainty, consequences, reversibility, and exposure to model error.

  2. Under deep ultimist uncertainty, no uniquely justified probability distribution over theological states is available.

  3. Model-specific supernatural rewards and punishments cannot rationally privilege one commitment without independent justification for the theology generating them.

  4. Strong religious commitment can create substantial opportunity costs, irreversibility, path dependence, and epistemic feedback effects.

  5. Agnostic inquiry is comparatively less model-specific, more reversible, and more compatible with continued information acquisition.

  6. Under deep uncertainty, absent robust dominance or independently established urgency, rational commitment should remain proportionate to evidential robustness and preserve valuable optionality.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{agnostic inquiry is the rational default policy under present ultimist uncertainty.} } $$

Together:

$$ \boxed{ \text{deep epistemic underdetermination} + \text{robust rational commitment} \Rightarrow \text{strong agnosticism}. } $$

What “strong” means

The word strong should not be misunderstood. Strong agnosticism is not stronger because it claims greater certainty. In fact, it claims less. It is stronger because it makes a normative claim rather than merely reporting a private psychological state. Weak agnosticism may say:

$$ \text{“I do not know whether God exists.”} $$

Epistemic agnosticism says:

$$ \text{“The presently available evidence does not sufficiently determine the matter.”} $$

Strong agnosticism adds:

$$ \boxed{ \text{“Given that underdetermination, high-confidence model-specific commitment is not presently warranted, and rational agents have prima facie reason to preserve epistemic optionality.”} } $$

This is not equivalent to atheism. Atheism makes a substantive claim about reality. Strong agnosticism makes a claim about the relationship between our present epistemic position and the level of commitment that position justifies. The distinction is essential.

A defeasible conclusion

The argument is therefore not permanent by design. Suppose a future observation strongly discriminated among ultimist models. Suppose a form of revelation became independently verifiable. Suppose some new philosophical framework sharply constrained the space of divine possibilities. Suppose a theological model began generating precise, successful, and independently risky predictions. Then the epistemic situation would change. And if the epistemic situation changed sufficiently, so should the decision. Thus:

$$ \text{strong agnosticism at }t_0 $$

does not imply

$$ \text{strong agnosticism at }t_1. $$

The policy is adaptive. Its commitment is not to doubt forever, but to maintain the relationship

$$ \boxed{ \text{degree of commitment} \propto \text{robustness of justification}. } $$

That principle is precisely what makes the position more than skepticism for its own sake.

Conclusion

Discussions of religion often treat the problem as though rationality required selecting whichever side has accumulated the most persuasive arguments. But persuasion is not the same thing as model discrimination, and model discrimination is not yet the same thing as rational commitment. The full structure is closer to:

$$ \text{evidence} \rightarrow \text{model identification} \rightarrow \text{epistemic confidence} \rightarrow \text{decision under uncertainty} \rightarrow \text{degree of commitment}. $$

Each transition requires justification. The argument of this essay has been that, in the case of ultimism, the earliest transitions remain deeply unstable. The model space is poorly constrained, its likelihoods often depend upon disputed divine psychology, the mechanisms proposed for identifying the correct model are themselves undercut, and the resulting uncertainty propagates forward into the decision problem. The rational response to that condition is neither to declare naturalism true nor to select one theological model merely because its promised rewards are large. It is to acknowledge the uncertainty at the level where it actually exists and allow that uncertainty to constrain the strength of our commitments. Strong agnosticism is therefore not the conclusion that ultimate reality is inaccessible. It is the conclusion that where our models outrun our ability to discriminate among them, our commitments should not outrun our evidence.
