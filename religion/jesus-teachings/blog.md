# Before Christianity: Compassion, Nonretaliation, and Justice Across the Ancient World

*An evidence-based essay and extensive non-biblical compendium, from the earliest written wisdom traditions to the philosophers of imperial Rome.*

> **The historical claim at issue is not whether Jesus or Christianity expressed ethical ideas distinctively.** It is whether compassion, forgiveness, nonretaliation, generosity, concern for strangers, and solidarity were *unknown* outside Christianity until Christians taught them. The surviving record makes that exclusive-origin claim untenable.

**Scope.** This work intentionally excludes Hebrew biblical texts, Jewish ethical literature, and Jewish philosophers, including Philo. Their exclusion is methodological, not a suggestion they are irrelevant. Here the focus is entirely on **non-biblical, non-Jewish traditions**. Christian texts appear only as evidence for the *ancient argument about Christian originality*, especially Celsus and Origen, not as sources for the non-Christian ethics surveyed. The geographic survey covers literate traditions of **ancient Afro-Eurasia**, not the entire planet. The absence of another region reflects limits of available evidence and scope, not an absence of moral thought there.

**Reading paths.** The [opening debate](#opening-the-debate-was-already-happening-in-antiquity) and [five-minute argument](#the-argument-in-five-minutes) provide the short essay. [Eight extended case studies](#eight-primary-source-case-studies) let readers compare the strongest witnesses closely. The [regional source atlas](#the-regional-source-atlas) preserves the extensive compendium, followed by [Roman institutional evidence](#roman-laws-customs-and-public-provision), [limits of the argument](#four-serious-objections-and-what-the-evidence-supports), a [thematic concordance](#thematic-concordance), and an [annotated bibliography](#annotated-reading-list).

**Dating note.** "Pre-Christian" sometimes means "before the emergence of Christianity as a movement," not necessarily "before the conventional start of the Common Era." Where that difference matters, the specific date is given. Material written in the first or second centuries CE is labeled as a *non-Christian continuation* rather than used to prove chronological precedence. Quotations are brief, attributed, and tied to passages; otherwise descriptions are paraphrases.

## Opening: The debate was already happening in antiquity

In the later second century CE, a philosopher named **Celsus** contended that a Christian moral instruction had an earlier Greek precedent. His example was the rule against retaliation, expressed in the memorable instruction to offer the other cheek when struck. He turned not to a modern critic, but to a fourth-century-BCE Greek dialogue: Plato's *Crito*.

In that dialogue, Socrates asks whether it is ever just to answer one injustice with another. His answer is no. Being hurt, he argues, does not make it morally permissible to hurt someone wrongfully in return. Celsus recognized the overlap and claimed that the Christian formulation was a coarser expression of an older teaching. In the English translation preserved by the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* collection, his criticism begins: **“This is an ancient saying”** (*Against Celsus* 7.58). [Read the transmitted passage](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04167.htm).

The episode is unusually valuable because Celsus's critique has not survived independently. We know it through **Origen**, the Christian writer who answered him around 248 CE. In *Against Celsus* 7.58, Origen reproduces the comparison with the *Crito*; in 7.59–61 he replies. He argues that a valid moral truth does not cease to be valid because others have taught it; then he maintains that Christian teaching communicates such truths in a form intelligible to ordinary people and therefore capable of broader moral effect. In the same public-domain English translation, Origen says an overlapping source **“does not affect the value of the truth communicated”** (7.59).

Celsus alleged a kind of borrowing and degradation. That allegation is **not proven** merely because Plato's passage is earlier. But Origen's response is equally illuminating: his defense did not require insisting that the Greek philosophers were ignorant of moral truth. Instead, he argued about *form, accessibility, and practical force*. Whatever one thinks of either polemicist, the exchange reveals that the distinction between **inventing a principle** and **making that principle persuasive** was already being debated in antiquity.

It also gives this essay a useful standard of proof. We can demonstrate that a particular moral idea is recorded before Christianity without claiming that Jesus read the earlier source. We can observe resemblance without erasing differences in metaphysics, social setting, or the kind of action demanded. We can recognize a tradition's effect on later history without inventing an exclusive beginning for it.

What follows widens the lens beyond Socrates. Babylonian wisdom literature advised kindness to adversaries. Egyptian instructions considered the poor, the widow, and the stranger. Indian traditions cultivated non-hatred, noninjury, and boundless goodwill. Chinese philosophers argued over reciprocity, inclusive care, and compassionate government. Greek thinkers questioned retaliation and the morality of harming enemies. Romans expressed expectations of clemency, justice, good faith, and public provision in both literature and institutions. These witnesses do not all teach the same system. They establish something narrower and historically decisive: **compassion and restraint had lengthy, diverse, non-Christian intellectual histories**.

The modern argument is sometimes framed as a choice between saying Christianity invented love and saying it simply copied older religions. Those are not the only alternatives. There is a much better historical account: Christian ethics emerged amid civilizations already debating moral obligations, and it developed its own configurations of ideas within that larger world.

### The terms must not be blurred

| Concept | What it means here | A strong ancient example |
|---|---|---|
| **Nonretaliation** | Wrongdoing by another does not entitle one to commit wrongful injury | Socrates, *Crito* 49b–d |
| **Forgiveness and forbearance** | Releasing resentment, refusing revenge, or moderating personal punishment; meanings vary | *Dhammapada* 3–5; Seneca on anger |
| **Positive care for adversaries** | Treating someone who acts badly with active goodwill or assistance | *Counsels of Wisdom*; *Dao De Jing* 49 |
| **Clemency** | A superior's restraint when entitled or empowered to punish | Roman *clementia*; Augustus's shield |
| **Beneficence** | Providing tangible help, resources, or protection | Egyptian instruction; civic benefaction |
| **Compassion / loving-kindness** | Concern for another's suffering, whether as emotion, discipline, or obligation | Buddhist *karuṇā* and *mettā*; Mencius |
| **Inclusive moral concern** | Duties extending beyond kin, city, rank, or—in some traditions—species | Mozi; early Stoicism; Jain nonviolence |
| **Legal justice and public provision** | Procedures and material institutions that regulate harms or provide goods | Roman appeals, extortion laws, grain distribution |

None of these concepts entails the others automatically. A law compensating property damage is not enemy-love. Nor is an emperor's pardon the same thing as forgiveness between equals. Clear comparisons require specifying *which* proposition a source actually supports.

### Three different questions about each witness

1. **When is the relevant wording securely attested?** A fourth-century-BCE dialogue has a different chronological weight from a saying attributed to a pre-Christian founder but preserved in a later collection. Ancient oral transmission and surviving manuscript dates also differ.
2. **What kind of evidence is it?** An ethical dialogue proves the principle was articulated; an inscription proves the ruler publicly endorsed a claim; a law proves a rule was adopted; a literary anecdote proves the conduct was intelligibly praised. None automatically measures compliance.
3. **How close is the parallel?** A *close* match directly expresses the proposition under discussion. A *moderate* match addresses the same obligation in a different way. A *contextual* example concerns public ethics or social practices but should not be recruited as proof of forgiveness or love.

### A compact chronology of unambiguous early witnesses

| Approximate date of document or composition | Source | What it independently establishes |
|---|---|---|
| Third millennium BCE | Sumerian *Instructions of Shuruppak* (early manuscript witnesses) | Articulated norms against harm, deceit, theft, and social disorder |
| Second millennium BCE | Egyptian *The Eloquent Peasant* and *Amenemope* tradition | Justice for vulnerable petitioners and ethical assistance to those in need |
| First millennium BCE (surviving tablets) | Akkadian *Counsels of Wisdom* | Kind conduct toward adversaries attested in pre-Christian manuscripts |
| Fifth–fourth centuries BCE | Plato's *Crito* and *Republic* | Direct prohibition against wrongful retaliation and against harming enemies as justice |
| Fourth–third centuries BCE | Mozi, Mencius, and classical Chinese ethical texts | Inclusive care, humane concern, and compassionate models of government |
| 260s–230s BCE | Ashoka's public inscriptions | Dated ruler's statements on suffering, moral conduct, tolerance, and public provision |
| 44 BCE | Cicero, *De Officiis* | Duties toward the injured, the stranger, and those who have wronged us |
| 27 BCE | Augustan shield of virtue, corroborated by a surviving copy | Clemency, justice, and duty honored as public political virtues |

This timeline distinguishes strong *documentary priority* from traditions whose surviving wording is later. The former alone suffices to disprove exclusive first invention; the latter broadens our understanding of ancient moral thought without needing artificial dates.

## Eight primary-source case studies

These case studies unpack the best evidence in more detail before the atlas resumes its encyclopedic breadth. The **source**, **comparison**, and **limit** are deliberately kept distinct.

### Case 1: Babylonia: kindness toward an adversary

The Akkadian *Counsels of Wisdom* is a remarkable text because it does not merely warn against criminal conduct or insist on politeness within a household. Among its instructions are lines counseling restraint and positive treatment of an adversary. Depending on translation and reconstruction, the injunction encourages the hearer to show the antagonist kindness and to respond to hostile conduct without becoming hostile in turn. It expresses an ethical intuition familiar from much later debates: the injury someone inflicts need not be reproduced by the person injured.

The date is worth getting right. Some accessible retellings casually date the text to the second millennium BCE as though that were a firmly established publication date. The [electronic Babylonian Library's textual introduction](https://www.ebl.badw.de/corpus/L/2/3) instead reports that the surviving manuscripts are **first-millennium-BCE** witnesses; composition in the latter half of the second millennium is possible but not certain. One datable manuscript was copied in the reign of Artaxerxes I or II, in either 458/457 or 398/397 BCE. The resulting claim is more than sufficient: this ethical advice existed in cuneiform transmission before Christianity by several centuries.

The larger work offers advice for a social world of scribes, families, courtly relationships, administration, and reputation. It is neither a universal charter of equality nor a treatise devoted solely to the love of enemies. Some of its prescriptions reinforce the social hierarchy of their time. But that is precisely why the counsel about adversaries is telling. The moral vocabulary of restraint and positive treatment was not imported into Mesopotamia by a later Christian missionary; it appears in a historically independent Near Eastern wisdom tradition.

**Evidence:** pre-Christian manuscript tradition; wisdom instruction. **Parallel:** close for positive response to antagonism; not proof of a universal doctrine of forgiveness. **Sources:** [electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.badw.de/corpus/L/2/3); Benjamin R. Foster, *Before the Muses* (modern translation, consult an authorized edition).

### Case 2: Egypt: the poor and the stranger

The *Instruction of Amenemope* combines practical prudence with overt moral concern. Its counsel against attacking the weak and taking advantage of the vulnerable goes beyond a generic instruction to obey those above oneself. In chapters 28–29 it specifically warns against abusing a widow encountered in the fields, recommends sharing with a stranger, and advises the operator of a ferry to charge the person who can afford the fare while allowing a poor traveler to cross. Its point is strikingly concrete: ethical conduct can require declining to extract every available advantage from someone in need.

Chapter 2 adds a different but related thought: the person who does wrong should not necessarily receive the same wrongdoing back. Instead the reader is encouraged to restrain anger and meet hostile behavior without becoming a mirror image of it. The result is a more textured ethic than mere social order. It includes guarded speech, consideration for the weak, generosity, and the discipline of not reproducing aggression.

Modern translations differ at several difficult points, and individual readings depend on the manuscripts and on Egyptological judgment. We should not take an attractive English rendering as a fixed original quotation. But the chapter and passage locators allow the reader to test the evidence. As a pre-Christian instructional tradition, *Amenemope* also demonstrates that practical concern for the destitute could be framed as a matter of religious morality *outside* Christianity.

What it does **not** establish is a state-administered program that reliably ferried all poor passengers for free. It is an ethical text, not an audit of Egyptian transport. Nor is its concern necessarily disinterested: the gods, social reputation, and prudence shape the instruction. Moral motivation in antiquity often combined religious, personal, and social reasons. Its central historical point remains: the assistance of strangers and vulnerable people was a recognizable object of ethical teaching well before Christianity.

**Evidence:** Egyptian wisdom text, pre-Christian composition and witnesses. **Parallel:** close for generosity, restraint, care for vulnerable strangers. **Sources:** [chapters 2, 28–29 in an English text](https://www.touregypt.net/instructionofamenemope.htm); [*The Literature of Ancient Egypt* (Simpson, ed.)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5vm2m5.31).

### Case 3: Buddhism: non-hatred under ordinary and extreme injury

The Buddhist record is unusually useful because different texts express different strengths of the same concern. In *Dhammapada* 3–5, dwelling on the memory of insults is portrayed as a mechanism that sustains conflict; hatred is not overcome through further hatred. In verse 223, the teaching becomes active: anger is to be answered with the contrary virtue. These are not merely exhortations to avoid breaking the law. They address the *mental habits* through which people sustain cycles of vengeance.

The *Mettā Sutta* (*Sutta Nipāta* 1.8) broadens the horizon. It encourages goodwill toward living beings without making the recipient's worthiness the condition of benevolence. The *Simile of the Saw* (*Majjhima Nikāya* 21) intensifies the demand: even under an extreme description of physical attack, the ideal practitioner is to guard against hatred and retain goodwill rather than return brutality as brutality. This is closer to a radical discipline of enemy-directed goodwill than the vague word "kindness" usually conveys.

There are meaningful differences from Christian enemy-love. Buddhist ethics locates the cultivation of *mettā* within a practical program for confronting craving, aversion, and suffering. Loving-kindness is an exercise in liberating the mind as well as caring for others. It also reaches beyond humans to living beings more broadly. Some formulations are aspirational contemplative practices rather than instructions for civil law or crime control. These differences help explain what the texts *mean*; they do not make their non-Christian ethical content vanish.

The evidence also requires a dating discipline. The early Buddhist movement predates Christianity, as the inscriptions of Ashoka independently establish. But the precise wording of a canonical verse preserved in later oral and written collections need not be as old as the historical Buddha. We can affirm the antiquity of the Buddhist tradition while presenting each quoted passage as part of the surviving canonical collection rather than a verified stenographic saying of its founder.

**Evidence:** early non-Christian Buddhist tradition with later textual transmission. **Parallel:** close for non-hatred and active goodwill, with qualification about dating individual verses. **Sources:** [*Dhammapada*](https://suttacentral.net/dhp/en/sujato), [*Mettā Sutta*](https://suttacentral.net/snp1.8/en/sujato), [*Majjhima Nikāya* 21](https://suttacentral.net/mn21/en/sujato).

### Case 4: China: care beyond one's own group

The Chinese evidence is strongest when the differences among its thinkers are kept visible. **Mozi** and the Mohists developed *jian ai*—often translated as "universal love," though **inclusive care** avoids implying that everyone must feel identical affection. The Mohists believed that partiality toward one's own family, state, or faction was a major source of aggression. They asked people and rulers to take the interests of others seriously across those boundaries, and they connected the principle to opposition to offensive warfare and policies that benefit the populace.

This did not make Mozi a modern egalitarian or eliminate every special obligation. His point was that ignoring other people's welfare simply because they lie outside one's group is morally and socially destructive. The argument has a distinctly political dimension: people and states should order conduct so that one person's or community's advantage does not come from injuring outsiders. It is therefore not only a private sentiment but a model of cooperative social life.

**Confucius** and **Mencius** offer an instructive contrast. The Confucian tradition often gives particular weight to familial bonds and graded duties while still requiring reciprocity and humane government. Mencius's example of a child at the brink of a well (*Mencius* 2A.6) argues that compassionate alarm can appear without a prior calculation of profit. That is an account of an ordinary human moral capacity, not a claim that compassion suddenly becomes possible only after hearing a religious teacher.

The *Dao De Jing* contributes a different kind of parallel. Chapter 49 associates the sage with goodness even toward those who are not good, and chapter 63 speaks of responding to hostility with virtue. These passages concern the cultivation of a particular kind of character and governance, not the Mohist machinery of public advantage. Their composition dates and textual strata are debated, but the tradition's pre-Christian roots are well established.

**Evidence:** pre-Christian Chinese philosophical corpora, with varying textual histories. **Parallel:** close for inclusive moral concern and positive treatment of unkind people. **Sources:** [*Mozi*, "Inclusive Care" (Chinese Text Project)](https://ctext.org/mozi/book-4); [*Analects*](https://ctext.org/analects); [*Mencius* 2A.6](https://ctext.org/mengzi/gong-sun-chou-i); [*Dao De Jing* 49](https://ctext.org/dao-de-jing).

### Case 5: Plato: wrongdoing does not license revenge

Socrates, as Plato represents him, argues in *Crito* 49b–d from a very simple but demanding premise: one should never do what is unjust. Crito is asked whether suffering an injustice changes that principle. It does not. The fact that someone else has injured you cannot transform wrongful retaliation into justice. This is not a sentimental request to like a persecutor; it is a claim about the stability of moral duty under provocation.

The surrounding drama makes the argument sharper. Socrates is imprisoned after an Athenian conviction and has an opportunity to escape. His inquiry is not undertaken from a place of safety. He is concerned that fleeing in defiance of what he regards as obligations to the city could make him guilty of a wrong. One need not agree with every part of his political reasoning to recognize the nonretaliation principle expressed in the dialogue. Indeed, the larger question of citizens' obligations to unjust institutions remains contested.

Plato makes a related argument in *Republic* 335b–e. The conventional slogan that justice consists of helping friends and harming enemies cannot survive Socrates' objection: injuring a person makes the person worse, whereas justice cannot have the distinctive function of making people more unjust. Another pre-Christian text, the *Apology* 41d–e, portrays Socrates declining to nurse anger toward those responsible for his sentence. The three passages do not teach identical things, but together they make it impossible to describe Greek philosophical morality as indifferent to wrongdoing committed against oneself.

The limits are important. *Crito* is not a complete theory of forgiving someone emotionally, and Plato is not there giving a universal ban on all legitimate punishment or force. Refusal to commit **injustice** in retaliation is the narrower claim. It is also a sufficiently direct parallel that Celsus could later invoke it against Christian assertions of moral novelty.

**Evidence:** securely fourth-century-BCE philosophical dialogues. **Parallel:** exceptionally close for nonretaliation. **Sources:** [Plato, *Crito*](https://adfonteslibrary.org/work-plato-crito); [*Republic*](https://adfonteslibrary.org/work-plato-republic); [*Apology*](https://chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/plato-the-apology-of-socrates-sb/).

### Case 6: India: an emperor acknowledges suffering

Ashoka's inscriptions supply a kind of evidence that philosophical dialogues cannot: public proclamations issued under the authority of a specific third-century-BCE ruler. In Major Rock Edict XIII, Ashoka refers to the conquest of Kalinga and describes the deaths, deportations, and grief it produced. He then associates moral achievement with *dhamma* rather than conquest through violence. The inscription does not erase the conquest; it makes the human suffering caused by imperial power a subject of royal moral self-evaluation.

The other edicts matter too. Major Rock Edict II records medical provisions for humans and animals, the cultivation of useful plants, and related public works. Edict XII counsels restraint and respect across religious communities. These passages document a ruler publicly identifying welfare, tolerance, and moral restraint as duties of government well before Christianity. They are also evidence for the administrative ambition to put at least some of those ideas into public practice.

Two cautions improve the case rather than weakening it. First, ancient edicts are not an independent survey showing that every subject received treatment or that the state was uniformly humane. Second, Ashoka did not renounce imperial government or every form of force. His idea of moral conquest is embedded in an imperial regime that retained the capacity to punish. Those limitations keep the evidence in its proper category: public statements and certain government measures, rather than universal nonviolence fully achieved.

The crucial historical point is nevertheless straightforward. An imperial administration outside the Mediterranean was already making claims about the anguish of war, care for people and animals, and respect for communities roughly three centuries before Christian institutions emerged. The ancient world cannot be partitioned into a compassionate Christian sphere and an unreflective pre-Christian one.

**Evidence:** dated imperial inscriptions and royal self-presentation. **Parallel:** direct for restraint, tolerance, and public welfare; contextual for private forgiveness. **Sources:** [Ashoka's edicts, overview and translations](https://www.cs.colostate.edu/~malaiya/ashoka.html); [scholarly assessment of Ashoka's inscriptions](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ashoka).

### Case 7: Cicero: duties to enemies and strangers

Cicero's *De Officiis* (*On Duties*), composed in 44 BCE for his son, is among the clearest demonstrations that the Roman Republic possessed a sophisticated, systematic account of social morality before Christianity. It is not a single maxim about being pleasant. It investigates what honorable conduct demands when loyalty, advantage, justice, political office, and other people's welfare collide.

In Book I, sections 20–24, Cicero connects justice with avoiding injury and honoring good faith. Sections 33–35 turn to an especially demanding case: the person who has wronged us. Cicero maintains that there remain duties even toward such a person and that responses to wrongdoing require proportion. He goes on to discuss warfare and the treatment of defeated enemies. His moral reasoning refuses the idea that being opposed to someone removes all obligation toward them.

Sections 42–43 guard against a counterfeit version of generosity. Giving to one person by unfairly taking from another is not beneficence but injustice disguised as virtue. Sections 50–52 then extend the moral community outward: human beings share language and reason, and certain kinds of help are owed even to strangers. There is an architectonic logic here. Good faith, beneficence, punishment, and social cooperation are parts of one account of duties rather than disconnected benevolent gestures.

Cicero was an elite Roman statesman, and his ethic reflects social hierarchies and the priorities of a republican ruling class. He did not write a modern declaration of human rights. His treatment of war could justify conquest in ways contemporary readers should scrutinize. But the philosophical accomplishment is real. Rome had a vocabulary of human fellowship and duties toward antagonists long before a Christian empire existed, and Cicero placed it inside an influential treatise on political and private life.

**Evidence:** securely dated pre-Christian prose treatise. **Parallel:** close for duties toward wrongdoers, strangers, and just beneficence. **Sources:** [*De Officiis*, Book I](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Officiis/1A%2A.html); [scholarly overview of Cicero](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cicero/).

### Case 8: Augustus: virtues made visible in political life

In 27 BCE the Roman Senate honored Augustus with a golden shield bearing four virtues: *virtus*, *clementia*, *iustitia*, and *pietas*—courage or excellence, clemency, justice, and dutifulness. Augustus later recorded the award in *Res Gestae* 34, and a marble copy of the shield's inscription survives from Arles. This is an unusually concrete public witness: clemency and justice were not simply ideas in a Greek philosopher's private study; they were virtues the Roman political order chose to advertise on a monument honoring its ruler.

The surviving replica matters because it gives material confirmation of the rhetoric. The inscription's exact phrasing reflects a specific Roman moral and political world, including duty toward gods and country. None of that makes Augustus a saint. The shield was an honorific instrument within a struggle for legitimacy after civil war. A ruler whose career contained brutal political conflict was asserting that he possessed the virtues of peaceable, legitimate leadership. A public ideal may be sincere, strategic, or both.

The *Res Gestae* also describes money and grain distributions, aid to veterans, public works, and treasury support. We should not call these acts indistinguishable from almsgiving to the destitute: many payments were civic, military, or politically useful, and the beneficiaries were selected by status. Their significance is that a non-Christian regime used material provision, public duty, and clemency to describe legitimate rule. That conclusion can be tested against dated inscriptions and laws, not only against philosophical rhetoric.

The most illuminating way to read John Williams's twentieth-century novel *Augustus* alongside this evidence is to treat the novel as a dramatization of tensions already present in the ancient sources. The ruler must be dutiful, merciful, loyal, decisive, protective, and politically successful; these requirements can collide, above all when the affairs of family become affairs of state. Williams did not create ancient Roman morality, and his imagined letters are not primary documents. He makes the contradictions of that morality vivid to modern readers.

**Evidence:** public honors, inscriptions, ruler's self-description, and documented provisions. **Parallel:** direct for clemency and justice as public virtues; contextual for material aid. **Sources:** [*Res Gestae* 3, 15–18, 34](https://droitromain.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/Anglica/resgest_engl.htm); [scholarly study of the Arles shield](https://www.persee.fr/doc/efr_0000-0000_1980_ant_43_1_1294); [Alison Cooley, *Res Gestae Divi Augusti* (Cambridge)](https://www.cambridge.org/highereducation/books/res-gestae-divi-augusti/40040CD015A69989F1EAC2C767C32D7D).

### Mesopotamia and the ancient Near East

*Third to first millennia BCE. Most entries are wisdom texts or royal/legal records, not proof of uniform social practice.*

#### *The Instructions of Shuruppak* — Sumer, third millennium BCE

One of the oldest attested works of wisdom literature is framed as advice given by Shuruppak to his son Ziusudra. Early copies reach back to the third millennium BCE, although the standard reconstructed text draws on later manuscripts. Its instructions discourage theft, robbery, lying, quarrels, and violence; it also addresses family relationships and restraint in speech. Some advice is self-interested or reflects a harshly hierarchical society. What matters here is that prohibitions against exploiting or injuring others were already presented as teachable moral and social wisdom thousands of years before Christianity.

**Primary:** [Oxford's Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature, *Instructions of Shuruppak*, lines 22–41, 60–62 and 250–260](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section5/tr561.htm).

#### Urukagina of Lagash — c. twenty-fourth century BCE

Reform inscriptions attributed to Urukagina represent a king as restraining abuses and promising that widows and orphans will not be handed over to the powerful. The documents are royal self-presentation, not proof of consistent protection. Yet the principle is unambiguous: abusing people without protectors was already recognized as the sort of injustice a legitimate ruler should oppose.

**Evidence:** [Discussion of the Urukagina inscription, Ur-Nammu, and other Near Eastern texts](https://rsc.byu.edu/vol-23-no-3-2022/cry-widow-fatherless-stranger), citing Urukagina Ukg. 04 B XI 30–31. This secondary study includes biblical material in its wider discussion; the evidence used here is exclusively its non-biblical Sumerian source.

#### Ur-Nammu and his law collection — c. twenty-first century BCE

The prologue attributed to Ur-Nammu states that the orphan must not be surrendered to the rich, nor the widow to the powerful, and contrasts people of modest and considerable means. Laws establish remedies for certain injuries and reflect the administration of justice through publicly articulated rules. They do not institute social equality; they nevertheless embody a prior and unmistakable concern with restraining the stronger party.

**Evidence:** [Overview with the Ur-Nammu prologue's surviving lines](https://rsc.byu.edu/vol-23-no-3-2022/cry-widow-fatherless-stranger), citing Ur-Nammu A iv 150–161 and C I 22–II 29.

#### Hammurabi of Babylon — eighteenth century BCE

The prologue to the laws of Hammurabi declares the goal of making justice prevail and preventing the strong from exploiting the weak. The epilogue presents the ruler as a protector of the oppressed. These are not modern equal-protection laws: the specific penalties vary strikingly by class, status, and sex. The historical point is not that Babylonian justice was humane by modern standards, but that public power was already justified by an articulated moral obligation to prevent oppression.

**Primary:** [Law code, prologue and epilogue, Yale Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp); [critical cuneiform and translated prologue, eHammurabi](https://ehammurabi.org/prologue).

#### Akkadian *Counsels of Wisdom* — probably composed in the second millennium BCE; extant manuscripts from the first millennium BCE

The first-millennium-BCE manuscript witnesses securely establish the text as pre-Christian, even if the proposed second-millennium composition cannot be established as precisely. Its advice to act kindly toward an adversary is one of this atlas's closest non-biblical precedents for positive behavior toward a wrongdoer. **Text: [electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.badw.de/corpus/L/2/3). Analysis:** [Case 1](#case-1-babylonia-kindness-toward-an-adversary).

#### Babylonian *Advice to a Prince* — first millennium BCE

This admonitory text presents abusive royal administration as dangerous and unjust, warning that neglecting lawful treatment of subjects threatens political and divine order. Its argument is partly prudential and tied to particular cities, rather than a declaration of universal rights. It nevertheless shows that responsible rule was an ethical as well as a practical expectation.

**Context:** [Studies of Mesopotamian wisdom and royal justice](https://www.ebl.badw.de/corpus/L/2/3); see also the modern overview *Mesopotamian Wisdom*, for the wider genre.

#### Ugaritic *Epic of Aqhat* — Late Bronze Age, second millennium BCE

In this mythological literature from Ugarit, the figure Danel is associated with judging the cases of the widow and the orphan. Here justice toward vulnerable people is a marker of proper judgment outside both the biblical canon and Greek philosophical literature. This is a **literary norm**, not documentation of an enacted legal welfare system.

**Reference:** The Ugaritic *Aqhat* text, often catalogued KTU 1.17–1.19; [historical discussion quoting the widow-and-orphan judgment motif](https://rsc.byu.edu/vol-23-no-3-2022/cry-widow-fatherless-stranger).

Regional conclusion: The ancient Near East demonstrates at least three distinct things: everyday rules against injury, public ideals of protecting the weak, and—in the *Counsels of Wisdom*—a striking instruction to meet the hostility of an adversary with kindness. None requires Christianity to explain its existence.

### Egypt

*Old Kingdom onward; wisdom instructions, stories of justice, funerary ideals, and officials' self-presentations.*

#### The wisdom attributed to Ptahhotep — composition traditionally situated in the Old Kingdom; surviving witnesses later

*The Instruction of Ptahhotep* teaches restraint of temper, thoughtful listening, fair dealing, and circumspection by people in positions of authority. A superior is cautioned not to use status as a license for arrogance. Its advice is often pragmatic and status-conscious. The value of this witness is that good treatment of others, attention to grievances, and self-mastery were part of established Egyptian moral instruction.

**Research:** For source-critical historical setting and Egyptian wisdom as ethical discourse, see [UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, 'Ethics'](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9q57q53s).

#### *The Eloquent Peasant* — Middle Kingdom, early second millennium BCE

This literary narrative follows a peasant seeking remedy after dispossession by a more powerful person. The peasant's pleas treat justice not as mere loyalty to one's social superior but as the obligation of an official to hear and protect the injured. The language of official authority includes care for the orphan, widow, and people without defenders. The drama exposes the contradiction between the celebrated ethic of justice and the actual vulnerability of the poor.

**Sources:** [Survey of ancient Egyptian ethics](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9q57q53s); [discussion of the specific *Eloquent Peasant* motif](https://rsc.byu.edu/vol-23-no-3-2022/cry-widow-fatherless-stranger).

#### *The Instruction for King Merikare* — Middle Kingdom literary tradition

This instruction to a ruler recommends just judgment, preventing wrongful punishment, refusing to oppress widows, and comforting people in distress. It imagines kingship as a moral undertaking, not merely coercive control. The traditional historical setting and the date at which the extant text was composed should not be treated as identical.

**Source:** [Translation excerpts and source discussion](https://rsc.byu.edu/vol-23-no-3-2022/cry-widow-fatherless-stranger).

#### *The Instruction of Amenemope* — probably late second or early first millennium BCE

Chapters 2, 28, and 29 counsel restraint toward the aggressive, practical help to the vulnerable, generosity to strangers, and accommodation for a destitute ferry passenger. These are ethical prescriptions, not administrative records proving that all travelers received such help. **Text:** [*Instruction of Amenemope*](https://www.touregypt.net/instructionofamenemope.htm). **Analysis:** [Case 2](#case-2-egypt-the-poor-and-the-stranger).

#### *Book of the Dead*, chapter 125 — New Kingdom onward, from the second millennium BCE

In the so-called 'negative confession' or declaration of innocence, the dead person denies wrongdoing including exploitation, theft, violence, and improper treatment of others. It depicts moral accountability as a condition of successful judgment after death. These statements are ritual declarations; one cannot infer that every Egyptian lived according to them.

Primary and academic sources: [University College London, *Book of the Dead* 125](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/bd125a.html); [UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, 'Ethics'](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9q57q53s).

#### Tomb autobiographies and the ideal of the benevolent official — Old Kingdom onward

Egyptian commemorative inscriptions repeatedly portray a worthy official as someone who feeds the hungry, clothes the naked, and aids people in distress. They are declarations designed to glorify the dead and therefore cannot be read as disinterested audits of conduct. They are strong evidence, however, for what generosity and benevolence were expected to look like in public self-presentation.

**Context:** [UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, 'Ethics'](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9q57q53s).

Regional conclusion: Egyptian ethics brings together private character, just administration, relief of the vulnerable, and judgment beyond death. None of these domains began with Christianity.

### Iran and Persian political ethics

*The antiquity of core Iranian moral thought is clear; precise dates of individual Avestan layers are contested.*

#### Old Avestan/Zoroastrian moral thought — pre-Christian; precise chronology disputed

The ancient Iranian tradition emphasizes right-minded choice, *asha* (truth/right order), and conduct aligned with the good. The formula conventionally rendered 'good thoughts, good words, good deeds' is attested in *Yasna* 35.2–3. It is inaccurate to treat the whole of later Zoroastrian theology as if every detail were securely available in the prophet's own time; but the ancient textual foundation of the ethical triad is real.

This ethic combines inner intention, truthful speech, and responsible action. It challenges any contention that pre-Christian religion dealt only in ceremonial requirements and lacked an integrated theory of ethical life.

Scholarly reference: Mary Boyce, ["Humata Hūxta Huvaršta," *Encyclopaedia Iranica*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/humata-huxta-huvarsta/); ["Gathas," *Encyclopaedia Iranica*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/).

#### Darius I and the inscription DNb — c. 500 BCE

On his tomb at Naqsh-e Rustam, Darius represents the ideal ruler as a person who prizes right action, opposes the Lie, and holds anger under rational control. He treats self-restraint as a feature of legitimate royal authority. This is royal ideological self-description, not proof of just imperial practice; the inscription is also a concrete, securely pre-Christian record of political ethics.

**Primary:** [Darius, inscription DNb, translation and Old Persian text](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/achaemenid-royal-inscriptions/dnb/).

**Methodological caution — the Cyrus Cylinder.** A sixth-century-BCE royal account of restoration and legitimation is not an ancient "universal human rights charter." See the relevant [museum discussion](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1880-0617-1941). It belongs in a discussion of ideological representations, not as a decisive proof of love or equality.

### South Asia: ethical philosophy, nonviolence, and public welfare

*Pre-Christian movements and inscriptions must be distinguished from precise dates of later canonical redactions.*

#### Early Upanishadic ethics — first millennium BCE

The *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* 5.2 presents an unforgettable threefold ethical lesson: restraint (*dama*), giving (*dana*), and compassion (*daya*). The story assigns these interpretations to different groups receiving the same syllable, before presenting all three as duties worthy of observance. This is a religiously grounded ethic that expressly unites self-control, generosity, and concern for others. The traditional narrative setting is not itself a composition date, but the early Upanishadic corpus is centuries older than Christianity.

**Primary:** [Patrick Olivelle, *The Early Upaniṣads*, translated text, *Brihadaranyaka* 5.2](https://wiswo.org/books/_resources/book-reference-pdfs/Olivelle-1998-The-Early-Upani%E1%B9%A3ads.pdf) (consult the translated passage; reproduction and quoting remain subject to publisher rights).

#### The early Buddhist movement and the Buddha — fifth/fourth centuries BCE traditionally; textual layers later

The ethical vocabulary of early Buddhism includes *karuṇā* (compassion), *mettā* (loving-kindness), and restraint of hatred and harmfulness. A distinctively powerful Buddhist idea is that cultivating goodwill is not contingent upon the other person's deserving it. Enemy, stranger, and friend alike become objects of deliberate moral practice.

We should not pretend that a printed Pali canon represents a stenographic transcript from the Buddha's lifetime. Rather, the traditions were orally transmitted, compiled, and preserved over time. Their secure pre-Christian existence is independently supported by the spread of Buddhism and Ashoka's third-century-BCE inscriptions.


**Reading:** [Scholarly context](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/).
#### *Dhammapada* 3–5 — the rejection of cycles of hatred

*Dhammapada* 3–5 portrays repeated recollection of injuries as fuel for hatred and says hatred is not overcome by further hatred. This is an early Buddhist non-hatred ideal, though the exact verses survive in transmitted collections rather than securely datable lifetime transcripts. **Text:** [*Dhammapada*](https://suttacentral.net/dhp/en/sujato). **Analysis:** [Case 3](#case-3-buddhism-non-hatred-under-ordinary-and-extreme-injury).

#### *Dhammapada* 129–130, 223 — empathy and answering anger with kindness

Verses 129–130 emphasize common vulnerability to suffering; verse 223 recommends answering anger and wrongdoing with their ethical opposites. **Text:** [*Dhammapada* 223](https://suttacentral.net/dhp223/en/sujato). **Analysis:** [Case 3](#case-3-buddhism-non-hatred-under-ordinary-and-extreme-injury).

#### *Mettā Sutta*, *Sutta Nipāta* 1.8 — loving-kindness for all beings

The *Mettā Sutta* teaches goodwill reaching beyond kin and familiar people toward living beings generally. It is not necessarily a historical transcript of the Buddha's own words; its moral scope is nonetheless significant. **Text:** [*Sutta Nipāta* 1.8](https://suttacentral.net/snp1.8/en/sujato). **Analysis:** [Case 3](#case-3-buddhism-non-hatred-under-ordinary-and-extreme-injury).

#### *The Simile of the Saw*, *Majjhima Nikāya* 21 — refusing hatred under extreme attack

In *Majjhima Nikāya* 21, a severe thought experiment about physical attack makes refusal of hatred and continuing goodwill an especially demanding moral ideal. **Text:** [*Simile of the Saw*](https://suttacentral.net/mn21/en/sujato). **Analysis:** [Case 3](#case-3-buddhism-non-hatred-under-ordinary-and-extreme-injury).

#### *Sigālovāda Sutta*, *Dīgha Nikāya* 31 — obligations woven into household life

This discourse describes duties among parents and children, teachers and students, spouses, friends, religious communities, and employers and workers. Its significance lies in treating moral obligation as a network of specific reciprocal duties, including proper care for those in subordinate economic positions. The duties are embedded in social hierarchy, but not reducible to the privileges of the dominant party.

**Primary:** [SuttaCentral, *Dīgha Nikāya* 31](https://suttacentral.net/dn31/en/sujato).

#### *Cakkavatti-Sīhanāda Sutta*, *Dīgha Nikāya* 26 — social poverty and moral disorder

This narrative argues that when a ruler fails to provide for people without means, poverty spreads, then theft and violent responses to theft proliferate. The lesson is not simply that individuals should act well; public policy and the material condition of the poor can shape the moral life of society. This is a particularly important counterexample to any claim that ancient non-Christian traditions lacked a social ethic. Its received canonical form cannot be precisely dated to the historical Buddha.

**Primary:** [SuttaCentral, *Dīgha Nikāya* 26](https://suttacentral.net/dn26/en/sujato).

#### Mahavira and the Jain tradition — sixth/fifth centuries BCE in traditional chronology

Jain ethics placed *ahiṃsā* (noninjury) at the center of a disciplined moral life. Jain vows prohibit killing or harming living beings and place strong emphasis on truthfulness, non-stealing, sexual restraint, and nonpossessiveness. The concern extends far beyond the human species. The tradition's rigorous attention to unintended harm challenges the claim that an expansive concern for others was uniquely Christian.

Textual caution: The *Ācārāṅga Sūtra* preserves early Jain materials, but the surviving textual form passed through oral transmission and later redaction. It is evidence for an ancient tradition; an unqualified first-hand quotation from Mahavira would overstate what is known.

**Primary:** [*Ācārāṅga Sūtra* (public-domain translation)](https://www.sacred-texts.com/jai/sbe22/index.htm) (older translation; check scholarly editions for philological questions).

#### Ashoka — c. 268–232 BCE, an emperor whose words survive on stone

Major Rock Edicts II, XII, and XIII address provision, interreligious restraint, and the suffering caused by war. Unlike many transmitted teachings, they are third-century-BCE inscribed declarations. They document royal moral commitments without establishing that subjects always benefited. **Source: [Edicts overview and translation](https://www.cs.colostate.edu/~malaiya/ashoka.html). Analysis:** [Case 6](#case-6-india-an-emperor-acknowledges-suffering).

#### *Bhagavad Gita* — final text generally located in the last centuries BCE and early centuries CE

The *Bhagavad Gita*, particularly 12.13–15, praises the person who is free of malice toward living beings, friendly, compassionate, restrained, and untroubled by resentment. Its moral reasoning is located within devotion, duty, and liberation, not Christian theology. Its precise composition and incorporation into the *Mahābhārata* are debated, so it is best treated as part of a pre-Christian-origin Indian intellectual tradition with a complex text history, rather than assigning a fabricated exact year.

**Locator:** *Bhagavad Gita* 12.13–15; seek a critical translation alongside the Sanskrit text.


**Reading:** [Primary text and commentary portal](https://www.gitasupersite.iitk.ac.in/).
#### The *Mahābhārata* and the ethic of noninjury — layered composition across centuries

The epic's didactic portions, especially *Śānti Parva* and *Anuśāsana Parva*, examine forgiveness, compassion, the ethics of kingship, generosity, and *ahiṃsā*. These are rich materials for exploring Indian moral thought, but the epic grew over an extended period, and not every verse can be assigned securely before the Christian era. It should supplement, not replace, the firmer evidence from Ashoka and early Buddhist/Jain traditions.


**Reading:** [Scholarly overview](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mahabharata).
#### The *Arthaśāstra* — an ancient Indian theory of public administration

The statecraft treatise traditionally attributed to Kautilya, with a complex and debated compositional history, provides a particularly vivid example of welfare as a governmental responsibility. Book 2, chapter 1, section 26 describes royal support for orphans, the elderly, the infirm, and people without protectors, including women needing subsistence and their children. This occurs alongside an unapologetically hardheaded account of state power, punishment, and espionage. Compassionate provision and coercive institutions coexisted within the same intellectual work. Its final redaction may postdate the rise of Christianity; it should not be labeled a securely third-century-BCE book merely because of its traditional attribution.

**Primary:** [*Arthaśāstra* 2.1.25–27, Sanskrit and translation](https://indike.org/pramana/in.niti.artha.kautilya.2).

#### The Tamil *Tirukkuṟaḷ* — important but not securely pre-Christian

The *Tirukkuṟaḷ* is a major Tamil work of moral instruction concerning virtue, hospitality, charity, and the treatment of others. Its composition is not securely pre-Christian: proposals vary widely and Kamil Zvelebil argued for a date around the fifth century CE. Therefore it is retained as a valuable later South Asian comparison, not an early chronological witness or a demonstration that its author had no exposure to Christianity. Scholarly orientation: [*Tacit Tirukkuṟaḷ: Religion, Ethics, and Poetics in a Tamil Literary Tradition*](https://www.academia.edu/49091710/Tacit_Tirukku%E1%B9%9Fa%E1%B8%B7_Religion_Ethics_and_Poetics_in_a_Tamil_Literary_Tradition), discussion of dating and transmission.

### China: reciprocity, humaneness, and inclusive concern

*The relevant classical philosophical corpora predate Christianity, but authorship and textual accretion vary.*

#### Confucius and *ren* — 551–479 BCE traditionally

In the *Analects*, Confucius repeatedly commends *ren* (humaneness), proper reciprocity, and the cultivation of conduct that does not impose unwanted burdens on others. *Analects* 15.24 gives the well-known reciprocal principle: what one would not desire for oneself should not be imposed on others. Other passages treat humane character as something developed in family, friendships, and governance.

**Limit: Confucian ethics often begins with graded obligations**, not identical affection toward every person. The text is transmitted through later compilation, so the sentence should be cited to the *Analects*, not represented as an independently recorded speech.

Primary/analysis: [Chinese Text Project, *Analects*](https://ctext.org/analects); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Chinese ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/).

#### Mozi and the Mohists — fifth/fourth centuries BCE

The Mohist chapters on *jian ai* (inclusive care) argue that injury between families and states grows from partiality and neglect of outsiders. Concern should be extended to people beyond one's own faction and translated into opposition to aggressive predation. **Text:** [*Mozi*, Inclusive Care](https://ctext.org/mozi/book-4); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mohism/). **Analysis:** [Case 4](#case-4-china-care-beyond-ones-own-group).

#### Mencius — c. 372–289 BCE

In *Mencius* 2A:6, the image of a child about to fall into a well illustrates an immediate reaction of alarm and compassion—not motivated by rewards or social approval. For Mencius, moral sympathy is a rudimentary human capacity that education and government must cultivate. In 1A:7, he describes a ruler moved by the suffering of an ox and argues that such a response can be expanded into concern for subjects.

This is an elaborate account of the moral psychology of compassion. It is centuries older than Christianity and makes social government, not only private sentiment, an object of moral judgment.

Primary/scholarship: [Columbia University, *Mencius* 2A:6 source extract](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/ps/china/mencius_human_nature.pdf); [Stanford Encyclopedia, Chinese ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/).

#### The *Dao De Jing*, chapter 49 — pre-Christian text tradition; composite authorship

Chapter 49 treats the sage as good even to people who are not good; the text's dating and compilation are debated, though the tradition is pre-Christian. **Text:** [*Dao De Jing*](https://ctext.org/dao-de-jing). **Analysis:** [Case 4](#case-4-china-care-beyond-ones-own-group).

#### The *Dao De Jing*, chapters 63 and 67 — answering hostility and valuing compassion

Chapter 63 advocates meeting resentment or hostility with *de* (virtuous conduct, sometimes translated 'kindness'). Chapter 67 praises compassion (*ci*), frugality, and a refusal to place oneself arrogantly ahead of others. These are parts of a philosophy of nonaggressive action and humility rather than a modern universal-rights doctrine.

**Primary:** [*Dao De Jing*, complete bilingual text](https://ctext.org/dao-de-jing/ens).

#### Xunzi — third century BCE

Xunzi rejected Mencius's optimistic explanation of moral beginnings, arguing instead that ethical character must be formed through training, norms, and conscious effort. Yet he emphatically treated social order, ritual restraint, and just governance as achievements of deliberate human moral cultivation. His importance here is that pre-Christian Chinese ethics was already conducting serious internal debates about the origins of human goodness.

**Research:** [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Chinese ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/).

#### Zhuangzi — fourth–third centuries BCE core, later compilation

The *Zhuangzi* challenges narrow moral conceit, conventional status, and inflexible judgments about others. Its stories often unsettle arrogant claims to possess the only correct viewpoint. It is not simply a manual of charity or universal benevolence and must not be used as though it proposed an identical ethic to Mohism. It does, however, enlarge the conceptual field of humility and sympathetic understanding.

**Text:** [Chinese Text Project, *Zhuangzi*](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi).

#### The *Lüshi Chunqiu* — compiled in 239 BCE

This encyclopedic collection assembles competing accounts of ethical government, the importance of life, and standards of sound public administration. It is significant evidence that a Chinese court before Christianity considered moral government, human welfare, and restraint as topics for systematic intellectual synthesis. Because it is a compilation, individual chapters may reflect distinct schools rather than one author's unified ethical doctrine.

**Primary:** [Chinese Text Project, *Lüshi Chunqiu*](https://ctext.org/lv-shi-chun-qiu).

#### The *Huainanzi* — presented in 139 BCE

The *Huainanzi* brings Daoist, cosmological, and political reflection together in a pre-Christian Han court setting. Its discussions of rulers and the natural world connect good government with an ability to respond to human needs rather than governing solely through punitive force. It is an additional example of the philosophical treatment of benevolent government outside the Mediterranean.

**Primary:** [Chinese Text Project, *Huainanzi*](https://ctext.org/huainanzi).

#### Han political practice and the limits of benevolence — second century BCE

The Han state developed public arguments about lenient punishment, agriculture, famine response, and duties of rulers to commoners. Emperor Wen's penal reforms in 167 BCE, for instance, aimed to replace certain mutilating penalties. These reforms had complicated consequences, including harsh replacement punishments; they are not proof of modern human rights. They show that penal proportionality and humane rulership were already contested political goals.

Regional conclusion: The Chinese evidence alone refutes the idea that reciprocity, universalistic moral concern, or government inspired by compassion had to await Christianity. Mozi, Mencius, and the *Dao De Jing* disagree with one another in instructive ways—and that disagreement is itself evidence of a developed pre-Christian ethical conversation.

### Archaic and classical Greece

*From epic hospitality through drama, professional ethics, and the Socratic argument against retaliation.*

#### Homer and hospitality in the Odyssey

*Archaic Greek epic, commonly dated around the eighth–seventh centuries BCE*

In *Odyssey* VI, the vulnerable shipwrecked traveler Odysseus is met by Nausicaa, who provides clothing, food, and a route to safe reception. The Phaeacians' response portrays hospitality (*xenia*) as an ethical and religious duty toward a stranger, not merely an optional favor to friends. Book IX sets this in relief through the Cyclops, whose violence against guests marks him as a transgressor of the norm. The poem does not propose unconditional access or equal status for all outsiders; hospitality is embedded in reciprocal, aristocratic expectations. But it is an early and prominent literary picture of an obligation to people away from home. **Primary:** [*Odyssey* Book VI](https://en.mouseia.org/texts/odyssey/book-6), [Book IX](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Hom.+Od.+9). **Parallel:** close for care of a vulnerable guest, not for forgiving an enemy.

#### Hesiod — c. 700 BCE

In *Works and Days* 213–285, Hesiod rebukes unjust judgment, bribery, exploitation, and violence. At 275–280 he contrasts predatory animals, which destroy one another, with humans, to whom justice (*dikē*) has been given. The moral distinction between social justice and violent domination is already explicit in archaic Greek poetry.

**Primary:** [Harvard Center for Hellenic Studies, *Works and Days*](https://chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/hesiod-works-and-days-sb/).

#### Solon of Athens — early sixth century BCE

Solon's reforms addressed debt bondage and civil conflict among Athenian citizens. In surviving poetic fragments he presents public disorder as the consequence of greed and injustice, not merely military weakness. The reforms were selective—Athenian slavery and exclusion survived—but political justice and protection against abuse by creditors were civic projects long before Christianity.

**Locators:** Solon, political elegies, especially fragment 4 (often called *Eunomia*); Aristotle, *Constitution of the Athenians* 6–12 (later witness to earlier reforms).


**Reading:** [Historical overview](https://www.worldhistory.org/Solon/).
#### Aeschylus — 525–456 BCE

*The Suppliants* dramatizes a community's decision whether to protect vulnerable foreign women seeking refuge, despite the political danger of defying those pursuing them. *The Eumenides* (458 BCE) stages the replacement of escalating personal revenge with a civic court and a public process of judgment. These dramas do not teach a modern asylum statute or comprehensive pacifism; they do establish that protection of the vulnerable and restraint of vendetta were live Greek moral problems.

**Locators:** Aeschylus, *Suppliants*; *Eumenides*, especially the establishment of the Athenian court.


**Reading:** [Primary text, *Eumenides*](https://classics.mit.edu/Aeschylus/eumendides.html).
#### Sophocles — c. 496–406 BCE

In *Antigone*, the obligation to bury an enemy of the state becomes an occasion for conflict between royal decree and a higher moral order. Antigone's family loyalty is not universal compassion in disguise. But the play centrally asks whether political power can override all duties owed to the vulnerable or condemned. It thereby shows that moral obligation beyond positive law was dramatically articulated in fifth-century Greece.

**Primary:** Sophocles, *Antigone*, especially Antigone's defense of the unwritten laws (around lines 450–460).


**Reading:** [Primary text, *Antigone*](https://classics.mit.edu/Sophocles/antigone.html).
#### Euripides — c. 480–406 BCE

*The Trojan Women* (415 BCE) depicts war from the perspective of women and children left exposed to slavery and loss after a city's defeat. *The Suppliant Women* treats the respectful burial of enemies as an obligation worth discussing and defending. The plays do not eliminate the moral contradictions of Greek warfare, but they ask audiences to recognize suffering even among the defeated, not merely among fellow citizens.

**Locators:** Euripides, *Trojan Women* and *Suppliant Women*.


**Reading:** [Primary text, *Trojan Women*](https://classics.mit.edu/Euripides/troj_women.html).
#### The Hippocratic medical tradition — Greek classical and Hellenistic antiquity

Greek medical writings develop an expectation that practitioners should act for patients' benefit and avoid foreseeable harm. The famous Hippocratic Oath promises boundaries on a physician's conduct, although its precise date, authorship, and early general acceptance are uncertain. 'First, do no harm' is not a verbatim sentence from the Oath and should not be misrepresented as one. What can be said is that professional duties toward vulnerable patients had a non-Christian ancient history.

Scholarly review: [Historical review of the Hippocratic Oath and its uncertain origins](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8368262/).

#### The Pythagorean movement — sixth century BCE onward

Ancient reports associate Pythagorean thought with kinship among living beings, restraint from animal killing, and purificatory practices. The texts preserve different and sometimes conflicting portrayals, much of them written centuries later. Pythagorean traditions are important antecedents of Greek debates about nonviolence toward animals; specific sayings should not be confidently put in Pythagoras's mouth without an early source.


**Reading:** [Historical scholarship on early Pythagoreanism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/).
#### Empedocles — fifth century BCE

In surviving fragments commonly placed in his *Purifications*, Empedocles depicts animal slaughter as morally alarming, partly because of his conception of transmigration and the kinship of living beings. Fragment DK B137 imagines sacrificial killing in terms of unknowing injury to a transformed family member. Its expansive concern contrasts sharply with a narrow ethic confined to fellow citizens.

Primary/scholarship: [Empedocles fragments, including DK B136–137](https://adfonteslibrary.org/work-presocratics-empedocles-fragments); [Cambridge discussion of the sacrificial fragments](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/reconstructing-empedocles-thought/proem-to-on-nature/A4910504B9C77DBBA6804B679BC3EC81).

#### Protagoras and the civic virtues — fifth century BCE

In Plato's *Protagoras* 320c–328d, the sophist presents a myth in which human society depends on a shared capacity for justice and respect. We cannot know how closely Plato's dialogue reproduces the historical Protagoras; we can certainly say that fourth-century Greek philosophy depicts ethics as a condition of human cooperation rather than divine revelation available to one community.


**Reading:** [Primary witness, Plato's *Protagoras*](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/protagoras.html).
#### Democritus — fifth/fourth centuries BCE

The surviving ethical fragments attributed to Democritus explore moderation, generosity, public responsibility, good conscience, and the importance of acting rightly whether or not others observe us. Questions of attribution and later collection must be acknowledged. The contribution is the development of a reflective morality of character and community within a non-Christian intellectual environment.


**Reading:** [Scholarly overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/democritus/).
#### Socrates in Plato's *Apology* — event 399 BCE; dialogue fourth century BCE

At *Apology* 41d–e, Socrates says he is not angry with the people who accused and condemned him, despite believing that they intended to injure him. This is not an indiscriminate claim that their conduct was acceptable; he judges it wrong. He separates moral condemnation of an action from hatred of the people responsible. That distinction has obvious relevance to later arguments about forgiveness and persecutors.

**Primary:** [Harvard Center for Hellenic Studies, Plato's *Apology*, 41d–e](https://chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/plato-the-apology-of-socrates-sb/).

#### Socrates in Plato's *Crito* — fourth century BCE

At *Crito* 49b–d, Plato's Socrates denies that being wronged grants anyone license to do wrong in return. This argument stands apart from whether he believed every act of force was wrong. **Texts:** [*Crito*](https://adfonteslibrary.org/work-plato-crito), [*Republic*](https://adfonteslibrary.org/work-plato-republic). **Analysis:** [Case 5](#case-5-plato-wrongdoing-does-not-license-revenge).

#### Socrates in Plato's *Gorgias* — fourth century BCE

At *Gorgias* 469b and 474b–475e, Socrates argues that doing injustice is worse than suffering it, because an injustice corrupts the character of its perpetrator. This is a deep reversal of a morality that judges everything by whether one gains power or avoids injury. Moral integrity, on this account, matters even when the just person is vulnerable.

**Primary:** [Plato, *Gorgias*](https://adfonteslibrary.org/work-plato-gorgias).

#### Plato's *Republic* — fourth century BCE

At *Republic* I.335b–e, Socrates rejects the slogan "help friends, harm enemies," asking how justice could consist in worsening another person. **Text:** [*Republic*, Book I](https://adfonteslibrary.org/work-plato-republic). **Analysis:** [Case 5](#case-5-plato-wrongdoing-does-not-license-revenge).

#### Xenophon — c. 430–354 BCE

Xenophon's *Cyropaedia* imagines political leadership built through discipline, just administration, generosity, and obligations between rulers and followers. It is an idealizing literary portrait of Cyrus, not a transparent historical biography of the Persian king. Its importance is the civic claim that rulers can earn loyalty through benevolence and fair dealing rather than terror alone. In the *Memorabilia*, Xenophon also portrays Socrates discussing practical virtue, friendship, and help to others.

**Primary:** [Xenophon, *Cyropaedia*](https://adfonteslibrary.org/work-xenophon-cyropaedia).

#### Isocrates — 436–338 BCE

In the *To Nicocles* and *Nicocles*, Isocrates discusses what citizens and rulers owe one another, emphasizing fairness, moderation, self-control, and the public benefits of just leadership. His rhetoric is aristocratic, and some advice primarily supports stable power. It nevertheless establishes that moral expectations of government were systematically taught as political education before Christianity.


**Reading:** [Primary source collection](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/).
#### Aristotle — 384–322 BCE

The *Nicomachean Ethics* VIII–IX develops friendship, reciprocal goodwill, mutual assistance, and generosity as essential to flourishing. Book V, especially 5.10, explains *equity* (*epieikeia*): a general law may be too rigid for an exceptional case, and the equitable person may forgo a maximal legal claim in favor of a fairer resolution. This is a major non-Christian theory of humane legal judgment, moral flexibility, and human relationships.

Aristotle defended an unequal social hierarchy and the doctrine of natural slavery; his ethics is neither modern universal equality nor unconditional love of enemies. The precise claim is that valuable theories of generosity, justice, friendship, and equity already existed.

**Primary:** [Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book V](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.5.v.html).

#### Thucydides and the moral contradictions of Athens — late fifth century BCE

Thucydides' *History of the Peloponnesian War* depicts both civic generosity and the brutal logic of domination. The funeral speech attributed to Pericles in Book II celebrates a city that helps others, while the Melian Dialogue in Book V portrays power stripped of moral restraint. These passages are historical-literary representations, not unanimous Greek commitments. Their significance is that the relation of power to justice was being argued over in the ancient city long before Christianity.

Greek conclusion: The case for pre-Christian nonretaliation is not dependent on vague parallels. *Crito* and *Republic* make it explicit, the tragedies dramatize it and its limits, and Aristotle develops legal equity and generosity. This is a developed moral conversation, not a moral vacuum.

It should not be translated mechanically as the modern philanthropic sector or treated as proof of a single egalitarian doctrine. Nevertheless, the existence and prestige of the term make it impossible to equate Greek ethics solely with competitiveness or vengeance. ### Hellenistic philosophy and cosmopolitanism

*Third to first centuries BCE; many teachings are attested through later witnesses, so the dating of any exact saying matters.*

#### Diogenes of Sinope and Cynic world citizenship — fourth century BCE

Later sources remember Diogenes calling himself a *kosmopolitēs*, a citizen of the world. The positive meaning of this slogan is debated; it might primarily reject local conventions rather than demand equal service to all humanity. Nevertheless, it is an early, non-Christian challenge to the idea that civic identity exhausts one's obligations and moral horizon.

Scholarly source: [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 'Cosmopolitanism'](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cosmopolitanism/), on Diogenes and the Cynics.

#### Zeno of Citium — c. 334–262 BCE

The founder of Stoicism contributed to the idea that human beings belong within a larger rational order. His *Republic*, preserved only in later testimony, imagined a community not ultimately partitioned by conventional civic boundaries. Later Stoics would develop the stronger argument that shared rational nature grounds mutual moral responsibility. Because Zeno's own work is lost, avoid falsely quoting later Stoic universalism as his verbatim teaching.


**Reading:** [Historical overview of Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/).
#### Cleanthes — c. 330–230 BCE

Cleanthes' *Hymn to Zeus* represents rational cosmic order as common to humanity and links wisdom with living in accord with it. The text is a reminder that religiously framed universal moral law was not a Christian invention. The Stoic theology is emphatically different from Christian doctrine.


**Reading:** [Stoic textual and historical context](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/).
#### Chrysippus — c. 279–206 BCE

Chrysippus systematized Stoic ethics, including the relationship of reason, justice, nature, and social responsibility. The survival of his teachings is indirect, through reports and quotations preserved by later authors. The major contribution of this school is a framework in which morality is not merely loyalty to one's group but something justified by shared rational nature.


**Reading:** [Historical overview of Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/).
#### Stoic *oikeiōsis* — third century BCE onward

This technical idea concerns the way living beings relate to what is their own and, in later Stoic moral development, come to recognize the claims of other human beings. The theory helps explain why family affection and self-preservation can be extended into wider duties. There are scholarly disagreements about the precise reconstruction of early Stoic accounts, and it would be misleading to present *oikeiōsis* as simple emotional pity or identical concern for everyone.

**Scholarship:** [Stoic cosmopolitanism and its earlier roots, Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cosmopolitanism/).

#### Epicurus — 341–270 BCE

Epicurus treats friendship as a major good in *Principal Doctrines* 27–28. In doctrines 31–38, justice is explained through mutual agreements neither to harm nor be harmed. His moral argument is grounded in the human condition and the search for a peaceful life, not a divine law of Christian revelation. The contractarian approach has limits: it is not automatically a theory of obligations toward beings incapable of reciprocity. Nonetheless it demonstrates a sophisticated non-Christian account of kindness, trust, security, and social cooperation.

**Primary:** [*Principal Doctrines*, English and ancient text](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/princdoc.html).

#### Theophrastus — c. 371–287 BCE

Reports of Theophrastus' moral objections to animal sacrifice, especially as preserved much later by Porphyry, expand philosophical concern beyond humanity. It is a suggestive Greek parallel to Indian arguments over noninjury, but the evidence is mediated by later authors. It should not be cited as a securely surviving standalone Theophrastean treatise on animal rights.


**Reading:** [Scholarly overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/theophrastus/).
#### Menander and the Greek New Comedy — c. 342–291 BCE

Menander's plays explored human fallibility, recognition, family reconciliation, forgiveness, and the need to judge others by more than first appearances. Dramatic comedy does not teach a unified, systematic moral philosophy, and individual famous maxims often survive in later anthologies of doubtful attribution. Still, the genre demonstrates the long Greek literary interest in clemency, sympathetic understanding, and social repair.


**Reading:** [Primary-source research catalogue](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/).
#### Panaetius of Rhodes — c. 185–109 BCE

Panaetius connected Stoic moral theory to the practical duties of a citizen, friend, and officeholder. His lost *On Duty* became a major source for the first two books of Cicero's *De Officiis*. Panaetius thus gives us a traceable intellectual bridge from pre-Christian Greek philosophy into Roman civic ethics.

**Source:** Cicero, *De Officiis*, Books I–II; [Stoic cosmopolitanism overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cosmopolitanism/).

#### Posidonius — c. 135–51 BCE

A later Stoic thinker whose work on human nature, social life, and emotion influenced Roman intellectual culture. The fragmentary record makes it unwise to assign him catchy modern-sounding quotations about unconditional love. His historical importance is the continuity and development of pre-Christian Stoicism in Rome's intellectual orbit.

Hellenistic conclusion: Universal moral community, the restraint of wrongdoing, the extension of concern beyond a single city, and the social value of friendship were major topics in non-Christian schools long before the first century CE.

### Rome before Christianity: public virtue and Republican moral theory

Roman history adds a different test: were these concepts merely private speculations, or were they also used to evaluate officeholders, laws, and ordinary relationships? The surviving evidence documents both. Its most important vocabulary is **fides** (good faith), **pietas** (dutiful conduct), **iustitia** (justice), **humanitas** (humane cultivation), **clementia** (restraint by the powerful), **liberalitas** (generosity), and **aequitas** (fairness). These words often operated within status hierarchies; the distinction between a publicly admired virtue and a universally protected entitlement is crucial.

#### Terence — c. 195/185–159 BCE

In the comedy *Heauton Timorumenos* (*The Self-Tormentor*), first performed in the second century BCE, one character justifies his concern for a neighbor with the famous line *Homo sum: humani nil a me alienum puto*—a recognition that another person's human affairs are not wholly alien to him. The dramatic context matters: this line is spoken by a particular character and is not necessarily a philosophical manifesto of universal altruism. Its enduring fame nevertheless reveals a compelling ancient Roman articulation of human fellowship and concern beyond oneself.

**Primary:** [Terence, *Heauton Timorumenos*, line 77, Perseus](https://beta.perseus.tufts.edu/urn%3Acts%3AlatinLit%3Aphi0134.phi002.perseus-eng2/line%3A1.1/); [Cambridge Classical Studies on the line's interpretive history](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antichthon/article/abs/homo-sum-humani-nil-a-me-alienum-puto-terence-heauton-timorumenos-77/F56A8982DF9B893FA03083809A82EA37).

#### Scipionic Roman intellectual life — second century BCE

Leading Romans engaged Greek philosophers and theorized the relationship between public office and moral virtue. Panaetius's connection with the circle of Scipio Aemilianus illustrates how Stoic concern with duties became part of Roman elite political thought before Christianity. One should avoid treating every later idealizing description of the 'Scipionic Circle' as an eyewitness document.


**Reading:** [Primary contextual witness, Cicero's *De Officiis*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Officiis/1A%2A.html).
#### Cicero, *De Officiis* — 44 BCE

Cicero's *De Officiis* (44 BCE) is a coherent account of justice, good faith, fair punishment, generosity, shared human fellowship, and duties even toward people who wrong us. His Book I treats aid to strangers and the danger of helping one person unjustly at another's expense. **Text:** [*De Officiis*, Book I](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Officiis/1A%2A.html). **Analysis:** [Case 7](#case-7-cicero-duties-to-enemies-and-strangers).

#### Cicero, *De Legibus* and *De Re Publica* — mid-first century BCE

These works connect genuine law with reason and moral obligation rather than naked command. *De Legibus* in particular develops the claim that human beings participate in a rational nature from which standards of justice can be discerned. The argument precedes Christian natural-law theology; later thinkers would develop, contest, and transform its implications.


**Reading:** [Cicero corpus and scholarship](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cicero/).
#### Cicero, *Pro Marcello* — 46 BCE

In his speech celebrating the pardon of Marcus Marcellus, Cicero praises Julius Caesar's *clementia* after civil war. The politics are obvious: a ruler's pardon is also a demonstration of superiority and a means of consolidating legitimacy. But the speech proves that mercy toward a defeated political enemy was an intelligible and publicly lauded Roman virtue before Christianity.


**Reading:** [Cicero corpus and scholarship](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cicero/).
#### Lucretius — c. 99–55 BCE

In *De Rerum Natura* Book V, Lucretius describes the emergence of families, mutual concern, cooperation, laws, and agreements as human beings develop social life. His Epicurean materialism explains ethical and civic cooperation through the human predicament rather than a distinctive divine revelation. His historical reconstruction is speculative, but the *existence of such a naturalistic explanation* is secure and pre-Christian.

**Locator:** Lucretius, *De Rerum Natura* V, especially the passages on the development of family ties and social agreements.


**Reading:** [Epicurean philosophical context](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lucretius/).
#### Julius Caesar's cultivated clemency — first century BCE

Caesar's willingness to pardon certain defeated opponents became a recognized aspect of his public image. These pardons did not erase his campaigns, executions, or political ruthlessness. The lesson is different: Roman statesmen already understood restraint when empowered to punish as a publicly legible mark of greatness. Whether Caesar displayed that virtue consistently is a separate question.

Primary/context: Cicero, *Pro Marcello*; Caesar's surviving political narratives and contemporary responses.


**Reading:** [Cicero on Caesar's clemency](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cicero/).
#### Sallust — 86–35 BCE

In *The Conspiracy of Catiline* 51, Sallust presents a speech by Caesar arguing that judges and senators must not let anger, hatred, or favoritism govern punishment. Caesar calls attention to the dangers of public officials treating vengeance as justice and discusses alternatives to summary execution. Ancient historians commonly reconstructed speeches; we should not pretend to possess a verbatim Senate transcript. It is still securely a late Republican literary articulation of rational, restrained justice.

**Primary:** [Sallust, *Catiline* 51](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/sallust/bellum_catilinae%2A.html).

#### Virgil — 70–19 BCE

In *Aeneid* VI.851–853, Anchises announces a political ideal that includes imposing peace and sparing those who have submitted. The ideal has a dark counterpart: Roman peace often followed conquest, and the epic ends in a killing that complicates the celebration of restraint. This complexity is precisely why the work matters. Pre-Christian Roman literature explicitly evaluated its empire in terms of clemency as well as victory.

**Locator:** Virgil, *Aeneid* VI.851–853 and XII (the final confrontation).


**Reading:** [Primary locator, *Aeneid* VI](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Verg.+A.+6).
#### Horace — 65–8 BCE

In *Satires* 1.3, Horace criticizes the practice of judging friends by their least attractive faults while excusing one's own failings. He commends tolerance, moderation, and a sense of proportion in judgment. This is not unconditional enemy-love; it is an established ethic of forbearance toward ordinary human imperfection.


**Reading:** [Primary locator, *Satires* I.3](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Hor.+S.+1.3).
#### Livy — 59 BCE–17 CE

In *History of Rome* V.27, Livy recounts the story of Camillus refusing to use abducted schoolchildren as leverage during a war against Falerii. Rather than exploit the children, Camillus rejects the schoolmaster's betrayal. The narrative praises just conduct even in war, and the story culminates in the notion that the Romans won through ***iustitia* and *fides*** rather than treachery.

**Dating warning: The narrated events are set in the early fourth century BCE, but Livy's written account comes from the late first century BCE / early first century CE. It is unquestionably evidence of the ideals Livy and his Augustan audience could recognize**, not secure documentation that Camillus uttered every reported word four centuries earlier.

**Primary:** [Livy, *History of Rome*, Book V.27](https://www.istrianet.org/istria/non-istrians/livy/works/ab_urbe_condita/1book05.htm); [modern analysis of *fides* in this episode](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004684539_008).

#### Augustus — 63 BCE–14 CE

In *Res Gestae* 34 Augustus records the shield awarded in 27 BCE for *virtus, clementia, iustitia,* and *pietas*; in sections 15–18 he recounts distributions and other material provisions. A marble replica from Arles corroborates the public honor. The work is self-promotional rather than a neutral record of his virtue. **Sources:** [*Res Gestae*](https://droitromain.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/Anglica/resgest_engl.htm); [Arles shield study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/efr_0000-0000_1980_ant_43_1_1294). **Analysis:** [Case 8](#case-8-augustus-virtues-made-visible-in-political-life).

#### Ovid, Roman social feeling and the politics of exile — 43 BCE–17/18 CE

Ovid's exile poetry (*Tristia* and *Epistulae ex Ponto*) explores dependence on friends, appeals for mercy, loyalty, and the suffering caused by exclusion from one's community. These are compelling witnesses to the *social vocabulary* of human obligation and clemency in Augustan Rome, though they should not be treated as systematic arguments for universal compassion.

### Roman laws, customs, and public provision

The different kinds of evidence must be kept separate. This matrix shows what each **can** and **cannot** establish. It belongs before the examples because it is the safeguard against converting a grain statute or a ruler's monument into a universal doctrine of love.

#### The Roman case in a single framework

| Evidence | It establishes | It does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Cicero, Seneca, Roman civic virtue vocabulary | Explicit ethical reflection on justice, generosity, clemency, and human obligations | That all Roman citizens practiced these consistently |
| *Provocatio* and anti-extortion legislation | Certain legal limits and avenues of redress before Christian rule | Equal legal rights irrespective of citizenship, gender, or status |
| Grain legislation and public distributions | State-backed efforts to provision selected populations | Universal poverty relief or exclusively altruistic motives |
| *Hospitium*, patronage, and benefaction | Social expectations of hospitality and material assistance | Love of all strangers or absence of power imbalances |
| Manumission and Seneca's advice on enslaved people | Recognition of certain shared human capacities despite status boundaries | A pre-Christian abolition movement in Rome |
| Augustus's shield and official narrative | Clemency, justice, and duty as celebrated ideals of political legitimacy | Personal virtue proved by a ruler's own claims |

**Conclusion:** The case for pre-Christian Roman moral thought and moral institutions is extremely strong. The case that Rome had fully realized a compassionate and egalitarian society is not, and need not be made. In fact, stating the difference makes the argument against *moral exclusivity* much more robust.

#### *Mos maiorum*: inherited norms of honorable conduct

The *mos maiorum*, the customs of the ancestors, was not a single law code. It was the Roman understanding that public and private conduct should be measured against inherited obligations, institutions, and examples of worthy behavior. The late Republic's political competition often involved rival claims to embody these traditions. It is a mistake either to romanticize this morality as universally humane or to dismiss it as empty propaganda. The language of virtue and the expectation that leaders should defend their conduct morally were part of Roman political culture before the Christian period.

Key, overlapping concepts included:

- ***Fides*:** trustworthiness, good faith, keeping commitments, and reliability in public and private relations. In Cicero's *De Officiis* I.23, keeping faith is fundamental to justice.
- ***Pietas*:** dutiful conduct toward family, divine powers, and community. It could inspire care and loyalty, but also demanded sacrifices that modern readers might regard as oppressive.
- ***Iustitia*:** justice, including restraint from unjustly harming others and respect for obligations.
- ***Humanitas*:** a varied ideal embracing civilized conduct, cultivation, humane sympathy, and sometimes generosity. Its meaning changed with context; it was not a comprehensive doctrine of equal human rights.
- ***Clementia*:** clemency or merciful restraint by someone with power to punish or destroy. It presupposed unequal power and could reinforce the superiority of the person granting mercy.
- ***Liberalitas*, *beneficentia*, and *benignitas*:** generosity, benefaction, and kindness, often linked to social standing and reciprocal obligation.
- ***Aequitas*:** fairness or equity, particularly significant when rigid rights or procedures could yield morally troubling results.

The *lexicon* itself is evidence of a pre-Christian moral discourse. Cicero, Sallust, Virgil, Livy, Augustus, and later Seneca and Valerius Maximus provide dated witnesses to its continuing development.

Primary anchors: [Cicero, *De Officiis*, Book I](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Officiis/1A%2A.html); [Augustus, *Res Gestae* 34](https://droitromain.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/Anglica/resgest_engl.htm); [Valerius Maximus, Book V.1](https://www.attalus.org/translate/valerius5a.html).

#### *Hospitium*: reciprocal duties of hospitality

Roman *hospitium* organized duties of welcome, protection, and reciprocal assistance, sometimes between families over generations and sometimes between communities. These arrangements presupposed durable bonds and social selectivity: they were not guarantees of hospitality to every outsider. The earlier but distinct Greek ideal of *xenia* is treated in the [Homer entry](#homer-and-hospitality-in-the-odyssey). The relevant Roman point is that guests and hosts already understood hospitality as a socially legible obligation. **Source locator:** Roman *hospitium* in Roman social and legal history; [Greek comparison in the *Odyssey* VI](https://en.mouseia.org/texts/odyssey/book-6).

#### Patronage and *clientela*: obligations between the powerful and the dependent

Roman patron-client relationships entailed reciprocal expectations. Patrons might assist clients with legal advocacy, food, money, political protection, access, or other services; clients could be expected to offer attendance, support, labor, and political loyalty. The arrangement supports a limited proposition: Roman society already regarded help, loyalty, and material assistance as socially enforceable or reputationally important duties. It does **not** establish egalitarian charity. Patronage often reproduced economic dependence, reinforced status differences, and benefited the patron politically.

This is especially important when discussing the *Augustus* novel. Rome's inhabitants did not normally approach morality as isolated individuals calculating private virtue. Obligation was built into households, kinship ties, civic status, client networks, friendships, and public office. The conflicts among those obligations were a recurring subject of Roman literature.


**Reading:** [Historical context for public benefaction and inequality](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/philanthropy/).
#### Appeal against magistrates: *provocatio ad populum*

Republican Roman law recognized forms of *provocatio*, an appeal by Roman citizens against certain severe coercive actions by magistrates. Ancient tradition associated early forms with the Republic's foundation; the historical details of those early accounts are disputed, but a *Lex Valeria* of 300 BCE is an important securely Republican marker. The protection illustrates the principle that a magistrate's coercive power could be subject to publicly established restrictions.

**Limit:** This was **not** universal due process or protection for everyone in Roman territory. Coverage, procedure, geography, and the rights of noncitizens and enslaved people differed dramatically. It is evidence of a Roman legal commitment to certain checks on official power, not of modern equality before the law.

**Research lead:** [Oxford Classical Dictionary, *provocatio* and Roman criminal-law scholarship](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550492871).

#### The *Lex Aquilia*: compensation for wrongful harm

The *Lex Aquilia*, generally placed in the third century BCE, helped define legal responsibility and monetary remedies for wrongful damage to property. It became foundational for Roman private law and later European legal thought. Its historical significance is the recognition that wrongdoing can create a duty of compensation rather than unrestricted revenge. But it protected economic interests and property ownership, including ownership of enslaved people; it should not be offered as proof of a general Roman right against personal cruelty.

**Research lead:** [Oxford scholarship on the *Lex Aquilia*](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550530088).

#### The *Lex Calpurnia de repetundis*, 149 BCE: action against official extortion

In 149 BCE the Roman tribune Lucius Calpurnius Piso introduced legislation establishing a standing jury procedure for dealing with certain forms of extortion by Roman officials. Later developments changed the courts' membership and procedures, and the system was repeatedly used in political struggles. Nevertheless, the legislation matters: wrongdoing by powerful officeholders was recognized as something requiring institutional redress.

The law did not end provincial exploitation, and trials could be biased or politically selective. Its historical function should be presented narrowly: Rome had legal mechanisms—however imperfect—to challenge particular official abuses before the emergence of Christian governance.

**Scholarship:** J. S. Richardson, [“The Purpose of the *Lex Calpurnia de repetundis*,” *Journal of Roman Studies*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/428BDC6C3D59EBF37088EA26A9321C41/S0075435800008479a.pdf/the-purpose-of-the-lex-calpurnia-de-repetundis.pdf).

#### The *Lex Sempronia frumentaria*, 123 BCE: subsidized grain

Gaius Gracchus's grain legislation provided for the sale of grain to eligible Roman citizens at a regulated price. This is a concrete example of public policy addressing food security and urban material needs well before Christianity. It emerged amid intense political conflict and is not best understood simply as charity: food policy could serve social stability, electoral purposes, and claims about the responsibilities of government.

Nor was it a modern system of means-tested welfare. Eligibility depended on Roman civic categories, and later modifications to the grain supply system changed its design. The historically defensible claim is institutional concern with provisioning, **not** a fully developed universal humanitarian state.

**Reference:** [William Smith's *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities*, “Frumentariae Leges” (historical reference)](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA%2A/Frumentariae_Leges.html).

#### Public generosity and *euergetism*: doing good, acquiring honor

Greek and Roman cities relied on benefactions from rulers and wealthy residents: buildings, theaters, baths, festivals, grain, oil, public dining, and various civic amenities. Historians call this practice *euergetism*, from a Greek word for benefaction. It shows that giving resources for others' use was a recognized ideal of public distinction. It also reveals a limitation: prominent benefactions often helped the city as a collective or honored the donor more than they relieved the most desperate poverty.

Distinguish three different claims:

1. A moral norm of benefaction existed: firmly supported by ancient texts and honorific inscriptions.
2. Substantial goods and services were provided: supported for particular cities, dates, and donor projects.
3. These measures were primarily motivated by concern for the poorest or served everyone equally: often *not* supported. Prestige, patronage, religion, political competition, and social inequality all mattered.

This nuance permits us to recognize genuine pre-Christian civic provision without making the equally unsustainable claim that ancient pagan philanthropy was identical to later Christian almsgiving or modern social welfare.

**Research:** [*Oxford Classical Dictionary*, “euergetism”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548933366); [Cambridge University Press, research on pagan almsgiving and poverty](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/poverty-in-the-roman-world/you-do-him-no-service-an-exploration-of-pagan-almsgiving/FBC4F32DF0418F9C05FB729DEDB325B2).

#### Manumission and the limits of Roman humanity

Romans developed established routes for manumitting enslaved people; some legally recognized forms could confer citizenship upon the formerly enslaved. This possibility shows a legal framework allowing significant changes in social status. But it did not make slavery humane, universal, or morally justified. Roman elites could praise shared humanity while owning people. A serious historian must acknowledge both facts instead of using the more sympathetic passages to conceal the coercive institution.

In the first centuries CE the emperor and senate also placed legal restrictions on manumission. Thus individual freedom within a slave society should not be confused with an abolitionist ethic. Seneca's Letter 47 is powerful precisely because it operates within this contradiction.


**Reading:** [Legal-historical context, Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673).
#### Mutual-aid and funerary associations

Associations (*collegia*) sometimes pooled contributions to meet needs such as funeral expenses. Inscriptions, including the regulations of the burial association at Lanuvium from 136 CE, give direct evidence of group organization and financial rules. These organizations show assistance through collective provision, not simply personal generosity. Their members paid dues; they did not amount to universally available assistance for the destitute, and their legal settings were varied.

**Research:** [Study of the Lanuvium funerary association and its regulations](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jclst/50/0/50_KJ00005745539/_article/-char/en).

#### Imperial *alimenta*: targeted support for children

A scheme usually associated with the reigns of Nerva and Trajan, the *alimenta*, used financial arrangements connected to Italian landowners to support certain freeborn children in Italian communities. Scholars debate important details of the program's design, funding, and aims. Since this is **after the rise of Christianity, it cannot be cited as a pre-Christian statute; the relevant point is that a Roman imperial administration continued to devise welfare-oriented measures outside a Christian system of government**. We should not claim that it supported all children, that it relieved every poor household, or that its motives are beyond dispute.


**Reading:** [Ancient Roman public giving context](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/philanthropy/).
### Non-Christian thinkers in the early Roman Empire

*First and early second centuries CE. These authors represent established non-Christian literary and philosophical traditions. No established borrowing from Jesus is shown for the teachings discussed, but **lack of evidence of influence is not proof that a thinker had never heard of Christians**.*

#### Valerius Maximus — writing around 30 CE

His *Memorable Deeds and Sayings* (5.1) has a chapter expressly devoted to ***humanitas* and *clementia***—humanity and clemency. He assembles Roman examples of treatment of defeated enemies, prisoners, and fallen foreign rulers. One anecdote praises the Senate for freeing Carthaginian captives without collecting a ransom; another describes honorable burial granted to a defeated king. These are **moral exempla**, not independent proof of every episode they recount. That is exactly why they are useful for studying Roman values: the author expected an audience to admire such actions.

This text was written at about the time associated with Jesus' public ministry. There is no evidence that its Roman exempla of mercy were taken from Christian teaching. Their referents and literary ancestry reach back into Republican Roman civic memory.

**Primary:** [Valerius Maximus, *Memorable Deeds and Sayings* V.1, 'Of Humanity and Clemency'](https://www.attalus.org/translate/valerius5a.html).

#### Seneca, *De Ira* (*On Anger*) — mid-first century CE

Seneca identifies anger as a dangerous basis for punishment, relationships, and government. He proposes habits of reflection, delay, fairness, and correction in place of revenge. His philosophical psychology derives from Stoic debates and earlier Greek treatments of passion. His aim is often to correct wrongdoing intelligently rather than to excuse it; rational restraint is not moral indifference.


**Reading:** [Primary and secondary reference to Senecan ethical texts](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/).
#### Seneca, *De Clementia* (*On Clemency*) — c. mid-50s CE

Addressing the young emperor Nero, Seneca makes clemency a central test of a ruler's proper use of power. The ruler must avoid cruelty and excessive punishment, even when entitled to retaliate. This is a demanding *ethic of asymmetric power*: the more one can hurt others, the greater one's obligation to restrain oneself. It remains paternalistic, and the ruler does not surrender his authority.

Important conceptual distinction: Stoic authors often separate reasoned clemency from *misericordia* understood as an irrational or destabilizing pity. It is inaccurate to equate Stoic *clementia* with every modern use of 'empathy' or Christian 'mercy.' The documented demand for humane restraint remains real.


**Reading:** [Primary and secondary reference to Senecan ethical texts](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/).
#### Seneca, *De Beneficiis* (*On Benefits*) — first century CE

This substantial treatise examines what it means to give, how an obligation arises, whether generosity should expect repayment, and why a person's intention matters. Seneca warns against giving help as a disguised commercial investment and encourages doing good even in the presence of ingratitude. The moral quality of an act is not exhausted by a material transfer or anticipated return.

The work is an exceptionally rich non-Christian literary source for generosity, gratitude, human interdependence, and the pitfalls of patronage.

**Primary:** [Seneca, *On Benefits*, Book I](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_Benefits/Book_I).

#### Seneca, *Moral Letters* 47 — shared humanity in a slave society

Seneca tells his correspondent to treat enslaved people as human beings and associates, not as beings of another nature. In a society where owners had extraordinary power, he urges gentler treatment, conversation, and recognition that fortune could reverse any status. His humane argument does not amount to opposition to the institution of slavery itself. Its moral importance lies in insisting on shared personhood across a hierarchy that routinely denied it.

**Primary:** [Seneca, *Moral Letters* 47](https://www.seneca.ink/letters/47-on-master-and-slave).

#### Seneca, *Moral Letters* 95 — humanity as something sacred

In Letter 95.33, Seneca condemns the Roman appetite for human bloodshed and describes a human being as sacred in relation to another human being (*homo, sacra res homini*). This is an especially striking critique of a society entertained by the suffering of others. His condemnation of cruel spectacle is thoroughly understandable within Stoic commitments to shared rational humanity.

**Primary:** [Seneca, Letter 95, Latin text](https://falsafa.ai/works/seneca-lucius-annaeus-letters-to-lucilius-bc8c6e/95-letter-95/original/).

#### Musonius Rufus — c. 30–before 102 CE

Musonius argues in Discourses 3–4 that women, like men, possess rational capacities and should therefore study philosophy and acquire virtue. His lectures also concern marital fidelity, discipline, practical responsibility, and the moral education of households. His views are not contemporary feminism; his family expectations remain conventional in important respects. But his argument for women's ability to reason and cultivate virtue shows a first-century pagan defense of their ethical education.

**Primary:** [Discourses 3, 4 and 13A, translations from *Women's Life in Greece and Rome*](https://diotima-doctafemina.org/translations/anthologies/womens-life-in-greece-and-rome-selections/iii-philosophers-on-the-role-of-women/).

#### Quintilian — c. 35–100 CE

In *Institutio Oratoria* I.3.14–17, Quintilian argues against beating children as a routine educational practice. He emphasizes humiliation, fear, damage to learning, and the opportunities abuse creates for those with power over children. He prefers skillful instruction and discipline without arbitrary violence. This is an explicit non-Christian concern for the vulnerability of children in an area where Roman authority could be harsh.

**Primary:** [Quintilian, *Institutio Oratoria* I.3.14–17](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/1A%2A.html).

#### Plutarch — c. 46–after 119 CE

In *On Controlling Anger*, especially chapter 11, Plutarch discusses how a person with power over household servants can allow rage to distort judgment. He commends listening to a dependent person's defense, delaying punishment until calmer, and using forgiveness when it can lead to reform. He even notes that harsh beatings can teach people to conceal wrongdoing rather than become better.

Plutarch's argument remains within a slaveholding world and does not proclaim abolition. It is still a developed account of the dignity of measured judgment and the possible moral effectiveness of pardon.

**Primary:** [Plutarch, *On Controlling Anger*, chapter 11](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/De_cohibenda_ira%2A.html).

#### Plutarch's other moral essays and biographies — first/second centuries CE

Plutarch also examines friendship, family affection, self-mastery, and nonhuman life in essays conventionally grouped as *Moralia*. His *Lives* portray political character through paired exempla of virtue and vice. The value is both literary and philosophical: Greek moral concerns remain active and distinctive in the age of the early churches. Not every story is reliable biographical evidence, and the surviving corpus is diverse rather than one unified argument.


**Reading:** [Primary-text collection](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/home.html).
#### Dio Chrysostom — c. 40–115 CE

In his *On Kingship* discourses, Dio presents the just ruler as one whose excellence is measured by the security and welfare of subjects, not merely the size of an empire. *Discourse* 3 contrasts the ruler who rejoices in the flourishing of those governed with the tyrant who treats them as means to personal luxury. This is a pagan intellectual's morally demanding conception of government for the benefit of others.

**Primary:** [Dio Chrysostom, *On Kingship*, Discourse 3](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Dio_Chrysostom/Discourses/3%2A.html).

#### Juvenal — late first/early second century CE

In *Satire* 15, Juvenal writes that the ability to feel others' suffering separates human beings from the animals and underlies mutual assistance and the building of communities. He discusses tears and sympathy not merely as private sentiment but as a basis of collective human life. A very important caution: his satires also exhibit disturbing stereotypes and prejudices. One humane argument is not proof of a consistently humanitarian author. It is, however, a clear Roman literary treatment of shared vulnerability.

**Primary:** [Juvenal, *Satire* 15, particularly the section on tears and human fellow-feeling](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Juvenal_and_Persius/The_Satires_of_Juvenal/Satire_15).

#### Other imperial moral literature: moral exempla, consolation, and civic duty

Beyond the most obvious names, Roman declamation, funeral praise, consolation literature, and biographies continued to evaluate character by restraint, generosity, loyalty, the keeping of commitments, and honorable treatment of adversaries. These genres are valuable precisely when approached carefully: they establish the existence of a social expectation, not necessarily the frequency of behavior that satisfied it.

Early imperial conclusion: Non-Christian authors active in the same broad period as early Christianity wrote at length about forgiveness, moderation, humane treatment, and obligations to other human beings. The sources are usually best understood as continuations of Stoic, Platonic, and Roman civic traditions, rather than as responses to Jesus.

### Later Stoics: strong conceptual parallels, cautious claims about contact

*Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius, and Hierocles illustrate the continuation of older Stoic ethics after Christianity began. Their own awareness of Christianity may differ and cannot always be demonstrated or excluded.*

#### Epictetus — c. 50–135 CE

*Discourses* I.18 and I.28 develop the position that people do wrong because they judge mistakenly what is advantageous or good. Rather than hatred, the appropriate response can be patient correction and understanding. This is a moral psychology rooted in Socratic and Stoic views of error, not a doctrine invented in the second century.

**Caveat:** *Discourses* IV.7 refers to 'Galileans,' commonly understood as Christians. Whether Epictetus understood much about Christian ethics is uncertain, but one cannot claim he certainly never encountered Christianity.

**Locator:** Epictetus, *Discourses* I.18, I.28, IV.7; *Enchiridion* 42–43.


**Reading:** [Primary text, *Discourses*](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/discourses.html).
#### Marcus Aurelius — 121–180 CE

In *Meditations* II.1, Marcus describes difficult and offensive people as still belonging to the same human community, naturally designed for cooperation. In VII.22 he argues that humans should love even those who err and explains how recognition of ignorance and common nature supports that attitude. These passages are among the closest later pagan analogues to the ideal of loving enemies.

**Caveat:** *Meditations* XI.3 appears to mention Christians, though the textual status and interpretation have been debated. Marcus is evidence of Stoic moral continuity, not a safe witness to total ignorance of Christians. Nor is the emperor's philosophical diary proof that his imperial policies always exemplified its ideals.

**Locator:** Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* II.1, VII.22, XI.3.


**Reading:** [Primary text, *Meditations*](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.html).
#### Hierocles — second century CE

Hierocles' image of concentric circles of concern begins with oneself and extends through immediate family, extended family, fellow citizens, and ultimately other people. The exercise draws more distant people morally inward. It does not require absolute identical obligations to everyone; indeed, recent scholarly interpretations emphasize continued distinctions of proximity and responsibility. It is nevertheless an eloquent pagan argument for broadening social concern, grounded in Stoic ideas older than Christianity.

Scholarly analysis: Ralph Wedgwood, ["Hierocles' Concentric Circles," *Oxford Studies in Ancient Philosophy*](https://academic.oup.com/book/46592/chapter-abstract/409999353).

Qualification in one sentence: A first- or second-century resemblance is not evidence that a later Stoic had no contact with Christians; the pre-Christian ancestry of the Stoic argument, rather than a claim about the philosopher's private awareness, is the stronger historical point.

## Reading *Augustus* with the ancient evidence

John Williams's *Augustus* (1972) is a literary reconstruction told through invented letters, journals, and reminiscences. Its significance for this study is interpretive, not documentary. It makes visible the conflict among virtues that Roman writers themselves named: clemency, loyalty, public honor, justice, family duty, and political obligation. Augustus's dealings with Julia bring that collision into focus. An emperor who claims to defend ancestral morality also inflicts profound suffering in his intimate circle.

To determine what Romans *actually* said or did, the modern reader must return to distinct classes of evidence. **Cicero** shows how a Roman statesman theorized obligations, including those owed to opponents and strangers. **Sallust, Virgil, and Livy** portray choices about mercy and fair treatment in political speech, epic, and historiography. **The Roman laws** show what certain groups could demand or contest in institutional settings. **The *Res Gestae* and the Arles shield** show how Augustus claimed and displayed legitimacy. None of these sources independently proves that Rome became uniformly kind, but together they document an unusually rich moral culture.

The most revealing contrast is between *clementia* and *iustitia*. The ruler exercising clemency can choose to pardon a subject because he holds superior power. Justice asks a different question: which standards bind the ruler and protect the ruled? The two virtues may complement one another, but they can also conflict. Augustus's program of moral regulation, the exile of Julia, and the politics of public benefaction make sense against precisely this background. [*Res Gestae* 34](https://droitromain.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/Anglica/resgest_engl.htm) and the [Arles shield](https://www.persee.fr/doc/efr_0000-0000_1980_ant_43_1_1294) ground the ancient part of the comparison.

It is also important to distinguish *public benefaction* from *relief of destitution*. Temples, baths, spectacles, grain sales, and military payments could advance common interests and bind followers to rulers. Their recipients were not necessarily selected for poverty, and donors were often rewarded with prestige. This is strong evidence that political communities had ideas of obligation and public provision; it is weaker evidence for private altruism or unconditional care. The strongest Roman argument is not that Rome was benevolent by modern standards. It is that Rome's public life was already saturated with ethical expectations, alongside the evident failures to meet them.

## Four serious objections and what the evidence supports

### 1. Similarity does not mean identity

This objection is correct. Plato's nonretaliation principle and Buddhist non-hatred differ in explanation, audience, and consequences. Roman *clementia* often presupposes the superior's right to punish. A poor traveler's ferry passage is generosity rather than an abstract affirmation of the equal dignity of all human beings. Recognizing these distinctions is not a concession that the parallels are meaningless; it is the condition of making a useful comparison. A claim that Christianity uniquely combined or theologically grounded several familiar precepts is different from the untenable claim that nobody had expressed those precepts at all.

The strongest distinctions are between **not retaliating** and **positively helping an enemy**, and between **individual compassion** and **law or government provision**. The *Crito* establishes the first of these; *Counsels of Wisdom*, the *Dao De Jing*, and some Buddhist traditions approach the second; Roman grain statutes and appeals establish neither, but show independent public moral concepts. The evidence should be compared at the precise level of the proposition, never inflated by the word "love."

### 2. Are the dates and independence certain?

Some are; others are not. Plato's fourth-century-BCE dialogues, Cicero's 44-BCE treatise, pre-Christian Egyptian and Babylonian manuscripts, and Ashoka's third-century-BCE inscriptions could not have borrowed from a Christian movement that did not yet exist. That is the logical basis of the exclusive-origin rebuttal. Later manuscripts of old oral traditions are more complicated: one must distinguish the antiquity of a school or religious movement from the datability of individual phrases and redactions.

Later Roman Stoics also require care. Epictetus's *Discourses* 4.7 may allude to Christians under the term "Galileans"; Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* 11.3 apparently refers to Christians, though its textual status has been contested. Their arguments about rational kinship and the moral evaluation of wrongdoers stand in a recognizably older Stoic lineage, but the historian should not pretend to know their private information environment. A philosophical tradition can be independent in origin while later practitioners coexist with and possibly encounter Christianity.

Finally, *independent of Christianity* never means *developed in absolute cultural isolation*. Greeks learned from others; Romans drew from Greek traditions; intellectual traffic crossed Afro-Eurasia. Demonstrable **chronological priority** answers the claim about Christian invention regardless of which earlier cultures influenced each other.

### 3. These societies were often brutal and unequal

They were. Greek poleis practiced slavery, Romans waged imperial wars, Indian and Chinese ethical theories emerged within stratified societies, and kings' inscriptions often advertise conduct that critics would dispute. This does not overturn the existence of moral ideals. It shows why we must differentiate **the stated norm**, **a formal institution**, **a reported action**, and **reliable evidence of compliance**.

A legal restriction on magistrates demonstrates that certain powers were contested and regulated, not that all people received due process. A public grain distribution demonstrates material provision to an eligible category, not necessarily generosity to the poorest. An emperor celebrating clemency does not establish that he refrained from political violence. The same genre discipline would have to apply when studying the gap between the proclaimed morality and actual behavior of later Christian polities. Anachronistic claims that any ancient civilization achieved modern rights standards weaken rather than strengthen this argument.

### 4. Christianity may have communicated, organized, or popularized these ideas differently

That is a significant historical hypothesis and deserves investigation. Christianity's religious motivations, communal practices, charitable organization, preaching, educational institutions, and eventual political influence may have altered the reach of ethical norms in some regions and periods. Those changes would need to be demonstrated from evidence about institutions, participation, funding, and outcomes. They are not established simply by asserting that Christianity was the first tradition to take compassion seriously.

Origen himself made an argument of this kind when responding to Celsus. He accepted the question of whether Greeks had expressed similar truths and defended the practical accessibility of Christian moral teaching. The historical value of the exchange is not that either debater was a modern impartial scholar. It is that two ancient intellectuals were already distinguishing **the prior formulation of a moral principle** from **the social or pedagogical effectiveness of a particular way of expressing it**. [*Against Celsus* 7.58–61](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04167.htm) is the essential witness.

**What the evidence supports:** non-Christian predecessors for specified ethical propositions, sometimes centuries or millennia before Christianity; a pre-Christian civic morality in Rome; ongoing non-Christian elaborations during the early Empire; and a historically documented ancient debate about moral originality. **What it does not establish:** direct borrowing by Jesus, identical ethics in every culture, universal implementation of ideals, or the claim that Christianity contributed nothing distinctive.

## Annotated reading list

The following bibliography preserves the original breadth of primary texts and modern scholarship. **Primary** means the ancient passage is the relevant witness; **scholarly guide** means the linked modern work analyzes it. Many translations are still copyrighted even where the ancient original is not. Readers should use cited passage numbers and consult editions appropriate to their research.

This selective bibliography is intended to help readers verify passages and extend the research. An ancient work's location is the crucial reference: translations, divisions, and page numbers differ; bibliographic links are guides, not guarantees that every excerpt is complete or available without a subscription.

### Editorial standards and source cautions

- **Dating:** A text's presumed composition, first surviving witness, and traditional attribution to a founder are different facts. Where only a later collection preserves wording, no claim about exact pre-Christian phrasing is made.
- **Genre:** An inscription attests a ruler's claim; a law attests an enacted rule; a philosopher's treatise attests articulated doctrine; literary speech may be invented within a moralizing narrative.
- **Translation:** Direct quotations in this essay are intentionally short and attributed. This manuscript does not reproduce long passages from copyrighted modern translations. Before publication, independently check any quote against the linked edition.
- **Links:** Primary-source passage numbers are supplied wherever possible. External sites sometimes move or change their edition; links and publication rights should receive a final publishing-platform check.
- **Scope:** The survey excludes Jewish and biblical ethical evidence by design and surveys ancient Afro-Eurasian written cultures rather than every historical society. Later imperial Roman authors are comparisons, not chronological proof that Christianity inherited their exact formulations.
- **Cyrus Cylinder:** Avoid the unsupported claim that it is an ancient universal declaration of human rights.
- **The *Tirukkuṟaḷ*:** Do not assign the whole work to a confidently pre-Christian date; specialist research is preferable to popular claims of an exact date.

## Conclusion: an older and more crowded moral world

The evidence does not describe a single moral system passed intact from one empire to the next. It records sustained disagreement. Socrates questions revenge; a Babylonian sage advises kindness toward adversaries; an Egyptian instruction considers the destitute passenger; Mozi asks communities to care beyond their own members; early Buddhist traditions discipline hatred; Ashoka advertises welfare and moral restraint; Cicero treats justice, generosity, and duties toward wrongdoers as parts of political ethics; the Roman Senate honors clemency and justice as desirable qualities in a ruler.

Many societies that produced these teachings continued to exploit, conquer, and punish. Their contradictions are real. So is the historical presence of the teachings. The existence of moral thought is not a referendum on a culture's ethical perfection.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore substantial but specific: **Christianity did not introduce compassion, forgiveness, nonretaliation, positive concern for outsiders, or public moral obligation into a world that had never conceived of them.** It entered a world that had argued over these ideals for centuries, often across independent, non-biblical traditions. How Christianity reframed, transmitted, institutionalized, and sometimes contradicted such ideals is a further historical subject; it cannot be settled by an exclusive-origin slogan.

Celsus already understood that an earlier philosophical precedent was relevant to claims of novelty. Origen replied that the value and communicative power of a moral teaching mattered at least as much as its supposed novelty. The ancient debate gives us a better question than "who invented kindness?": **what did particular traditions ask people to do, how did they justify those duties, and what actually changed when their teachings were put into practice?**
