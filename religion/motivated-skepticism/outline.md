# **Analyzing Arguments from Motivated Skepticism**

### *Toward a New Argumentation Scheme for Selective Skepticism*

# Introduction (≈1 page)

Introduce a recurring argumentative pattern that appears in apologetics, pseudoscience, conspiracy theories, and ideological discourse.

Emphasize an important distinction:

> Skepticism itself is not the problem. Rational inquiry depends upon skepticism.

The problem is **selective skepticism**—skepticism that is deployed against one conclusion while ignoring the implications that skepticism would have for an interconnected body of knowledge.

State the central thesis:

> Many arguments instantiate a recurring pattern that can be analyzed as an **Argument from Motivated Skepticism (AMS)**. These arguments derive much of their persuasive force by treating knowledge as though it consisted of isolated propositions rather than an interconnected epistemic network.

# 2. Reductio Through Epistemic Consequences (≈1 page)

Introduce the general strategy.

Rather than defending every technical detail, temporarily grant the skeptical premise.

Then ask:

> **What follows?**

Explain that this is a form of **reductio through epistemic consequences.**

The issue is not simply contradiction.

It is inconsistency with the skeptic's remaining commitments.

Introduce the distinction:

**Local skepticism**

vs.

**Propagated skepticism**

Knowledge behaves like a network.

Removing one foundational node affects many others.

This shifts the debate from

"Is there uncertainty?"

to

"Can this uncertainty be consistently maintained?"

# 3. The Argument from Motivated Skepticism (AMS) (≈1 page)

Present the proposed Walton-style scheme.

### Scheme

Premise 1

Claim C is supported by evidence E.

Premise 2

Some feature of E introduces uncertainty.

Premise 3

Therefore confidence in C should be significantly reduced.

Hidden Premise

The skeptical consideration applies locally and does not undermine other accepted beliefs supported by the same evidence or methodology.

Conclusion

Reject (or suspend belief in) C.

Explain that the hidden premise usually performs nearly all of the argumentative work.

Discuss why AMS is defeasible rather than deductively invalid.

# 4. Hidden Assumptions of AMS (≈1.5 pages)

Introduce the assumptions one by one.

## H1 Locality

The criticism affects only the target claim.

---

## H2 Isolation

The challenged proposition can be detached from the remainder of scientific knowledge.

---

## H3 Asymmetric Burden

Uncertainty counts heavily against unwanted conclusions but not favored ones.

---

## H4 Non-propagation

The consequences of skepticism stop where the arguer wishes.

---

## H5 Selective Evidential Standards

Different standards of justification are quietly used for competing claims.

---

## H6 Stable Background Knowledge

Everything else can remain fixed while one conclusion changes.

Explain why each assumption is psychologically plausible while epistemically problematic.

# 5. Critical Questions (≈1.5 pages)

Following Walton's framework, develop systematic critical questions.

## Scope

Does the skepticism apply only here?

---

## Consistency

Would the arguer apply the same reasoning elsewhere?

---

## Dependency

What additional beliefs depend upon the challenged premise?

---

## Propagation

If accepted, what further conclusions become doubtful?

---

## Practical Commitment

Does the arguer behave consistently with those implications?

---

## Magnitude

How large an error would actually be required?

---

## Independent Confirmation

Do independent methods converge upon the same conclusion?

---

## Alternative Explanation

Can the skeptic explain the remaining evidence?

---

## Network Stability

Can the criticism be integrated without destabilizing large portions of accepted knowledge?

Explain that these questions frequently expose weaknesses **without requiring specialized technical expertise.**

# 6. Why AMS Is So Persuasive (≈1 page)

This section explains **why intelligent people often find these arguments compelling.**

The rhetorical success of AMS is itself philosophically interesting.

## Cognitive Asymmetry

Creating doubt is easier than constructing knowledge.

A single unanswered question can appear stronger than decades of converging evidence.

---

## Local Attention

People naturally focus on the criticized premise.

They rarely trace downstream implications.

---

## Hidden Networks

Most people do not appreciate how interconnected scientific knowledge actually is.

Geology appears separate from nuclear engineering.

Evolution appears separate from genetics.

Climate science appears separate from fluid dynamics.

In reality these disciplines mutually reinforce one another.

---

## Burden-Shifting

The skeptic asks one question.

The defender appears obligated to explain an entire scientific discipline.

This creates the illusion that the skeptic occupies the stronger position.

---

## Availability Bias

Concrete objections are cognitively vivid.

Large epistemic networks are abstract.

The human mind naturally overweights the former.

---

## Motivated Reasoning

The skepticism often originates from commitment to a preferred conclusion rather than neutral evaluation of evidence.

This selective deployment gives AMS much of its persuasive force.

Conclude:

AMS succeeds rhetorically because it exploits normal features of human cognition rather than because it provides a superior explanation.

# Conclusion (≈0.5 page)

Reiterate the central distinction.

The issue is **not skepticism.**

The issue is skepticism that is:

* selective,
* insulated from its consequences,
* inconsistent with broader epistemic commitments.

Summarize the contribution of the paper.

AMS provides:

* a new argumentation scheme,
* an account of its hidden assumptions,
* a systematic set of Walton-style critical questions,
* an explanation for its rhetorical effectiveness,
* and a framework for evaluating arguments in terms of **epistemic consistency rather than technical specialization.**

Close with a broader philosophical observation:

> Rational inquiry requires more than asking whether a claim might be mistaken. It requires asking whether our reasons for doubt can be consistently sustained across the interconnected web of beliefs that gives those reasons their meaning. Selective skepticism often appears persuasive precisely because it invites us to ignore that web. The purpose of the Argument from Motivated Skepticism is to make those hidden connections visible.
