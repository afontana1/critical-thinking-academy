## I. Introduction

The apparent compatibility of cosmological fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony obscures a deeper tension between the metaphysical assumptions that make each argument persuasive. Fine-tuning gains force from the idea that conscious, intelligent life depends upon a highly restricted range of physical conditions. Psychophysical harmony, by contrast, gains force from the apparent contingency of the relation between physical states and phenomenal states: the same physical or functional organization could, in principle, have been associated with radically different experiences. The more freedom one grants to the psychophysical mapping, however, the less obvious it becomes that the physical constants had to be finely adjusted in order for conscious life to exist at all.

Neil Sinhababu’s “electrons in love” objection brings this tension into focus. If God could have associated rich mentality with radically simpler physical systems under different psychophysical laws, then the usual inference from life-permitting physics to divine design is weakened. Conversely, if sophisticated mentality is tightly constrained by physical complexity, then the modal space appealed to by psychophysical harmony appears narrower than its defenders often suggest. The point is not that the two arguments are formally contradictory. Rather, they seem to pull in opposite directions unless they are embedded within a deeper and more carefully specified metaphysics of mind, matter, modality, and divine action.

That requirement creates a broader problem for natural theology. If one argument succeeds only by adopting one set of metaphysical assumptions, while another succeeds only by adopting a different set, then their combination cannot simply be treated as a cumulative case for “theism” without first showing that those assumptions belong to a single coherent theory. Otherwise, the explanatory burden is being carried by auxiliary commitments introduced on an argument-by-argument basis. This is where the charge of **metaphysical cherry-picking** becomes relevant: one risks selecting whatever metaphysical framework is most favorable to the conclusion in each local debate, while continuing to present the resulting arguments as evidence for one unified hypothesis.

The problem is amplified by the fact that “theism” itself is rarely specified with enough precision to generate the relevant predictions. Different theistic models can differ substantially in their views about embodiment, consciousness, divine preferences, value, natural law, and the extent to which God’s reasons are accessible to us. These differences matter because arguments from fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony depend not merely on the existence of a creator, but on claims about what such a creator would be likely to value and choose. In other words, both arguments depend upon substantive assumptions about divine psychology.

This paper argues that the tension identified by Sinhababu is therefore best understood as a case study in a more general methodological instability within philosophical apologetics. The central problem is not simply that particular arguments are controversial, nor that their premises can be disputed. It is that the assumptions used to support different arguments may fail to form a stable global model. When this happens, cumulative-case reasoning risks becoming a form of theoretical overfitting: rich theological assumptions are introduced when they increase the probability of the observed evidence, while a thinner and less committal conception of theism is retained when those same assumptions become vulnerable to objection.

The challenge, then, is not merely to show that fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony can each be reconciled with some form of theism. It is to show that both emerge from the same independently motivated metaphysical and theological framework. Without that prior specification, the cumulative case risks confusing post hoc accommodation with genuine predictive success.

## II. Fine-Tuning and Its Hidden Psychophysical Assumption

The standard fine-tuning argument is usually presented as an argument about the apparent sensitivity of life to the fundamental constants and initial conditions of the universe. The relevant physical parameters appear to occupy a narrow range compatible with the emergence of stable matter, complex chemistry, stars, planets, and eventually organisms capable of observation and reflection. The evidential force of the argument is then supposed to arise from the contrast between the apparent improbability of such a life-permitting universe under an unguided hypothesis and its greater expectedness under theism.

Yet this framing can obscure an important background assumption. The inference from life-permitting physics to intelligent or conscious life does not follow from physics alone. It depends upon some view about what kinds of physical systems are capable of realizing sophisticated mentality. In practice, the argument treats the possibility of observers as strongly constrained by the possibility of complex physical organization. If the constants had prevented stars, chemistry, or biological complexity, then there would have been no beings like us—and, more importantly for the argument, no beings sufficiently minded to count as the relevant kind of observers.

The tacit structure can be represented roughly as follows:

$$
\text{No sufficiently complex physical organization}
\rightarrow
\text{No sophisticated conscious observers}.
$$

This conditional is rarely the explicit target of the fine-tuning argument, but it performs substantial work within it. The argument does not merely require that our specific biological form depends upon fine-tuned physics. That much is comparatively uncontroversial. It requires something stronger: that the kinds of conscious, intelligent, or valuable beings relevant to the argument could not readily have existed under radically different physical conditions.

This distinction matters because the evidential significance of fine-tuning depends on what exactly is being treated as improbable. If the target is merely the existence of human beings, then the argument risks becoming anthropocentric in an uninteresting sense: of course our particular form of life depends upon the conditions that produced it. The stronger and more philosophically significant claim is that the universe had to fall within a narrow physical range in order for sophisticated mentality to exist at all, or at least in order for the kinds of minds a benevolent creator would plausibly value to exist.

That stronger claim imports a psychophysical assumption into what is often presented as a purely physical argument. It presupposes that mentality cannot simply be realized independently of the structural complexity made possible by our physical laws. If rich consciousness could be associated with radically simpler physical systems, then the fact that our universe permits complex chemistry and biology would no longer appear necessary for the existence of minded beings. The relevant question would cease to be merely whether the constants are finely tuned for organisms like us and would instead become whether those constants were required for consciousness in any sufficiently sophisticated form.

This is where the fine-tuning argument becomes vulnerable to a challenge from the metaphysics of mind. Its force depends not only on the rarity of life-permitting physics, but on a restriction concerning the range of possible realizers of mentality. The narrower that psychophysical range is taken to be, the stronger the fine-tuning argument appears. If only highly complex physical systems can support rich consciousness, then the physical preconditions for such systems acquire obvious evidential importance. But if the relation between physical organization and mentality is considerably more flexible, then the apparent necessity of fine-tuned physical conditions begins to erode.

This background commitment is particularly important because it is not forced upon the theist by theism itself. A theist who already accepts that mentality can exist independently of a physical substrate, as in the case of God, has at least prima facie reason to resist the idea that sophisticated consciousness must always depend upon highly complex physical realization. Even if one denies that this immediately establishes the possibility of simple physical objects possessing rich mental lives, it shows that the fine-tuning argument cannot simply assume, without further defense, that sophisticated mentality requires the kinds of elaborate material structures found in our universe.

The fine-tuning argument therefore contains a metaphysical dependency that is easy to miss. Its apparent conclusion concerns the explanatory significance of physical constants, but its force is partly determined by a prior answer to a psychophysical question: how constrained is mentality by physical complexity? Neil Sinhababu’s “electrons in love” objection presses directly on this hidden assumption. If the psychophysical relation could have been arranged differently, then a universe inhospitable to biological complexity need not thereby have been inhospitable to consciousness.

## III. Sinhababu’s “Electrons in Love” Objection

Neil Sinhababu’s objection to fine-tuning begins by challenging the assumption that the physical conditions necessary for organisms like us are also necessary for intelligent or valuable mentality as such. His argument turns on the metaphysical possibility of alternative psychophysical laws: laws connecting physical states with mental states in ways radically unlike those found in our world. If such laws are possible, then even a universe that is physically hostile to chemistry, biology, or complex nervous systems might nevertheless contain sophisticated conscious beings. Sinhababu’s memorable example is a world in which very simple physical properties are sufficient to realize rich mental states, so that a proton and an electron might literally instantiate romantic desire and become “electrons in love.”

The example is intentionally strange, but its argumentative purpose is straightforward. The fine-tuning argument ordinarily moves from the claim that most possible values of the physical constants would not permit the structures required for life to the conclusion that most such universes would not contain intelligent observers. Sinhababu contests this second step. Even if radically different constants would eliminate carbon, planets, stars, or organisms resembling anything in the actual world, it does not immediately follow that such universes would be devoid of intelligence or happiness. Under sufficiently “mind-friendly” psychophysical laws, simple physical systems could instantiate extremely sophisticated mental lives. Sinhababu therefore argues that, for a much wider range of physical constants, intelligent life may remain metaphysically possible once the psychophysical laws themselves are allowed to vary.

The relevant contrast can be expressed as follows. The fine-tuning argument tends to assume something like:

$$
\text{Non-life-permitting physics}
\rightarrow
\text{No intelligent conscious beings}.
$$

Sinhababu instead introduces an additional variable:

$$
\text{Physical laws and constants}
+
\text{Psychophysical laws}
\rightarrow
\text{Possible forms of mentality}.
$$

Once the psychophysical laws are included, the first conditional no longer follows automatically. A universe incapable of producing biological brains might still produce minds if the connection between mentality and physical realization were radically different from the actual one.

This matters especially for the comparative probability at the heart of the fine-tuning argument. Suppose the theistic hypothesis is invoked to explain why the constants fall within the narrow region that permits complex embodied life. The usual intuition is that a God who values intelligent conscious beings would have reason to select constants that make such beings possible. But if God could instead achieve the same general end by choosing very different psychophysical laws, then it becomes less clear why a life-permitting set of physical constants should be especially expected on theism. The theist would have multiple routes to the production of conscious agents:

$$
\text{Fine-tuned physics}
+
\text{actual psychophysical laws}
\rightarrow
\text{intelligent life},
$$

but also, potentially,

$$
\text{non-life-permitting physics}
+
\text{mind-friendly psychophysical laws}
\rightarrow
\text{intelligent life}.
$$

If both routes are available to a divine creator, then the existence of intelligent life does not strongly select the first. The fine-tuning argument cannot simply infer that God would need to tune the physical constants into the narrow range required by our form of embodiment.

Sinhababu’s objection is strengthened by a point internal to theistic metaphysics itself. A defender of theism ordinarily already accepts at least one instance of sophisticated mentality existing without a complex physical brain: God. Sinhababu argues that this makes it difficult for the fine-tuning advocate simply to dismiss alternative psychophysical possibilities on the ground that rich mentality necessarily requires highly complex physical realization. If a divine mind can exist without any physical body at all, then the theist needs an additional argument for why created minds could not, in principle, be associated with vastly simpler physical structures.

This does not establish that electrons could actually think, experience pleasure, or fall in love under the laws of our world. Nor does the objection depend on panpsychism being true. The argument is modal rather than empirical. Sinhababu needs only the claim that sufficiently different psychophysical laws are metaphysically possible. If that possibility is admitted, then the apparent necessity of finely tuned physical conditions for the existence of minds becomes much harder to maintain.

A natural response is to restrict the fine-tuning argument to universes governed by the actual psychophysical laws. Conditional on the actual relation between minds and brains, the physical constants may indeed need to occupy a very narrow range for intelligent observers to exist. Sinhababu explicitly considers this strategy, but it comes at a cost: the theist must now explain why a divine creator should be expected to choose the actual, comparatively restrictive psychophysical arrangement rather than a more permissive one. The explanatory burden has therefore been moved rather than eliminated. What began as an argument from the improbability of the physical constants now depends upon assumptions about why God would choose one set of psychophysical laws rather than another.

This is the point at which Sinhababu’s objection becomes especially important for the present argument. The dispute is no longer exclusively about cosmology. It concerns the amount of modal freedom that exists in the relationship between physical organization and consciousness. If that relationship is highly flexible, then fine-tuning loses much of its apparent necessity. If it is highly constrained, the fine-tuning argument is in a stronger position. Yet this same question about psychophysical flexibility reappears in the argument from psychophysical harmony, where the direction of argumentative pressure is reversed.

## IV. Psychophysical Harmony and the Reverse Pressure

The argument from psychophysical harmony places pressure on the very assumption that protects fine-tuning from Sinhababu’s objection. Whereas the fine-tuning argument is strengthened when sophisticated mentality is treated as tightly constrained by physical organization, psychophysical harmony derives much of its force from the claim that the connection between physical states and phenomenal states is highly contingent. The same physical or functional system, it is argued, could have been associated with very different conscious experiences. What is striking about the actual world is that the psychophysical mapping is not arbitrary, but systematically aligned with cognition, behavior, and survival.

Pain provides the clearest example. In the actual world, states that dispose an organism to withdraw from tissue damage are typically accompanied by experiences that feel bad. Likewise, states associated with nourishment, reproduction, social attachment, and other beneficial activities are often accompanied by experiences that feel good. Perceptual experience displays a similar pattern. When an organism forms a judgment that it is seeing red, its phenomenal state is ordinarily an experience of red rather than an unrelated color or an entirely different sensation. The relationship between experience and function therefore appears not merely correlated, but appropriately coordinated.

The significance of this harmony depends upon the possibility of disharmony. If physical or functional organization uniquely necessitated the corresponding phenomenal state, there would be little mystery in the fact that avoidance behavior is accompanied by pain rather than pleasure. The relationship would simply follow from the nature of the physical system. The argument becomes interesting only if many alternative psychophysical mappings are genuinely possible. The same physical system that produces pain in the actual world might instead have produced pleasure, emotional neutrality, visual experience, or some other phenomenal state entirely.

Schematically, the space of possibilities must include something like:

$$
P_1 \rightarrow M_1,
$$

while also allowing:

$$
P_1 \rightarrow M_2,
$$

$$
P_1 \rightarrow M_3,
$$

and perhaps a vast number of other mappings, where $$P_1$$ represents a fixed physical or functional state and $$M_1, M_2, M_3$$ represent very different phenomenal states.

Against that background, the actual mapping appears unusually well ordered. A world in which injury caused approach behavior because the associated experience was pleasurable, or in which perceptual judgments systematically failed to correspond with phenomenal experience, would be psychophysically disharmonious. The observed world instead contains widespread coordination between phenomenal character, cognition, motivation, and behavior. The theistic explanation is that such harmony is more expected if the psychophysical relation was selected by an intelligent agent with reason to create conscious beings whose experiences appropriately guide their actions and beliefs.

The structure of the argument can therefore be represented roughly as:

$$
\text{Large space of possible psychophysical mappings}
+
\text{small harmonious subset}
\rightarrow
\text{harmony is surprising without selection}.
$$

The larger the space of possible mappings, the more striking the actual harmony becomes. If almost any phenomenal state could have accompanied a particular functional state, then the fact that pain accompanies avoidance, pleasure accompanies attraction, and perception tends to correspond to judgment appears increasingly nontrivial.

But this is precisely where the argument begins to conflict with the assumptions that strengthen fine-tuning. If the psychophysical relationship is sufficiently flexible that a single physical system could have supported radically different phenomenal lives, then it becomes difficult to insist, without further argument, that sophisticated mentality itself is narrowly confined to the kinds of physical structures made possible by our finely tuned universe. The modal freedom used to generate the psychophysical harmony problem can also be used to generate Sinhababu’s alternative worlds.

Suppose, for example, that the relationship between physical organization and mentality is genuinely governed by contingent psychophysical laws. Once that contingency is admitted, it is no longer obvious why the actual psychophysical laws could not have been replaced by laws under which much simpler physical structures supported conscious thought, memory, desire, pleasure, and agency. The modal principle appealed to by psychophysical harmony therefore threatens to reopen the very possibility that the fine-tuning defender needed to restrict.

The relationship can be expressed as a tradeoff:

$$
\text{Greater psychophysical freedom}
\rightarrow
\text{greater improbability of harmony}
\rightarrow
\text{stronger psychophysical harmony argument},
$$

but at the same time:

$$
\text{Greater psychophysical freedom}
\rightarrow
\text{more possible realizers of mentality}
\rightarrow
\text{weaker necessity of life-permitting physical constants}.
$$

The inverse relationship also holds. If one responds to Sinhababu by insisting that sophisticated minds can only arise from highly complex physical structures, then the space of psychophysical possibilities has already been constrained. The more that physical organization determines or limits mentality, the less obvious it becomes that the actual psychophysical relationship was selected from an enormous space of arbitrary alternatives. This does not automatically defeat the argument from psychophysical harmony, since one might hold that physical complexity constrains the existence of consciousness while leaving its qualitative character radically underdetermined. But that distinction is itself a substantive metaphysical thesis requiring independent justification.

This is the central tension. The fine-tuning argument benefits from a restrictive picture of psychophysical possibility, while the psychophysical harmony argument benefits from a permissive one. Put simply:

$$
\boxed{
\text{More psychophysical freedom}
\Rightarrow
\text{stronger psychophysical harmony}
\text{ but weaker fine-tuning}
}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Less psychophysical freedom}
\Rightarrow
\text{stronger fine-tuning}
\text{ but weaker psychophysical harmony}
}
$$

This does not show that no coherent position can support both arguments. One could attempt to distinguish between the metaphysical conditions required for the existence of sophisticated consciousness and the conditions determining its phenomenal character. A defender might argue, for instance, that highly complex physical organization is necessary for sophisticated cognition while the particular qualitative experiences associated with that organization remain contingent. Such a position would block the move from psychophysical contingency to “electrons in love” while preserving enough contingency to make harmony surprising.

The difficulty is that this reconciliation cannot simply be assumed. It requires a substantive theory of which aspects of the mind–body relation are necessary, which are contingent, and why. Once that deeper theory is introduced, the evidential force of the two arguments depends upon its plausibility. The tension between fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony is therefore not best understood as a direct contradiction, but as a demand for metaphysical specification. The defender of both arguments must explain why mentality is constrained enough to make fine-tuning significant while remaining unconstrained enough to make psychophysical harmony improbable.

It is at this point that the issue becomes broader than either argument individually. If the required metaphysical distinctions are introduced only after the tension becomes apparent, and primarily for the purpose of preserving both arguments, then the question arises whether the background metaphysics is independently motivated or merely being adjusted to protect a preferred conclusion. That is the point at which the problem of metaphysical cherry-picking enters the discussion.

## V. The Need for Deeper Metaphysical Commitments

The tension between fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony does not amount to a formal contradiction. There are coherent ways of distinguishing the metaphysical commitments required by each argument. A defender might hold, for example, that the existence of sophisticated cognition requires highly complex physical organization, while the phenomenal character associated with that organization remains contingent. On this view, a sufficiently complex brain may be necessary for mentality of the relevant sort, but there is still no necessity that a particular functional state be accompanied by pain rather than pleasure, or by one perceptual quality rather than another. Such a position could, in principle, preserve both the fine-tuning argument and the argument from psychophysical harmony.

But this response does not dissolve the problem so much as relocate it. Once such distinctions are introduced, the success of both arguments depends upon a deeper metaphysical account of the relationship between physical structure, cognition, and phenomenal consciousness. The defender must specify which features of mentality are tightly constrained by physical organization, which are only contingently related to it, and why the boundary should be drawn where it is. These are not minor clarifications. They determine the modal space over which each argument operates.

The same is true of possible theistic responses to Sinhababu. A theist might grant that God could have instantiated rich mentality in simpler physical systems while maintaining that God had independent reasons not to do so. Perhaps God values embodied agency, stable causal interaction, evolutionary development, or a world governed by elegant and uniform natural laws. Any of these claims could, in principle, explain why God would prefer a universe containing biologically embodied minds rather than one populated by mentally sophisticated particles.

Yet once such considerations are invoked, the argument is no longer supported by theism alone. It depends upon a much richer package of assumptions concerning divine aims, the value of embodiment, the structure of creation, and the conditions under which conscious life is worth realizing. The relevant hypothesis begins to look less like:

$$
T = \text{God exists},
$$

and more like:

$$
T^* = T + M + V + D,
$$

where $$M$$ represents a particular metaphysics of mind, $$V$$ a particular theory of value, and $$D$$ a particular account of divine motivation.

This matters because the evidential force of the argument must then be assessed relative to the richer hypothesis. If fine-tuning is highly probable only given a theology on which God strongly prefers embodied, physically complex, law-governed agents, then it is that theology—not bare theism—that generates the prediction. Likewise, if psychophysical harmony is highly probable only given a metaphysics on which phenomenal character is radically contingent but cognition remains physically constrained, then that metaphysical structure is doing indispensable explanatory work.

The central issue therefore becomes one of independent justification. Are these deeper commitments antecedently motivated by a coherent theory of mind and God, or are they introduced only when needed to preserve the evidential force of a particular argument? The difference is crucial. There is nothing illegitimate about an argument depending upon background metaphysical assumptions. Every philosophical argument does. The problem arises when those assumptions remain implicit until a difficulty appears, and are then modified in whatever way is required to protect the desired conclusion.

At that point, what initially looked like two independent pieces of evidence for theism may instead depend upon a carefully calibrated set of auxiliary assumptions designed to make both observations fit. The more precise those assumptions become, the more pressing the question of whether they are being discovered through independent reasoning or selected retrospectively because they allow the arguments to survive.

The fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony tension therefore exposes a burden that cumulative natural theology cannot avoid. Before the arguments can be treated as mutually reinforcing, their shared metaphysical background must be specified. Otherwise, apparent compatibility may conceal a deeper instability: one argument is evaluated under one conception of mind and divine action, another under a different conception, and the two are combined only at the level of their common conclusion.

This is the point at which the problem can be described as one of **metaphysical cherry-picking**. The concern is no longer simply whether a reconciliation is possible. The more important question is whether the reconciliation arises from a stable and independently defensible worldview, or whether the worldview itself is being adjusted argument by argument in order to preserve an antecedently preferred conclusion.

## VI. Metaphysical Cherry-Picking and Model Instability

The problem becomes more serious once the fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony arguments are viewed as parts of a cumulative case. A cumulative case is not strengthened merely by collecting arguments that terminate in the same conclusion. The arguments must also be supported by a sufficiently stable background theory. If one argument requires one set of metaphysical assumptions and another requires a substantially different set, then simply labeling both conclusions “evidence for theism” can conceal important differences in the hypotheses actually doing the explanatory work.

This is the point at which the charge of **metaphysical cherry-picking** becomes especially relevant. The concern is not merely that the defender of theism adopts controversial metaphysical views. The stronger criticism is that different metaphysical assumptions may be selected locally, depending upon which argument is currently being defended. A restrictive theory of the relationship between consciousness and physical complexity may be adopted when defending fine-tuning, while a far more permissive account of psychophysical possibility may be invoked when defending psychophysical harmony. Each choice is understandable when considered in isolation. The problem is that the two choices are not automatically parts of a single coherent theory.

This can be represented by distinguishing theism itself from the auxiliary assumptions required by particular arguments. Let:

$$
T = \text{theism},
$$

and suppose the fine-tuning argument requires a collection of additional assumptions:

$$
A_{FT} = \{\text{relevant assumptions about consciousness, embodiment, value, and divine aims}\}.
$$

The actual hypothesis generating the alleged fine-tuning prediction is therefore not simply $$T$$, but:

$$
T + A_{FT}.
$$

Likewise, suppose the psychophysical harmony argument requires:

$$
A_{PH} = \{\text{assumptions about psychophysical contingency, rational harmony, and divine aims}\}.
$$

Its operative hypothesis is:

$$
T + A_{PH}.
$$

The fact that both contain $$T$$ does not establish that they constitute two independent confirmations of the same theory. That would require showing either that:

$$
A_{FT} \approx A_{PH},
$$

or that both sets of assumptions are independently entailed by, or at least naturally derived from, a single more comprehensive theistic model.

Without this further step, the reasoning risks becoming locally successful but globally unstable. Fine-tuning might be evidentially favorable relative to one highly specified version of theism:

$$
P(E_{FT}\mid T + A_{FT})
>
P(E_{FT}\mid N),
$$

while psychophysical harmony might favor another:

$$
P(E_{PH}\mid T + A_{PH})
>
P(E_{PH}\mid N).
$$

But neither comparison by itself establishes that a single theistic hypothesis predicts both observations well. What would actually be required for a cumulative argument is something closer to:

$$
P(E_{FT},E_{PH}\mid T^*)
>
P(E_{FT},E_{PH}\mid N),
$$

where $$T^*$$ is one sufficiently specified and independently motivated version of theism.

This distinction between **local explanatory success** and **global model coherence** is crucial. A theory can be made to explain many observations if its auxiliary assumptions are allowed to vary freely from one case to another. But the fact that a hypothesis can be adapted to explain each datum individually does not mean that the hypothesis had strong predictive content before the evidence was known. A model whose parameters are repeatedly adjusted in response to each new observation can achieve excellent fit while possessing relatively little independent explanatory power.

This is why the analogy to overfitting is useful. In statistical modeling, a sufficiently flexible model can often be made to accommodate an observed dataset extremely well. Yet excessive flexibility can reduce confidence that the model captures an underlying structure rather than merely reproducing the data it was constructed to fit. A similar danger arises in natural theology when metaphysical and theological assumptions are repeatedly modified so that whatever feature of the world is under discussion becomes probable on theism.

The point is not that all theoretical adjustment is illegitimate. Philosophical and scientific theories are routinely refined in response to problems. What distinguishes legitimate refinement from ad hoc accommodation is that the revisions should be constrained by considerations independent of the particular observation they are introduced to explain. An account of the mind-body relation, for example, should ideally be motivated by a broader philosophy of mind rather than chosen primarily because one version strengthens fine-tuning and another strengthens psychophysical harmony. Likewise, claims about what God values should arise from an independently defensible theology rather than being introduced selectively whenever they make a particular feature of reality more expected.

The danger becomes especially clear when auxiliary assumptions move in opposite directions depending upon argumentative need. When defending fine-tuning, the theist may emphasize the rarity of physically embodied intelligent life and the demanding structural conditions required for sophisticated minds. When defending psychophysical harmony, the same broader apologetic project may emphasize how radically differently consciousness could have been mapped onto physical processes. When confronting the “electrons in love” objection, the space of psychophysical possibility may then be restricted again by appealing to the special value of embodiment, biological complexity, or stable causal interaction.

Each move may be individually defensible. What is methodologically questionable is the absence of an antecedent theory explaining why precisely this combination of restrictions and freedoms should be accepted. If the metaphysical space is narrowed or expanded according to which argument currently benefits from the adjustment, then the direction of justification has been reversed. Instead of beginning with a theory and asking what follows from it, one begins with the desired explanatory result and selects the theoretical commitments that will produce it.

This is why metaphysical cherry-picking is more than the ordinary observation that philosophical arguments depend upon controversial premises. It concerns the **selection procedure** governing those premises. A stable theory should constrain what the defender is allowed to say across different arguments. Commitments adopted in one context should generate consequences in others, even when those consequences are inconvenient. If a position concerning psychophysical possibility strengthens one argument but weakens another, that tradeoff should be acknowledged rather than dissolved by silently shifting metaphysical frameworks.

The burden on a cumulative case for theism is therefore stronger than showing that every relevant feature of the world is compatible with some version of theism. Compatibility is cheap when the hypothesis is allowed sufficient internal variation. The more important question is whether a single form of theism, equipped with a stable set of metaphysical commitments, makes the entire body of evidence comparatively expected.

This exposes a deeper methodological principle:

$$
\boxed{
\text{A cumulative case requires cumulative coherence, not merely cumulative compatibility.}
}
$$

Fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony provide a useful case study because the tension between them makes the underlying instability visible. The same phenomenon, however, extends beyond these two arguments. Once “theism” is treated as a broad family containing many different metaphysical and theological specifications, a further question emerges: what exactly is the hypothesis whose probability is supposedly being increased by the cumulative case?

## VII. “Theism” Is Not a Single Predictive Hypothesis

The problem of metaphysical cherry-picking is intensified by a more basic issue: “theism” is often treated as though it were a single, sufficiently specified hypothesis, when in fact it names a broad family of views with substantially different metaphysical and theological commitments. This matters because evidential arguments require a hypothesis with enough content to generate expectations. A claim as minimal as “God exists” does not, by itself, determine what kinds of worlds God would create, what forms of consciousness God would value, whether God would prefer embodied life, or whether God would favor harmonious psychophysical laws.

Different forms of theism can answer these questions in radically different ways. One theist might hold that consciousness can exist independently of any physical substrate. Another might think created mentality requires embodiment. One might regard psychophysical laws as freely chosen by God, while another might see them as necessary consequences of the natures of minds and bodies. A theist might endorse substance dualism, hylomorphism, idealism, panpsychism, or some version of physicalism compatible with divine creation. Likewise, different theological traditions can disagree about the kinds of goods God would prioritize, the extent to which divine reasons are accessible to human understanding, and the degree of freedom God possesses in choosing the structure of creation.

These differences are not peripheral to fine-tuning or psychophysical harmony. They directly affect the probabilities those arguments attempt to assign. A form of theism on which God strongly values biologically embodied rational agents may make a finely tuned physical universe comparatively expected. A form of theism on which minds need no complex physical realization may not. Similarly, a theology on which God freely selects psychophysical laws for the sake of rational and affective harmony may strongly predict psychophysical harmony, while a theory on which psychophysical relations are metaphysically necessary would remove much of the alleged coincidence in the first place.

It is therefore misleading to write simply:

$$
P(E\mid T),
$$

where $$T$$ stands for “theism,” unless the content of $$T$$ has been specified with enough detail to determine how the evidence bears on it. More realistically, what is called theism consists of a family of hypotheses:

$$
T = T_1 \lor T_2 \lor T_3 \lor \cdots,
$$

where each $$T_i$$ represents a different specification of divine nature, metaphysics, values, and creative intentions.

Once this is recognized, the relationship between evidence and theism becomes more complicated. Suppose fine-tuning is highly expected on $$T_1$$ but not on $$T_2$$, while psychophysical harmony is highly expected on $$T_2$$ but not on $$T_1$$. It would be illegitimate simply to treat both observations as independent confirmations of a single undifferentiated hypothesis. The fact that different members of a family can explain different observations does not establish that the family as a whole predicts all of them well.

This becomes particularly important in cumulative-case reasoning. If the argument from fine-tuning derives its force from one version of theism, while psychophysical harmony derives its force from another, then the two arguments cannot simply be added together without specifying how much prior probability should be assigned to each theological model and how each model performs against the full range of evidence. Formally, the probability of the evidence given a disjunctive theistic hypothesis would depend on the weighted performance of its constituent theories:

$$
P(E\mid T)
=
\sum_i P(E\mid T_i)P(T_i\mid T).
$$

The fact that there exists some $$T_i$$ for which the evidence is highly expected is therefore not enough. If most plausible forms of theism do not strongly predict the evidence, then the likelihood of the evidence on “theism” may be much lower than the rhetoric of the individual argument suggests.

This reveals an important distinction between **accommodation** and **prediction**. A sufficiently broad family of theories may be able to accommodate many different possible outcomes. If the universe is finely tuned, one form of theism can explain why. If it is not finely tuned but contains simple conscious entities, another form might explain that. If psychophysical laws are contingent and harmonious, one theology can accommodate them; if they are necessary, another theology can accommodate that instead. Such flexibility can make theism difficult to rule out, but it does not automatically make theism predictively powerful.

Indeed, there is a tradeoff between breadth and informativeness. The wider the range of worlds compatible with a hypothesis, the less strongly any particular observation is predicted by that hypothesis. A highly specific theological theory may make strong predictions, but it is correspondingly more vulnerable to counterevidence. A very broad conception of theism may survive a wider range of possible observations, but only because it says less about what should be expected.

This creates a recurring instability in apologetic argumentation. When evidential support is being claimed, a comparatively rich form of theism is often employed. God is taken to value conscious life, rational agency, moral freedom, embodied relationships, beauty, regular natural laws, and psychophysical harmony. These substantive claims allow the theist to argue that certain features of the world are likely given divine creation. Yet when difficulties arise, it is possible to retreat to a much thinner conception of theism and insist that theism itself makes few commitments about what kind of universe God would create.

The two conceptions cannot perform the same argumentative function. A thin theism may be defensible against a wide range of objections precisely because it makes few predictions. But for the same reason, it cannot generate the strong likelihoods required by design arguments. Conversely, a richly specified theism can generate those likelihoods, but then the additional theological commitments must themselves be defended and consistently applied.

This produces a dilemma for cumulative natural theology. If theism is defined minimally enough to encompass the wide variety of views ordinarily counted as theistic, then it is unclear whether it possesses enough predictive content for arguments from fine-tuning or psychophysical harmony to get started. If it is specified richly enough to make those observations likely, then the argument is no longer evidence for “theism” in general, but for a much narrower theological theory.

The evidential conclusion should therefore track the specificity of the hypothesis that actually generated the prediction. If the operative model is:

$$
T^* =
\text{a God with a particular nature, value structure, metaphysics, and set of creative aims},
$$

then the evidence, if successful, supports $$T^*$$ rather than theism simpliciter. To subsequently present that result as evidence for theism in the abstract risks obscuring how much substantive theological machinery was required to obtain the likelihood advantage in the first place.

This concern leads directly to the problem of divine psychology. Once theism is specified enough to generate predictions, those predictions inevitably depend upon claims about what God would value, prefer, intend, and choose. The question is therefore no longer simply whether God could produce the observed world, but whether we have any principled basis for assigning a high probability to God producing this world rather than the many alternatives that theism appears to leave open.

## VIII. Divine Psychology and the Problem of Theistic Prediction

Once theism is specified richly enough to generate evidential predictions, the problem of divine psychology becomes unavoidable. Fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony do not follow merely from the proposition that God exists. They require further claims about what God would value, what kinds of beings God would wish to create, and what means God would prefer for realizing those ends. The evidential force of these arguments therefore depends not simply on metaphysics, but on assumptions about divine motivation.

The fine-tuning argument, for example, requires more than the existence of a powerful creator. To make a life-permitting universe probable, one must assume that God has reason to create conscious or intelligent beings, that these beings are valuable in a way that provides God with reason to actualize them, and, more specifically, that God has reason to realize them through a universe containing the sort of physical complexity permitted by our constants. Once Sinhababu’s objection is taken seriously, the argument requires still more. The defender must explain why God would prefer complex embodied agents over radically simpler systems capable of supporting mentality under alternative psychophysical laws.

The operative structure therefore looks less like:

$$
T \rightarrow E_{FT},
$$

and more like:

$$
T + D_1 + D_2 + D_3 + \cdots \rightarrow E_{FT},
$$

where the $$D_i$$ represent claims about divine preferences and purposes.

Psychophysical harmony requires an analogous set of commitments. The defender must assume that God would value a systematic fit between phenomenology and function: that pain should rationalize avoidance, pleasure should support attraction, perceptual experience should align with belief and report, and conscious states more generally should bear an intelligible relationship to action and cognition. Yet none of this follows directly from the bare claim that an omnipotent and omniscient creator exists. It depends on substantive judgments about which forms of conscious life a perfect being would regard as worth creating.

The relevant likelihood is therefore not simply:

$$
P(E\mid T),
$$

but something closer to:

$$
P(E\mid T,D),
$$

where $$D$$ stands for a theory of divine psychology.

This introduces an epistemic difficulty. In order for theism to gain evidential support through comparative likelihoods, we must have some justified basis for thinking that the observed state of affairs is more likely given God than its alternatives. But if divine preferences are themselves uncertain, then the likelihood term becomes correspondingly uncertain. It is not enough that God **could** have reason to produce a finely tuned or psychophysically harmonious world. The argument requires that such a world be sufficiently expected on theism to create an evidential contrast with naturalism.

This distinction between possibility and probability is crucial. Much apologetic reasoning moves too quickly from:

$$
\text{God could have a reason to produce }E
$$

to:

$$
P(E\mid T)\text{ is high}.
$$

But those claims are very different. An omnipotent agent may have possible reasons for realizing many mutually incompatible worlds. Unless there is some principled account of the relative strength of those reasons, the mere availability of a divine rationale does little to establish that our world was antecedently probable.

Sinhababu’s objection again makes this especially clear. If God desires conscious life, perhaps fine-tuned physics is one means of achieving that goal. But if alternative psychophysical laws would also permit conscious life under radically different physical conditions, then the divine goal underdetermines the means. To recover the original likelihood claim, the defender must add further assumptions about God’s preference for physical complexity, embodiment, natural-law regularity, evolutionary history, or some related good. Each additional move makes the divine psychology more specific.

The same dynamic occurs with psychophysical harmony. Even if one grants that a good God would have reason to prefer harmonious psychophysical laws, it remains open whether that reason would dominate all competing considerations. A world with different psychophysical relationships might realize other goods. A creator might value autonomy, novelty, diversity, simplicity, or forms of consciousness radically unlike our own. Without a sufficiently developed theory of divine value, it is difficult to justify assigning high probability to the precise form of harmony we observe.

This problem becomes more serious when natural theology alternates between confidence and skepticism about divine psychology. In favorable contexts, strong claims are often made about what God would likely do: God would value conscious life, rational agency, moral goodness, reliable cognition, orderly natural laws, or harmonious experience. These claims are then used to generate evidential predictions. Yet when apparently unfavorable evidence is introduced—gratuitous suffering, hiddenness, disorder, evolutionary waste, or other features of the world that seem difficult to reconcile with perfect goodness—the response may appeal to our limited ability to understand God’s reasons.

There may be legitimate reasons for such epistemic humility. The problem is the asymmetry. If human beings are too cognitively limited to infer much about what God would prefer when confronting disconfirming evidence, then the same limitation threatens the favorable likelihood judgments used by design arguments. One cannot simply assume transparent access to divine purposes when the evidence appears supportive and invoke divine inscrutability when it does not.

The methodological tension can be put this way:

$$
\text{Predictive theism requires accessible divine psychology},
$$

whereas:

$$
\text{defensive theism often benefits from inscrutable divine psychology}.
$$

The more accessible God’s motivations are taken to be, the easier it becomes to argue that certain features of the universe are expected on theism. But the same accessibility makes theism more vulnerable when the world contains features that appear inconsistent with those motivations. Conversely, the more inscrutable divine reasons are taken to be, the easier it becomes to resist certain objections, but the harder it becomes to generate the strong predictions required by fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony.

This creates a further version of the instability already identified. Theism can be made predictively rich when explanatory success is needed and predictively thin when counterevidence appears. But a hypothesis cannot legitimately derive evidential support from specific predictions while simultaneously denying that sufficiently specific predictions can be made whenever they become inconvenient.

The problem of divine psychology therefore sits at the center of the broader critique. It connects the underspecification of theism with the instability of its auxiliary assumptions. Once a theistic hypothesis is made specific enough to explain fine-tuning or psychophysical harmony, it acquires substantive commitments about divine value and intention. Those commitments should then remain in place when the theory is assessed against the rest of the evidence.

This point also clarifies why the next methodological concern is not merely rhetorical. If a richly specified conception of God is invoked to generate favorable predictions, but a much thinner and more inscrutable conception is invoked to defend against objections, the argumentative structure begins to resemble a motte-and-bailey. The predictive theology does the evidential work, while the minimal theology absorbs the criticism.

## IX. Motte-and-Bailey and Shotgun Apologetics

The instability described above creates a natural opening for a motte-and-bailey structure. The “bailey” is a comparatively rich and predictive conception of God. It includes substantive claims about divine values, divine purposes, the importance of conscious life, the desirability of rational agency, the value of embodiment, the preference for orderly natural laws, and the appropriateness of psychophysical harmony. These commitments are what allow the theist to argue that certain features of the world are more probable given theism than they would be otherwise.

The “motte,” by contrast, is a much thinner and less committal form of theism. It may amount to little more than the claim that there exists an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good creator whose reasons may exceed human understanding. This thinner position is much harder to dislodge because it makes comparatively few predictions. But precisely for that reason, it cannot do the same explanatory work as the richer theology.

The problem arises when the argument moves back and forth between these two levels of specification. When fine-tuning or psychophysical harmony is being offered as positive evidence, theism is treated as predictively rich. We are told that God would plausibly value conscious beings, rationally integrated experience, stable causal order, or some other good that makes the observed feature expected. But when contrary evidence is raised, the conception of God can contract. The defender may then insist that divine reasons are too complex for us to know what God would or would not permit.

This movement can be represented schematically:

$$
\text{Rich theism}
\rightarrow
\text{strong predictions}
\rightarrow
\text{positive evidential argument},
$$

followed by:

$$
\text{Thin theism}
\rightarrow
\text{weak predictions}
\rightarrow
\text{resistance to counterevidence}.
$$

The asymmetry is epistemically significant. The rich model earns evidential credit by making the world seem expected, while the thin model avoids evidential liability by refusing to make equally specific commitments. If the evidential case depends on the richer model, then objections should also be assessed against that same model. Otherwise, the hypothesis receiving confirmation is not the same hypothesis being defended.

This concern becomes even more serious when apologetic reasoning is “shotgunned” across a large number of arguments. A cumulative case may include arguments from fine-tuning, psychophysical harmony, morality, consciousness, reason, religious experience, contingency, beauty, and so forth. There is nothing inherently problematic about a cumulative case. Multiple independent pieces of evidence can genuinely increase support for a theory. The difficulty arises when each argument relies on a different local specification of God.

Suppose one argument requires:

$$
T_1 = \text{a God who strongly values embodied rational life},
$$

while another requires:

$$
T_2 = \text{a God who permits broad psychophysical contingency},
$$

and another requires:

$$
T_3 = \text{a God whose reasons are substantially inscrutable}.
$$

If these specifications are not shown to be mutually compatible parts of a single coherent model, then their evidential contributions cannot simply be added together. The fact that each argument terminates in the word “God” or “theism” does not establish that they are confirming the same hypothesis.

This is the deeper problem with shotgun apologetics. The cumulative appearance may be generated by breadth rather than coherence. A large number of arguments can create the impression that many independent lines of evidence converge on the same conclusion, while in reality each argument may depend on its own bespoke theological and metaphysical background assumptions.

The relevant requirement is not merely:

$$
E_1 \text{ supports some } T_i,
$$

$$
E_2 \text{ supports some } T_j,
$$

$$
E_3 \text{ supports some } T_k,
$$

but rather:

$$
E_1,E_2,E_3 \text{ jointly support the same } T^*.
$$

Without this stronger condition, the cumulative case risks becoming a collection of local accommodations rather than a unified explanatory theory.

The analogy to overfitting becomes useful again here. A model with enough adjustable parameters can often be made to fit a large dataset. But a model that must alter its parameterization for every individual datum has not thereby demonstrated strong predictive power. It may instead be tracking the data after the fact. Likewise, an apologetic framework with enough flexibility in divine psychology, metaphysics, and theology can often explain why God would permit or desire almost any observed feature of reality.

This is especially problematic when no clear evidential defeat conditions are specified. If finely tuned constants are observed, that can be explained by divine concern for life. If they were not finely tuned but consciousness still existed, that might instead be explained by different psychophysical laws. If psychophysical harmony exists, God values harmony. If severe disharmony existed, perhaps God had unknown higher-order reasons. If the universe is orderly, God values order. If it is disorderly, divine purposes may be inaccessible.

A theory capable of surviving all such possibilities may be resilient, but resilience alone is not evidential strength. The more outcomes a theory can accommodate without significant revision, the less informative its successful accommodation of the actual outcome becomes.

This is why the motte-and-bailey and shotgun problems reinforce one another. The shotgun strategy multiplies locally successful arguments, while the motte-and-bailey structure allows the underlying theological commitments to expand and contract as needed. Together, they create the appearance of cumulative support without necessarily providing a stable cumulative model.

The methodological demand is therefore straightforward: the same theistic hypothesis should be carried through the entire evidential case. If the positive arguments require a rich conception of God, then that rich conception must remain in place when objections are considered. If the defender instead wishes to retreat to a minimal and inscrutable theism, then the evidential ambitions of the positive arguments must be correspondingly reduced.

The issue is not that philosophical apologetics uses many arguments. The issue is whether those arguments are genuinely cumulative rather than merely adjacent. A real cumulative case requires not only multiple lines of evidence, but also a single sufficiently specified theory capable of bearing the weight of all of them at once.

## X. Apologetics, Commitment Structures, and Truth-Tracking

The preceding concerns point toward a broader methodological criticism of philosophical apologetics. The strongest version of this criticism does not depend on attributing insincerity, bad faith, or conscious manipulation to individual apologists. The problem can arise even when a defender is entirely sincere. What matters is the structure of the reasoning: whether the method allows a prior commitment to remain fixed while the surrounding metaphysical and theological assumptions are repeatedly adjusted in response to objections.

This distinction is important. It would be difficult, and often unfair, to argue that apologetic reasoning is simply motivated by a desire to preserve belief rather than discover truth. But one can make a stronger and more defensible methodological claim: a framework may systematically encourage confirmation-preserving reasoning even when those using it are genuinely attempting to reason well. If the central commitment is treated as non-negotiable while auxiliary commitments remain highly flexible, then apparent counterevidence will tend to produce modifications to the surrounding theory rather than revisions to the central belief itself.

The structure can be represented as follows:

$$
T = \text{fixed commitment to theism},
$$

while:

$$
A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots
$$

represent auxiliary assumptions about consciousness, value, divine motivation, modality, natural law, or human epistemic limitations.

When some observation $$E$$ appears difficult to reconcile with the current model, the framework need not revise $$T$$. Instead, it can move from:

$$
T + A_1
$$

to:

$$
T + A_2.
$$

If a further difficulty arises, it can move again:

$$
T + A_2
\rightarrow
T + A_3.
$$

There is nothing inherently illegitimate about revising auxiliary assumptions. Any sophisticated theory develops in response to new arguments and evidence. The epistemic concern arises when there is no sufficiently independent constraint on which revisions are permitted. If the only stable feature of the theory is the conclusion that theism must remain viable, while almost everything else is negotiable, then the framework begins to lose falsifiability in a broader philosophical sense. It becomes unclear what evidence would actually count against the view rather than merely prompting another adjustment.

This is closely related to the difference between **truth-tracking** and **commitment-preservation**. A truth-tracking method should expose a theory to genuine risk. The same commitments that allow a theory to explain favorable evidence should also generate vulnerabilities. A theory that predicts order should incur some evidential cost when confronted with disorder. A theory that predicts rational psychophysical harmony should incur some cost if systematic disharmony is observed. A theory that appeals to accessible divine reasons in one context should not be able to invoke complete divine inscrutability in another without paying an explanatory price.

By contrast, a commitment-preserving structure can allow the evidential standards themselves to shift. Positive evidence may be evaluated under a richly specified theology, while negative evidence is evaluated under a more minimal one. Metaphysical necessity may be emphasized when contingency would create a problem, while contingency may be emphasized when necessity would weaken an argument. Divine motives may be treated as intelligible when they generate favorable likelihoods and opaque when they generate unfavorable ones.

The danger is therefore not merely that apologetic arguments sometimes contain ad hoc responses. It is that the method can become **directionally asymmetric**. The space of acceptable theoretical revisions may be constrained primarily by one requirement:

$$
\text{preserve } T.
$$

If this is the organizing principle, then the theory is no longer being tested in the same way as a hypothesis whose central commitments are themselves allowed to rise or fall with the evidence.

This helps explain why the earlier problems of metaphysical cherry-picking, divine psychology, motte-and-bailey reasoning, and argumentative shotgunning are not independent defects. They can all be manifestations of the same deeper structure. Metaphysical cherry-picking allows the auxiliary theory to change. Divine psychology provides a large space of possible explanations for why God would choose one world rather than another. The motte-and-bailey permits shifts in how much theological content is asserted at any given moment. Shotgunning multiplies the number of independent routes by which some feature of reality can be interpreted as confirmatory. Together, these practices can make the central conclusion highly resistant to evidential pressure.

The resulting problem resembles theoretical overfitting. A sufficiently flexible framework can explain the observed data very well after the fact. But explanatory fit is not the same thing as predictive success. The crucial question is whether the theory would have assigned substantial probability to the evidence before the evidence was incorporated into the model.

In ideal form, the direction of reasoning should be:

$$
\text{Theory}
\rightarrow
\text{Predictions}
\rightarrow
\text{Comparison with evidence}.
$$

The problematic structure reverses this:

$$
\text{Observed evidence}
\rightarrow
\text{selection of auxiliary assumptions}
\rightarrow
\text{construction of a favorable theistic explanation}.
$$

The latter procedure can produce an explanation for almost any observation, but its capacity to do so is precisely what weakens its evidential significance.

This does not entail that philosophical apologetics is uniquely guilty of such reasoning. Similar problems can arise in metaphysics, political philosophy, scientific theorizing, and virtually any domain in which researchers have strong antecedent commitments. Nor does it follow that every apologetic argument is merely post hoc rationalization. The criticism is methodological and conditional: to the extent that apologetic practice permits unrestricted adjustment of its auxiliary commitments while preserving the central conclusion, its capacity to track truth is weakened.

The appropriate response is therefore not to abandon cumulative reasoning, but to impose stronger constraints on it. A serious cumulative case should specify its theoretical commitments in advance, identify which assumptions are doing the predictive work, and explain how those assumptions apply consistently across different domains of evidence. It should also acknowledge genuine tradeoffs. If a commitment adopted to strengthen fine-tuning weakens psychophysical harmony, that cost should be counted. If a theory of divine psychology makes some data expected but other data surprising, both effects should enter the overall assessment.

Most importantly, the theory must allow for the possibility of evidential failure. If no conceivable pattern of evidence would significantly reduce confidence in the central hypothesis because some new auxiliary assumption could always be introduced, then the framework has ceased to function as a serious comparative explanation.

The broader criticism can therefore be stated without speculating about anyone’s motives. The problem is not that apologists necessarily reason dishonestly. It is that an unconstrained commitment structure can make post hoc accommodation too easy. If theism is to function as a genuine explanatory hypothesis rather than a conclusion protected by indefinitely adjustable auxiliaries, then its defenders must specify in advance what the theory commits them to, what it predicts, and what evidence would count against it.

## XI. Objection: Is This Just Ordinary Theory Refinement?

A defender of natural theology can object that the preceding criticism sets an unrealistically high standard. No serious theory arrives fully specified in advance, and there is nothing inherently suspicious about refining one’s metaphysics in response to objections. Scientific theories are routinely modified when new evidence reveals that an earlier formulation was incomplete. Philosophical theories develop in the same way. The fact that a theist introduces distinctions concerning embodiment, psychophysical necessity, divine value, or the nature of consciousness does not by itself show that the revisions are ad hoc.

This objection is correct as far as it goes. The problem is not simply that auxiliary assumptions are added or modified. Nor is the standard that every commitment must be fixed before any evidence is considered. The relevant distinction is between **constrained theory refinement** and **post hoc accommodation**.

A legitimate refinement should satisfy at least three conditions. First, the new commitment should possess some justification independent of the particular evidential problem it is introduced to solve. Second, it should generate consequences beyond the local argument in which it first appears. Third, those consequences should be allowed to count against the theory when they are unfavorable.

Consider again the response to Sinhababu according to which God has reason to prefer embodied, physically complex creatures rather than mentally sophisticated particles. Such a response is not automatically ad hoc. A theist might have a broader and independently developed theology according to which embodiment, interpersonal dependence, causal interaction, vulnerability, and participation in a common physical world are genuine goods. If those commitments precede the fine-tuning debate and consistently shape the theist’s expectations elsewhere, they may provide a principled response to the “electrons in love” objection.

But that response acquires theoretical costs. If embodiment is assigned substantial value in order to explain why God would choose finely tuned physics, then the same claim must constrain the theory in other contexts. It cannot simply disappear when embodiment creates difficulties elsewhere. Likewise, if the defender claims that sophisticated cognition necessarily requires complex physical realization, that metaphysical commitment should restrict which psychophysical possibilities may later be invoked when defending psychophysical harmony.

This is the central difference between a theory and a collection of local responses. A theory imposes cross-contextual constraints. A commitment adopted in one part of the system limits what can be said in another. Ad hoc accommodation, by contrast, occurs when assumptions are permitted to change precisely where they become inconvenient.

The distinction can be represented schematically. Legitimate refinement has the form:

$$
T + A
\rightarrow
\{E_1,E_2,E_3,\ldots\},
$$

where a single auxiliary commitment $$A$$ has consequences across multiple evidential domains.

The problematic structure is closer to:

$$
E_1 \rightarrow A_1,
$$

$$
E_2 \rightarrow A_2,
$$

$$
E_3 \rightarrow A_3,
$$

where each observation prompts the introduction of a new assumption chosen primarily because it preserves the desired conclusion.

The issue is therefore not whether natural theology may develop increasingly sophisticated metaphysical commitments. It plainly may. The question is whether those commitments reduce the theory’s flexibility by imposing genuine constraints, or merely increase its flexibility by supplying additional ways to accommodate whatever is observed.

A defender might further argue that this criticism applies equally to naturalism. Naturalists disagree about consciousness, modality, value, laws of nature, and the foundations of probability. There is no single fully specified naturalistic theory either. This point should also be granted. An evidential comparison between theism and naturalism cannot fairly treat naturalism as a perfectly precise hypothesis while criticizing theism for internal diversity.

But this response does not eliminate the problem. It instead generalizes the methodological requirement. Both hypotheses must be specified at the level at which they are actually making predictions. If a particular fine-tuning argument compares a richly specified form of theism against a particular naturalistic cosmology, then the comparison should be made at that level of specificity. What is illegitimate is allowing one side to inherit the predictive advantages of a narrow hypothesis while retaining the defensive flexibility of a broad one.

The same principle applies to both sides:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Predictive strength must be assessed at the same level of theoretical specification.}
}
$$

If theism is treated as a broad disjunction of many possible theological models, then its likelihoods must reflect that breadth. If it is narrowed to a specific theology that strongly predicts the evidence, then the evidential conclusion should concern that theology, and the additional commitments required by it must themselves be evaluated.

This clarification actually strengthens the central criticism rather than weakening it. The argument is not that theism must be uniquely complete or immune to revision before it can enter evidential reasoning. The demand is more modest: the theory should be sufficiently stable that its successful predictions cannot be generated merely by choosing different versions of it after the relevant evidence has been identified.

The same applies to divine psychology. It is reasonable to infer some divine preferences from a prior conception of perfect goodness. What is methodologically problematic is inferring whichever preference happens to make the current observation likely. If perfect goodness independently implies that God values rational agency, embodiment, or psychophysical coherence, then those claims may legitimately generate expectations. But they should also constrain what the defender can say when the world appears inconsistent with those values.

The resulting standard is therefore not hostile to natural theology. In fact, it describes what a stronger natural theology would need to look like. The defender would begin with a comparatively unified account of God, value, mind, embodiment, and divine action, and then ask what worlds that account makes more or less probable. The resulting predictions would be allowed to succeed or fail together.

Under that model, the tension between fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony would not necessarily be fatal. A sufficiently developed metaphysics might indeed explain why complex physical organization is required for sophisticated cognition while phenomenal character remains contingent enough for psychophysical harmony to be surprising. But the justification for that distinction would have to come from the metaphysical theory itself, rather than from the fact that the distinction happens to preserve both apologetic arguments.

The burden of argument is therefore shifted rather than eliminated. It is not enough to show that some reconciliation between fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony can be constructed. The relevant question is whether there are independent reasons to believe the metaphysical framework that performs the reconciliation. If there are, then the apparent tension may represent genuine theoretical refinement. If there are not, then the reconciliation risks being little more than a carefully engineered accommodation.

This distinction is essential to the broader critique. The problem with metaphysical cherry-picking is not that philosophers revise their views. It is that revisions can become epistemically cheap when they are unconstrained by commitments outside the particular argument being defended. A theory earns explanatory force not merely by being able to fit the world, but by taking enough antecedent risks that its successful fit is informative.

## XII. Conclusion

Neil Sinhababu’s “electrons in love” objection is valuable because it exposes a tension that is easy to miss when fine-tuning and psychophysical harmony are considered separately. Fine-tuning gains force from the idea that sophisticated conscious life depends upon a narrow range of physically complex conditions. Psychophysical harmony gains force from the idea that the relation between physical organization and phenomenal consciousness could have been radically different. The more freedom one grants to the psychophysical mapping, the less obvious it becomes that life-permitting physical constants were necessary for the existence of minds. The more tightly one constrains mentality by physical structure, the less surprising the actual psychophysical mapping appears.

The point is not that the two arguments are logically inconsistent. A sufficiently developed metaphysics may distinguish between the physical conditions required for sophisticated cognition and the psychophysical conditions determining phenomenal character. It may also provide independent reasons for thinking that God would prefer embodied, law-governed, physically complex creatures over radically simpler conscious systems. But once these distinctions are introduced, the evidential case no longer rests on fine-tuning or psychophysical harmony alone. It rests on a deeper and substantially more specific metaphysical and theological framework.

That is precisely where the broader methodological problem emerges. If the auxiliary assumptions required by one argument differ from those required by another, then the cumulative case cannot simply treat both as independent evidence for an undifferentiated hypothesis called “theism.” The defender must first show that the assumptions belong to a single coherent model. Otherwise, one risks selecting metaphysical commitments locally, according to whichever configuration makes the current argument most successful.

The problem is therefore one of **global model coherence**. It is not enough that some version of theism can explain fine-tuning and some version can explain psychophysical harmony. Nor is it enough that theism can be supplemented with whatever additional commitments are necessary to accommodate each datum after the fact. A genuinely cumulative case requires a single sufficiently specified theory under which the relevant observations are jointly expected.

The appropriate evidential question is therefore not merely:

$$
P(E_i\mid T_i),
$$

for a sequence of individually favorable theistic models. It is closer to:

$$
P(E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_n\mid T^*),
$$

where $$T^*$$ is a stable and independently motivated theological and metaphysical framework.

This requirement also clarifies the importance of divine psychology. Once theism is specified strongly enough to generate predictions, it necessarily includes assumptions about what God values, what kinds of beings God would create, and what means God would prefer for realizing those goods. These assumptions cannot be invoked only when they produce favorable likelihoods and then abandoned when they generate difficulties. If a theory of divine motivation is doing the predictive work, then that same theory must also bear the evidential costs of its less favorable implications.

The same point applies to the broader structure of philosophical apologetics. A cumulative case is not strengthened merely by multiplying arguments. If each argument relies on a different auxiliary theology, a different metaphysics, or a different degree of confidence about divine motives, then the apparent convergence may be largely rhetorical. What looks like many independent confirmations of one theory may instead be many locally optimized explanations sharing only a very general conclusion.

This is why the distinction between explanation and prediction is essential. A highly flexible framework may be capable of explaining almost any observation after it occurs. But the very flexibility that makes post hoc accommodation easy can weaken the evidential significance of the explanation. A theory is most informative when its commitments constrain what should be expected and expose it to the possibility of failure.

The methodological ideal is therefore straightforward:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Specify the theory first, then ask what it predicts.}
}
$$

This does not require a fully complete theology or metaphysics before any evidential reasoning can begin. It requires only that the assumptions doing the explanatory work be made explicit, independently defended where possible, and applied consistently across different arguments. Where those assumptions generate tradeoffs, the tradeoffs should be counted rather than eliminated through selective reformulation.

Seen in this light, the significance of Sinhababu’s objection extends well beyond cosmological fine-tuning. “Electrons in love” functions as a diagnostic case. It reveals how quickly the evidential force of an argument can depend upon background metaphysical assumptions that are rarely stated in the argument itself. Once those assumptions are made explicit, apparent pieces of cumulative evidence may begin to pull against one another.

The deeper challenge for natural theology is therefore not merely to produce arguments that can each be made compatible with theism. It is to demonstrate that those arguments arise from one stable and independently justified conception of God, mind, value, and creation. Without that coherence, the cumulative case risks collapsing into metaphysical cherry-picking: the theory changes wherever necessary, while the conclusion remains fixed. At that point, what appears to be explanatory power may instead be the product of theoretical flexibility.

Sinhababu’s tension thus exposes a broader epistemic question at the heart of philosophical apologetics: whether theism is being treated as a genuine predictive hypothesis, or as a conclusion around which a sufficiently adaptable metaphysical framework is constructed. Until that question is answered, the existence of many arguments for theism should not by itself be mistaken for a unified cumulative case.
