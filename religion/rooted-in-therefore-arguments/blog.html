Rooted-In Therefore Arguments

I am sick of bad arguments. So much so, that it's distracting me from other productive things I could be doing. It's not so much bad arguments per se, but the way they get repeated ad nauseum and proliferate throughout the masses of people who fail to see the weaknesses or simply don't want to acknowledge them. What bothers me most is the way these arguments are wielded while discussing contentious topics: as if they are beyond reasonable doubt and therefore confirm the broader ideology from which they came.

One example that repeatedly bothers me among certain conservatives and Christian nationalists is the claim that Enlightenment values are somehow "rooted in the Bible," despite Enlightenment thinkers frequently criticizing, revising, or explicitly rejecting biblical principles and ecclesiastical authority. The usual formulation is that "the Bible" or "Christianity provided the seeds for these principles to blossom." To me, that is hilarious, because it is exactly the kind of rhetoric that understates the actual total evidence. The claim that Enlightenment values—individual liberty, secular governance, scientific rationalism, freedom of conscience, and related ideals—are simply "rooted in the Bible" or "sprung from Christianity" often functions as a post-hoc rationalization that ignores the antagonism between many Enlightenment thinkers and Christian orthodoxy. On its face, the argument is nonsense. As I'll show, the same logic can just as easily be used to argue that Christianity planted the seeds for later fascist movements.

The deeper issue, however, is not that Christianity had no influence whatsoever on European modernity. After more than a millennium of institutional and intellectual dominance in Europe, it would be extraordinary if Christianity had contributed nothing to the conceptual environment from which the Enlightenment emerged. The problem is the slide from that weak and obvious proposition to much stronger claims: that Christianity generated liberal values, that those values are fundamentally Christian, or that they could not have emerged without Christianity. Those are very different historical claims, and they require very different levels of evidence.

Core thesis: vague genealogical language such as rooted in, sprang from, or planted the seeds for compresses several distinct causal claims into one flattering metaphor. Once those claims are separated and subjected to ordinary standards of historical causation, the argument becomes much weaker—and, in its strongest forms, often collapses.

Part I The Claim and the Problem Before arguing about whether Enlightenment values are “Christian,” we need to separate several different historical claims that are often collapsed into the same metaphor.

What is actually being claimed?

The expression "Christianity planted the seeds of the Enlightenment" sounds modest, but it is usually doing more work than it appears to. Sometimes it means only that Enlightenment thinkers lived in societies already shaped by Christian institutions. Sometimes it means that certain Christian concepts were among many ingredients later transformed into modern ideas. Sometimes it means that Enlightenment values are genealogically Christian. And sometimes the rhetoric becomes explicitly counterfactual: without Christianity, there would have been no liberal democracy, no human rights, no freedom of conscience, or no modern secular order.

These propositions are not interchangeable. Cultural background is not the same thing as conceptual origin; conceptual origin is not the same thing as causal necessity; and causal contribution is not the same thing as ideological ownership. Much of the rhetorical force of the "seed" metaphor comes from allowing the speaker to move among these claims without ever specifying which one is being defended.

Four claims that should be kept separate:
  • Influence: X affected Y in some identifiable way.
  • Contribution: X was one among several causes or enabling conditions that helped produce Y.
  • Genealogy: some components of Y developed historically through X or through institutions shaped by X.
  • Necessity: without X, Y probably would not have emerged, or would have emerged in a substantially different form.

These claims become progressively stronger. Evidence for influence does not automatically establish contribution; contribution does not automatically establish genealogy; and none of those automatically establishes necessity.

The historical friction the metaphor hides

Figures such as Voltaire, Diderot, Hume, Jefferson, and Paine were often sharply critical of organized religion, particularly Christianity as practiced by established churches. Voltaire mocked the Church relentlessly—Écrasez l'infâme!, "crush the infamous thing." Thomas Paine, in The Age of Reason, ridiculed Christian doctrine. David Hume argued against miracles and questioned religion's epistemic foundations. John Locke was more religious than many of these figures, but still argued for toleration and political arrangements that limited ecclesiastical coercion. The point is not that every Enlightenment thinker was anti-Christian; they plainly were not. The point is that the intellectual movement cannot be described honestly as the uncomplicated maturation of biblical principles.

Enlightenment thought developed through a dense historical matrix: Renaissance humanism, the recovery and reinterpretation of classical Greek and Roman texts, the Scientific Revolution, Reformation and post-Reformation conflict, new commercial and political institutions, wars of religion, challenges to monarchical authority, and disputes within Christianity itself. It also developed in direct response to centuries of censorship, confessional coercion, inquisitorial institutions, witch persecutions, and political theocracy. If Enlightenment values were already intrinsic to Christian doctrine in anything like their later political form, the obvious historical questions remain: why did they emerge so late? Why were advocates of toleration, heterodoxy, scientific inquiry, and religious dissent so often persecuted, exiled, censored, or killed by Christian authorities? It is absurd to imagine a belief system "planting seeds" that require roughly seventeen centuries to blossom while its institutions repeatedly suppress people trying to cultivate those very ideas.

The "seed" metaphor therefore has a built-in asymmetry. Positive developments are retrospectively treated as latent Christian achievements, while authoritarian theocracies, divine-right monarchies, crusades, religious wars, anti-scientific persecution, witch trials, heresy hunts, colonialism justified through missionary work, and Christian antisemitism are treated as aberrations or historical accidents. But if mere presence in the cultural soil qualifies as a seed, then these developments are just as eligible for Christian ancestry as liberal democracy is—often more directly so.

Christianity also contains doctrines and institutional traditions emphasizing hierarchy, obedience, moral absolutism, submission to divine authority, and the priority of divine command over individual judgment. Those features can be linked to later authoritarian ideologies at least as easily as Christian dignity language can be linked to liberalism. Enlightenment values, by contrast, frequently emphasize autonomy rather than obedience, rational criticism rather than faith, toleration rather than exclusivist truth claims, and human rights that cannot simply be overridden by divine command. Some Christians later reinterpreted their faith in light of these values—liberal theology is an obvious example—but reinterpretation under pressure is not the same thing as being their original source.

To test the argument fairly, however, it is not enough to attack the weakest formulation. The next step is to reconstruct the strongest plausible version from the Christian advocate's perspective.

Part II The Strongest Version of the Christian-Origin Argument Steelman the claim first: what would an intelligent advocate actually point to, and what hidden inferences are required to reach the conclusion?

A charitable reconstruction

Let's be charitable and construct the argument in premise form from the advocate's perspective. The goal is to show the best supporting evidence available, identify the hidden assumptions connecting that evidence to the conclusion, and only then evaluate the argument's actual strength.

Premise Evidence an advocate might offer Inference required
P1. Imago Dei implies inherent human dignity and equality. Genesis 1:27 declares that humans are created in the image of God. Christian theologians such as Augustine, Aquinas, and Calvin have been read as grounding intrinsic dignity in this doctrine. Modern Christian rights theorists such as Jacques Maritain explicitly connected Christian anthropology to universal human rights. Theological equality before God must be relevantly continuous with political equality and universal rights.
P2. Christianity developed moral universals involving equality of souls, accountability, and conscience. Galatians 3:28 declares that there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, for all are one in Christ. Luther's "priesthood of all believers" can be construed as a seed for egalitarianism. Individual judgment before God can be linked to personal conscience and moral autonomy. Religious equality, personal accountability, and conscience must develop into secular egalitarianism and political autonomy rather than remaining concepts internal to salvation or church life.
P3. Christian concepts and institutions laid groundwork for rights, liberty, and democracy. Protestant reformers encouraged literacy and education, supporting individual inquiry. John Locke invoked Christian ideas while defending natural rights. Protestant resistance theory, including works such as Vindiciae contra tyrannos, prefigured later arguments for resistance to tyranny. These institutions and arguments must be more than antecedents or contributors; they must be sufficiently formative to justify treating later liberalism as rooted in Christianity.
P4. The Enlightenment arose inside historically Christian societies. The Enlightenment occurred largely in post-Reformation Europe rather than in Confucian, Hindu, or Islamic civilizations. Many Enlightenment thinkers were educated in Christian institutions even when they later rebelled. Christian theology had dominated European intellectual vocabulary for centuries, so new ideas inevitably had to engage it. Cultural embeddedness must amount to causal dependence rather than mere context, opposition, or one influence among many.
C. Therefore Enlightenment values are rooted in biblical Christianity and could not have emerged without it. The preceding considerations are combined into a cumulative genealogy: Christian anthropology, conscience, literacy, resistance theory, and long-term cultural formation supposedly make liberal modernity intelligible. The evidence must establish not merely contact or contribution but a sufficiently strong genealogical and counterfactual dependence to support the conclusion.
If X laid the foundation for Y, and Y developed from X, then Y is rooted in X.

The hidden assumptions

The argument does not usually claim that the Bible explicitly teaches modern liberal democracy. Instead, it claims that preconditions such as moral equality, dignity, personal conscience, literacy, and resistance to tyranny were incubated within Christian theology and culture, such that centuries of Christian formation made Enlightenment ideals possible. That softer wording is more plausible—but it still requires several hidden assumptions.

  • HA1: Cultural continuity means causal dependence: if Enlightenment thinkers lived in Christian cultures, their ideas must derive substantially from Christian doctrine.
  • HA2: The presence of a concept in both Christianity and Enlightenment thought implies a formative historical relationship rather than convergence, reinterpretation, or coincidence.
  • HA3: Positive Enlightenment values could not have emerged independently, or through non-Christian intellectual sources, in anything like their historical form.
  • HA4: Earlier Christian thinkers and institutions gradually developed ideas that evolved into Enlightenment concepts rather than merely using superficially similar language for importantly different concepts.
  • HA5: Opposition to churches and Christian authority does not negate deeper dependence on Christian metaphysics or institutions.
  • HA6: Christian contributions were sufficiently important relative to Greek philosophy, Roman law, Renaissance humanism, scientific inquiry, political conflict, commercial development, and other causes to justify giving Christianity privileged genealogical status.

None of these assumptions is self-evidently absurd. Some may be true in specific places, periods, or intellectual lineages. The problem is that the conclusion becomes much stronger than the premises unless these bridging claims are independently demonstrated. The fact that Locke cites Christian texts, for example, is evidence of influence; it is not by itself evidence that liberal natural-rights theory was impossible without Christianity.

The easiest way to expose how permissive this reasoning is, is to hold the inferential structure constant while changing the conclusion. If the method can generate equally plausible genealogies toward outcomes its advocates reject, then the method has weak discriminatory power.

Part III The Symmetry Test A good causal rule should discriminate between genuine and spurious genealogies. If the same loose rule supports incompatible or ideologically inconvenient lineages, the rule itself is suspect.
The symmetry test: if an inference rule can be used with comparable plausibility to derive genealogies that point in radically different directions, that does not automatically prove every conclusion false. It does show that the inference rule, by itself, has very little power to establish which genealogy is actually causal, distinctive, or necessary.

Symmetry Test 1: Christianity → Fascism

Let's construct a parallel argument using the same logical structure, assumptions, and rhetorical framing, but this time concluding that Christianity laid the foundation for fascism. The point is not to claim that Christianity intended fascism. The original argument does not claim Christianity consciously intended liberalism either. The point is to expose how ambiguous causal "seed" language can justify dramatically different ideological lineages with equal surface plausibility.

  1. The Bible and Christian tradition promote hierarchy, obedience to authority, and divinely ordained order.
    • Romans 13:1–7 instructs believers to be subject to governing authorities because authority is established by God.
    • The Church developed strict hierarchies—popes, bishops, priests, laity—that can be compared structurally with authoritarian political systems.
    • The Divine Right of Kings was justified through Christian theology: political authority came from God rather than the people.
  2. Christianity historically fused religion with state power, suppressing dissent and enforcing moral and ideological conformity.
    • The Inquisition, the Index of Forbidden Books, and suppression of heresies—including campaigns against groups such as the Cathars and later confessional persecution of Protestants or Catholics in different regimes—institutionalized religious conformity.
    • Byzantine symphonia, Catholic monarchies, and later Lutheran state churches exemplified different forms of throne-and-altar integration.
    • Witch hunts, persecution of Jews, confessional repression, and censorship demonstrate sustained institutional hostility to ideological plurality.
  3. These doctrines and institutions normalized authoritarianism and subordinated individual conscience to higher authority.
    • Christian moral systems often demand obedience to divine law over personal judgment.
    • Church institutions historically disciplined not only action but belief and speech, providing a model of comprehensive normative regulation.
    • Augustine's City of God places human history within a cosmic drama of obedience and rebellion, allowing dissent to be moralized as sin.
  4. Fascist regimes drew on analogous structures: rigid hierarchy, sacralized authority, collectivist identity, ritual, and suppression of pluralism.
    • Fascism valorizes strong leaders, sacred tradition, discipline, national unity, and obedience.
    • Mussolini and Franco embraced Catholic imagery and institutions in different ways; Hitler's government negotiated a concordat with the Catholic Church.
    • Fascist aesthetics of ritual, unity, discipline, sacred community, and moral absolutism can be made to look culturally consonant with centuries of Christian ceremonialism and hierarchy.
  5. Therefore, Christianity laid the cultural and ideological groundwork for the development of fascist ideologies.

The same assumptions reappear

  • HA1: Cultural continuity implies causal influence.
  • HA2: Conceptual or structural resemblance implies ideological descent.
  • HA3: Values internal to a tradition may evolve in unintended directions.
  • HA4: Long-term institutional structures condition later political developments.

Again, this argument does not claim Christianity intended fascism. It claims that Christianity normalized authoritarian structures and sacralized political authority, making later authoritarian formations more culturally intelligible when they appeared. Structurally, the reasoning is the same: both arguments rely on historical arcs, cultural embeddedness, selective conceptual resemblance, and long-term institutional conditioning. That makes them equally vulnerable to selective historical emphasis, oversimplification of complex traditions, and neglect of competing causes or mediating factors.

Symmetry Test 2: Earlier Traditions → Christianity

Now take the "seed" argument to its logical conclusion and turn it against Christianity itself. If ideological descent is established by loose continuities, indirect influence, conceptual resemblance, and long-term cultural proximity, then Christianity was not a wholly original religious system appearing in a vacuum. It developed within Jewish, Near Eastern, Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman worlds that already contained many concepts later reworked by Christians.

The broad proposition here is not fringe: historians of ancient Judaism and Christianity routinely study these religions within the wider cultural environments in which they developed. The strength of particular proposed lines of influence varies. Some relationships are well documented through language, institutions, texts, conquest, migration, and intellectual contact; others—especially broad comparisons involving mystery cults or "dying-and-rising gods"—are more disputed and should not be treated as equally secure. That qualification matters, because the whole point of this essay is to demand more than resemblance.

  1. P1: Ancient Mediterranean, Persian, Egyptian, and Mesopotamian religions contained ideas and narrative structures later found in Jewish and Christian traditions.
    • Zoroastrianism: traditions involving cosmic conflict between good and evil—classically associated with Ahura Mazda and Angra Mainyu—along with final judgment, resurrection of the dead, heaven and hell, and eschatological renewal have often been discussed in relation to developments in post-exilic Jewish apocalyptic thought. Direct lines of influence remain debated in their details, but the period after the Babylonian exile, when Jewish communities came under Persian rule, provides a concrete historical setting in which influence is possible.
    • Hellenistic mystery religions and cults: traditions associated with Dionysus, Mithras/Mithraism, and other Mediterranean cults included initiation rites, sacred or communal meals, ritual purification, spiritual rebirth language, and divine-savior motifs. These have often been compared with baptism, Eucharistic meals, resurrection, and salvation in Christianity. Claims that Christianity simply borrowed sacramental structures or "dying-and-rising god" myths from Dionysian, Mithraic, or similar cults are contested, so the parallels are evidence to investigate rather than proof of descent.
    • Egyptian traditions: the Osiris complex includes death, restoration or resurrection, judgment, and salvation motifs that have long attracted comparison, although comparison alone does not prove Christian dependence.
    • Mesopotamian traditions: flood narratives, divine kingship, law associated with the gods, covenantal forms, and cosmic ordering appear in Sumerian and Akkadian texts centuries before Israelite religion and Christianity. The story of Noah has obvious literary parallels with earlier Mesopotamian flood traditions such as those preserved in the Epic of Gilgamesh, while codes such as Hammurabi's provide an older Near Eastern context for law presented under divine authority.
  2. P2: Judaism, the direct predecessor of Christianity, evolved through periods of exile, conquest, Persian rule, Hellenization, and Roman domination.
    • Exilic and post-exilic Judaism developed amid Babylonian and later Persian imperial contexts, during which Jewish eschatological and angelological traditions changed substantially.
    • Scholars have long debated possible Persian influence on Jewish apocalypticism, resurrection, angelology, demonology, and eschatology.
    • After Alexander, Greek language, philosophy, literary forms, and political institutions permeated the eastern Mediterranean. Philo of Alexandria provides an explicit example of Jewish scripture being interpreted through Hellenistic philosophical categories.
  3. P3: Early Christianity inherited and reinterpreted Jewish apocalyptic traditions while operating inside a Hellenistic and Roman intellectual world.
    • The Gospels and Paul's letters participate in Greco-Roman literary and rhetorical environments while reworking Jewish messianism.
    • Jesus is presented using categories intelligible within Jewish and wider Mediterranean traditions of divine agency, kingship, salvation, sacrifice, exaltation, and—in comparative scholarship—divine-savior figures. Similarity to Greco-Roman savior figures does not itself demonstrate borrowing, but it places early Christian claims inside a broader Mediterranean religious vocabulary.
    • Later Christian theology uses Greek metaphysical vocabulary such as logos and ousia.
    • The Logos language of John 1 is especially intelligible against Jewish Wisdom traditions and Hellenistic philosophical usage. It is often discussed alongside Stoic and Platonic uses of logos and, especially, the work of Philo of Alexandria, a Hellenized Jewish thinker who explicitly interpreted Hebrew scripture through Greek philosophical categories.
  4. P4: Christianity therefore emerged as a historically syncretic and adaptive product of Jewish tradition operating within Persian, Hellenistic, Roman, and older Near Eastern cultural matrices.
    • Christianity inherited Jewish monotheism and scripture while developing doctrines in interaction with apocalyptic, philosophical, political, and ritual environments that predated it.
    • The strongest version of this claim distinguishes confidence levels: Jewish monotheism and scripture are direct inheritance; proposed Zoroastrian contributions to eschatology are plausible but debated in degree; Hellenistic philosophical influence is demonstrable in many later formulations; and claims of direct "mystery cult sacramentality" require much more caution than simple motif matching.
    • Those inherited and transformed materials helped give Christianity its distinctive shape.
  5. C: Thus, these earlier traditions laid the groundwork or “planted the seeds” for the emergence of Christianity.

The analogous assumptions

  • HA1: Cultural transmission over time implies ideological dependence.
  • HA2: Similarities in doctrine or myth suggest inheritance.
  • HA3: No religious or philosophical system arises in a vacuum.
  • HA4: Foundational ideas can persist through indirect transmission.

This applies the same rhetorical structure used to claim Christianity birthed the Enlightenment or fascism. If deep cultural influence and shared themes over time are sufficient to establish descent or "seeding," then Christianity was itself seeded by older traditions. In several cases the argument is stronger than the loose Christianity-to-Enlightenment analogy because chronological and geographical proximity are tighter and actual transmission mechanisms—imperial rule, diaspora, Hellenization, bilingual intellectual culture, and textual reuse—can be specified. But even here, resemblance alone is not enough. The stronger cases are stronger precisely because they can move beyond resemblance toward documented contact, mediation, transformation, and textual evidence.

What the symmetry test shows

The point is not that all three genealogies are equally true. It is almost the opposite: a method that treats them as equally supportable on the basis of resemblance and cultural continuity is too weak to tell us which relationships are historically important. The symmetry test undermines the uniqueness of the "seed" inference. To discriminate among these cases, we need a better account of causation.

Once the examples are stripped away, the underlying reasoning is remarkably simple. That simplicity is exactly what makes the metaphor rhetorically powerful and historically dangerous.

Part IV What Is Wrong With the “Seed” Argument? The problem is not one bad historical example. It is a generic inference pattern that repeatedly mistakes resemblance, context, and contact for robust causal explanation.

The generic form

X contains ideas A, B, C → later Y contains A′, B′, C′ → therefore X “planted the seeds” for Y.

All three formulations—Christianity → Enlightenment, Christianity → Fascism, and earlier traditions → Christianity—can be made to fit this structure. The argument usually relies on five hidden assumptions: similarity implies lineage or causation; temporal or cultural proximity implies influence; institutional continuity implies ideological inheritance; the absence of X would imply the absence of Y; and X's core ideas are necessary, sufficient, or at least unusually important causes of Y's emergence.

Five recurring errors

1. Resemblance is not genealogy

The first premise is usually some version of "X had ideas that resemble Y." But similarity is not causality. Many belief systems independently develop analogous ideas because human beings repeatedly confront similar problems. The golden rule, afterlife beliefs, flood narratives, moral reciprocity, hierarchy, and concepts of justice can emerge in more than one culture without a single line of descent. Convergent cultural evolution is real.

Even genuine historical parallels do not tell us what kind of relationship exists. A Christian doctrine of "equality before God" is not automatically the same concept as political egalitarianism; religious freedom from sin is not the same concept as political liberty; and a "kingdom" in apocalyptic theology is not equivalent to a modern state. Ideas can share words or structures while inhabiting radically different conceptual universes.

2. Contact is not adoption

The second move is often "X transmitted ideas to an intermediary system." But transmission does not imply adoption. Cultures encounter, resist, reinterpret, hybridize, suppress, and selectively absorb foreign ideas. Judaism under Persian and Greek rule did not simply become Zoroastrian or Greek; Christianity in a Roman-Hellenistic environment defined itself against polytheism even while using Greek language and philosophy; Enlightenment writers formed in Christian societies could use Christian vocabulary while attacking Christian institutions.

Cultural interaction is therefore not a straight pipeline. A serious genealogy needs to identify what was transmitted, by whom, through which texts or institutions, and how the receiving tradition changed it.

3. Context is not causal dependence

The third move is "Y developed in a context shaped by X." This is where a post-hoc error easily appears. Existing inside a historical environment does not mean being caused by its dominant ideology. Development can occur because of a tradition, through a tradition, despite a tradition, or in direct opposition to it. Enlightenment thinkers lived in Christian Europe; Christianity emerged in a Roman-Hellenistic world. Neither fact, by itself, tells us the direction or strength of the causal relationship.

4. Influence is not necessity

Even when influence is established, the conclusion frequently inflates it into necessity: without Christianity there would be no human rights; without Greek metaphysics there would be no Christian theology; without Persian religion there would be no Jewish apocalypticism. These claims require counterfactual evidence that ordinary contact or resemblance does not supply. Historical causes are usually contributory or enabling rather than fully necessary or sufficient, and similar outcomes may be reachable through multiple causal paths.

5. Genealogy is not identity

Finally, even genuine descent does not imply that the later concept remains essentially the earlier concept. Intellectual history is full of rupture, contradiction, inversion, and reinvention. A later movement can inherit vocabulary from an earlier tradition while transforming its meaning so extensively that treating the later idea as merely the mature form of the earlier one is misleading. This is why arguments about "roots" are especially prone to understating rupture.

Claim Generic defeater
Similarity = causation Correlation and resemblance do not establish lineage; convergent development is possible.
Proximity = influence Contact does not establish adoption; resistance and selective reinterpretation matter.
Historical context = source Development can occur despite, against, or through transformation of a dominant tradition.
Doctrinal overlap = descent Concepts mutate across traditions; verbal continuity can mask conceptual discontinuity.
Foundational = necessary Multiple historical paths can produce similar outcomes; necessity requires counterfactual support.

A burden-of-proof ladder

One useful way to expose overreach is to force the claimant to climb a burden-of-proof ladder rather than leap from resemblance to civilizational ownership. Each step requires more evidence than the one before it.

  1. Antecedence: Did X actually precede Y?
  2. Resemblance: Are the relevant concepts genuinely analogous, rather than merely sharing vocabulary?
  3. Contact: Could the people or institutions responsible for Y have encountered X?
  4. Transmission: Is there evidence that the relevant ideas were actually transmitted?
  5. Adoption: Did the later actors explicitly or implicitly use those ideas?
  6. Transformation: How were the ideas altered, resisted, combined, or reinterpreted?
  7. Causal contribution: Did X materially affect the development of Y?
  8. Counterfactual importance: Would Y have been substantially less likely, or importantly different, without X?
  9. Comparative importance: Was X more causally important than competing explanations?
Most loose "Christian roots" arguments can establish something near steps 1–3 and then rhetorically jump to steps 8–9. That is the central overreach.

We can honestly make this argument better. There is something to it, but when grounded in a vague metaphorical concept such as "seed," it is pathetically weak. Historical explanation needs mechanisms, counterfactuals, competing causes, and evidence of actual transmission.

Part V What a Serious Historical Causal Argument Requires Replace metaphor with causal inference: identify mechanisms, define concepts, trace lineages, compare alternatives, and state the limits of the claim.

Four families of causal criteria

The original list of requirements can be organized into four larger questions. This keeps the method readable without sacrificing any of the criteria.

A. Establish an actual causal relationship

  1. Causal necessity, sufficiency, or contribution: show what kind of cause X is supposed to be. If Christianity had never existed, would liberal democracy have become highly improbable? If not, perhaps Christianity was an enabling or contributory cause rather than a necessary one. Most historical causes are neither fully necessary nor fully sufficient.
  2. Counterfactual sensitivity: a causal explanation should change the expected outcome under a plausible counterfactual. Without a proposed influence, would Y emerge differently, later, or with lower probability? For example, if Zoroastrian apocalyptic dualism significantly influenced later Jewish eschatology, then a serious account should say what we would expect Jewish eschatology to look like without that contact rather than merely noting resemblance.
  3. Temporal proximity and timing: causes must line up with the period in which the relevant transformation occurs. Watch for anachronism and retroactive projection of later meanings into earlier texts.
  4. Asymmetry of influence: a causal claim should have direction. If every A → B claim can be mirrored by an equally good B-like or contrary genealogy without changing the evidentiary standard, the original inference is probably vacuous.

B. Show the mechanism

  1. Mechanistic clarity and specificity: identify how the ideas moved. Which actors, texts, schools, institutions, policies, educational systems, or political events transmitted and transformed them?
  2. Intermediate variables and causal chains: trace multi-step sequences rather than asserting X → Y. For example: Greek metaphysical vocabulary → Hellenized Jewish thinkers such as Philo → Christian Logos discourse → later Christological formulations.
  3. Track intellectual lineages: use explicit references, citations, schools, networks, and institutional pathways. Locke cites Hooker and the Bible, but also engages Hobbes, natural-law traditions, political conflict, and reason. Deconstruct abstract ideas into components and trace their transformations: for example, Christian freedom as freedom from sin can be distinguished from Reformation-era liberty of conscience and, later, Enlightenment political liberty. A serious genealogy follows those mixtures and semantic changes rather than assigning one parent.
  4. Textual and doctrinal evidence: rely on primary sources where possible. Which thinkers explicitly invoked earlier doctrines? Which rejected them? Which terms can be shown changing meaning across identifiable texts?

C. Define what was actually transmitted

  1. Disentangle conceptual content: do not lump together abstract "values." Christian human dignity is not automatically Kantian autonomy; spiritual freedom is not automatically political liberty; equality of souls is not automatically equality before civil law.
  2. Account for contestation: strong historical arguments show that ideas were debated, rejected, distorted, selectively adopted, and transformed. Protestant and Catholic understandings of authority and conscience differed radically. Progress often arises through conflict or rejection rather than smooth inheritance.

D. Bound the claim

  1. Control for multiple causation: intellectual history rarely has a single parent. The Enlightenment involves Greek philosophy, Roman law, Christian theology, Renaissance humanism, the Scientific Revolution, printing, commercial change, confessional conflict, political upheaval, colonial encounter, and more.
  2. Scope conditions and limits: state where the claim applies. Is it regional, temporal, denominational, class-specific, or institutional? Protestant literacy reforms may have shaped parts of Northern European civic culture without explaining Mediterranean political development or every strand of Enlightenment thought.

A stronger reconstruction

Once these standards are imposed, the original claim becomes narrower—but also much more defensible. Instead of saying that Christianity "planted the seeds" of the Enlightenment, we can formulate a non-monocausal claim that specifies mechanisms, allows falsification, acknowledges rupture and contestation, and cannot be mirrored by an equally strong counterclaim without changing the evidence.

A historically responsible reconstruction: Certain strands of Christian thought and particular Christian institutions—such as some Protestant emphases on conscience, literacy, resistance theory, and individual engagement with Scripture—created educational, theological, and political structures that contributed to the development of some Enlightenment values, especially in specific early modern European contexts. Those contributions interacted with classical philosophy, Renaissance humanism, scientific inquiry, commercial and political change, and intra-Christian conflict. Enlightenment ideas also developed through tension with, transformation of, and often direct opposition to Christian authority structures.

That claim is less flattering to apologetic narratives because it gives up exclusivity, necessity, and ownership. But it is better history. It allows Christianity to be both contributor and obstacle; it allows particular Christian concepts to be transformed into ideas their earlier users would not have recognized; and it allows modern liberalism to have multiple causal lineages rather than a single civilizational parent.

Applying the framework to Christianity's predecessors

Using the same framework, we can also see why some claims about Christianity's development from earlier religious and philosophical environments are stronger than a vague "seed" metaphor. The general thesis is not compelling merely because earlier religions contain vaguely similar motifs. It becomes stronger where we can identify temporal and geographical proximity, concrete contact, intermediary traditions, textual evidence, conceptual transformation, and historically plausible transmission.

Criterion Christianity's predecessor influences
Temporal proximity Persian, Hellenistic, Mesopotamian, Jewish, and Roman systems predate Christianity and overlap with the periods in which Second Temple Judaism and early Christianity developed.
Geographical proximity The relevant traditions operated in the same or neighboring regions of the eastern Mediterranean and Near East.
Transmission mechanisms Exile, imperial rule, Hellenistic conquest, Roman administration, diaspora communities, bilingual education, philosophical schools, and scriptural interpretation provide identifiable channels.
Primary textual evidence Texts such as Daniel, 1 Enoch, Philo, the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint, Pauline letters, and Johannine writings can be compared within known historical settings rather than merely by motif.
Conceptual genealogy Resurrection, Satan, judgment, Logos discourse, angelology, apocalypticism, and related concepts can be studied across intermediate Jewish and Hellenistic traditions.
Contestation and adaptation Early Christians did not simply copy surrounding traditions; they rejected, reinterpreted, and recombined inherited Jewish and Greco-Roman concepts.
Intermediate variables Second Temple Judaism is an identifiable cultural and theological conduit between older Near Eastern/Persian/Hellenistic environments and early Christianity.
Falsifiability Specific claims about textual borrowing, linguistic dependence, chronology, or conceptual transmission are open to scholarly dispute and can be strengthened or weakened by evidence.

The general view that Christianity emerged from earlier Jewish and wider ancient traditions is not fringe. The evidence is extensive and multidisciplinary, and many mechanisms of cultural interaction are historically traceable. But precision still matters: not every proposed parallel is equally strong, and a claim does not become sound merely because it points in a direction I happen to find persuasive. The method has to be symmetrical.

Once the causal standard is made explicit, the dispute changes. The question is no longer whether Christianity was "present" somewhere in the background, but what exactly it contributed, through which mechanisms, relative to what alternatives, and with what counterfactual importance.

Part VI What Follows, and Why the Story Persists First settle the historical inference. Only then ask the separate rhetorical question: why is this particular genealogy so attractive and persistent?

The historical conclusion

For complex historical claims like the main argument, it is crucial to consider the total evidence. By narrowly selecting a subset of the evidence and combining it with a permissive argument structure, we can construct conflicting genealogies and leave ourselves at an impasse. A stronger method forces us to distinguish influence from contribution, contribution from genealogy, and genealogy from necessity. It asks for mechanisms, mediation, counterfactuals, competing causes, and conceptual change.

Once that standard is applied, the original strong claim—that modern secular liberal values are fundamentally biblical, uniquely Christian, or impossible without Christianity—is embarrassingly ridiculous. A weaker claim survives: some Christian ideas, institutions, conflicts, reforms, and educational structures contributed to some developments that later became part of liberal modernity. But Christianity was also an object of resistance, a source of coercive institutions, and itself an evolving product of earlier intellectual and religious worlds. None of this supports the apologetic conclusion that secular modernity is simply Christianity in disguise.

The broader lesson: intellectual history does not have single parents. Traditions can be causes, obstacles, conduits, adversaries, vocabularies, and raw materials at the same time. A movement may develop partly from a tradition and partly against it. Historical causation is a network of transformation, not a family tree with one rightful owner.

Why the narrative remains attractive

None of the foregoing depends on the motives of the people making the argument. Even a completely sincere advocate faces the same evidentiary problems. The logical and historical critique stands on its own. Only after establishing that should we ask a separate question: why do apologists and conservative culture warriors continue to push such a pathetic argument?

And yet, this does not stop people like Jordan Dumbfuck Peterson and apologists from proliferating the argument. The claim that Christianity is the root of secular liberal values is often attractive not because it survives rigorous historical scrutiny, but because it serves several strategic rhetorical and ideological purposes at once. For many conservative Christians and Christian nationalists, modern liberal societies—democracy, human rights, pluralism, secular institutions—can feel ideologically alien or even threatening. Saying "secularism is Christian in origin" allows those societies to be rhetorically repossessed.

First, the argument preserves Christianity's moral authority in a culture that increasingly operates outside its theological framework. Modern liberalism can be redescribed as an extension of Christian civilization rather than a break from it. Secular institutions are then made to owe Christianity a debt: you may have rejected God, but you still live in His house.

Second, in the context of culture-war politics—especially in the United States—the argument functions defensively against secularism, atheism, and progressive ideologies. It blunts criticism by reframing supposedly secular values as Christian values. The religious advocate can then answer criticism with: "We built this culture, and you are erasing it," or "You claim to value freedom, but where do you think it came from?" The genealogy becomes a claim of civilizational ownership.

Third, apologetic and political circles often overlap, so the argument can reinforce believer identity. Christianity is presented not merely as personal faith but as the indispensable historical engine of civilization. That narrative protects believers from the charge that Christianity has been anti-reason, anti-progress, or historically regressive by constructing an opposite story of indispensable contribution: "Without Christ, there is no democracy; there are no human rights."

The Just-So Story

But this is simply a Just-So Story. It fits a pre-existing worldview and explains everything neatly, but it ignores contradictions, cherry-picks evidence, and romanticizes the past rather than analyzing it critically. It is comfortable and flattering to Christian audiences, but historically reductive. It is a strategic narrative—a form of cultural apologetic—that is ideologically motivated, selectively reasoned, and rhetorically loaded rather than empirically demonstrated.

The better conclusion is not that Christianity contributed nothing to the Enlightenment, nor that every counter-genealogy is automatically true. It is that claims of historical "roots" have to earn their causal force. Influence should not be inflated into ownership; resemblance should not be inflated into descent; cultural background should not be inflated into necessity. Once the metaphor is replaced with an actual causal framework, the history becomes more complicated, more conditional, and much more interesting.
