The Market Fundamentalism Feedback Loop

In the first two parts of this blog series, I argued that supply-side economics is theoretically and empirically weak, then examined why the ideology nevertheless persists among the public. This post takes a somewhat more rigorous approach.

I have also alluded to the way supply-side economics is commonly paired with austerity policies. We previously introduced market fundamentalism: the extension of neoliberal market ideology into broader areas of social and political life. For the purposes of this post, I will treat these concepts as equivalent and construct a system-dynamics model that captures some of the causal forces introduced in the previous installment.

1. The infrastructure behind market fundamentalism

We begin with the Atlas Network1 and the State Policy Network (SPN)2, two organizations that function, in effect, as “think tanks for think tanks.” They serve as hubs in a wider network and provide infrastructure for market fundamentalism—not only around narrow tax policy, but also around austerity, neoliberal “reforms,” climate inaction, and libertarian culture-war issues.

Rather than merely producing arguments themselves, these organizations help create and support other organizations by providing instruction, training, funding, and networking. The result is an infrastructure for propagating the same broad ideological package across many institutions and policy domains.

The ideological package

Across the Atlas ecosystem and related conservative and libertarian think tanks, the same bundle of ideas appears repeatedly:

  • Supply-side economics – tax cuts, deregulation, labor “flexibility,” privatization framed as growth engines.
  • Austerity & “fiscal responsibility” – spending caps, balanced-budget rules, welfare and pension cuts, often in the middle of downturns.
  • Neoliberal structural reforms – privatizing utilities and pensions, trade and capital account liberalization, independent central banks plus weakened unions and social protections.
  • Climate inaction / environmental skepticism – from full denial to “delay/derail” strategies that oppose binding regulations.3
  • Libertarian cultural politics – anti-union, school vouchers/charters, “parental choice,” anti-DEI, anti-ESG, hostility to public health mandates, etc.

Atlas Network and State Policy Network

Atlas Network describes its mission as supporting “classically liberal” and “pro-freedom” organizations worldwide and helping them learn how to “win the battle for hearts and minds” on free markets and small government.1 In practice, this extends market fundamentalism beyond conventional economic policy into areas such as pensions, schools, environmental regulation, and culture.

Atlas is a meta-network. Founded in 1981, it now claims ~580–590 partner organizations in 100+ countries.1 Its budget is around $24–28M per year, including $7–8M in grants to partners, while roughly 4,000 people are trained annually through Atlas Network Academy in fundraising, marketing, leadership, and campaign tactics.1

Atlas describes itself as a connector—a “think tank that creates think tanks”—providing seed grants, training, and networking to pro-market organizations around the world.1 Recent research by Vucetic (2024), “Atlas Asunder?,” treats Atlas as a global neoliberal thought network. Analyzing dozens of partner organizations’ outputs from 2017–22, the study finds that they prioritize “free market” causes and often view rising radical-right movements as tactical allies rather than threats.4

Investigative work by Lee Fang has also described Atlas-linked think tanks as deeply involved in Latin American politics, including the training of activists and politicians associated with austerity and privatization agendas and campaigns against left governments in Brazil, Argentina, Bolivia, and elsewhere.5

In the United States, the State Policy Network (SPN) mirrors Atlas at the state level. Formed in 1992 as an outgrowth of Heritage-inspired state groups, it now has 65+ member think tanks across all 50 states, along with hundreds of affiliates.2 Its stated mission is to advance “market-oriented public policy solutions” and “limited government” at the state level.2

SPN generates roughly $25–27M per year in revenue, in addition to tens of millions in combined revenues among affiliates.2 It is closely linked to ALEC, which develops model state legislation, and to major national advocacy organizations such as Americans for Prosperity and Americans for Tax Reform.2

SPN affiliates promote state tax cuts and spending caps, welfare cuts and Medicaid rollbacks, school vouchers, attacks on unions framed as “workplace freedom,” and deregulation in energy, environmental, and labor policy.2 Hertel-Fernandez’s State Capture argues that SPN, ALEC, and Koch-linked groups have reengineered state policy regimes in ways that entrench neoliberal and anti-labor rules.2

2. How the network produces and distributes influence

Network building and organizational replication

From a systems and network perspective, Atlas, SPN, and related organizations function as central hubs in a multilayered graph. Their role is not limited to funding ideas: they also manufacture organizations by seeding and scaling new nodes—the logic behind the phrase “a think tank that creates think tanks.” Grants of a few thousand dollars, combined with training and organizational support, can help launch new think tanks, especially in the Global South, including organizations in Peru, Africa, and Eastern Europe.1

These new nodes import both a shared ideological template—free markets and anti-statism—and a shared organizational playbook built around policy papers, op-eds, index reports, and media outreach. In network terms, this resembles scale-free network growth: a small number of highly connected hubs such as Atlas, SPN, and Koch-related funds accumulate links to many peripheral but ideologically aligned nodes.

Atlas explicitly operates Atlas Network Academy and regional Liberty Forums, where partners receive training in fundraising, marketing, leadership, media engagement, grassroots organizing, and the framing of policy disputes as moral conflicts such as freedom versus statism.1 ABC News has described partners as being trained to “win the battle for hearts and minds.” The activity, in other words, extends well beyond the production of white papers into political communication.1

SPN plays a similar role at the state level, offering training intended to help member think tanks operate more like coordinated business franchises while remaining aligned around school choice, tax cuts, union weakening, and deregulation.2

Expertise and policy production

Conservative think tanks produce what might be called “policy-based evidence” in several forms: short studies supporting tax cuts, deregulation, privatization, and austerity; freedom and economic-freedom indices that encode neoliberal metrics and penalize countries or states with larger public sectors; and climate-skeptic books and reports that manufacture uncertainty about scientific findings.

Dunlap and colleagues identify a strong connection between conservative think tanks and 108 climate-change-denial books, arguing that these publications are important vehicles for attacking mainstream climate science.13 Boussalis and coauthors similarly find that the volume of climate-contrarian content from conservative think tanks has grown sharply, while the messaging has shifted from questioning whether climate change is real toward arguing that policy responses are harmful—a shift consistent with strategies of delaying or weakening regulation.14

Multi-channel diffusion

Information diffuses through several channels—media, politics, movement organizations, and direct communication with the public.

The first is the think tank → media channel: op-eds, press releases, policy briefings, and readily available “experts” who can be quoted by journalists working on deadline, especially within ideological media ecosystems.

A second channel runs from think tanks → politicians and staffers. It includes model legislation, such as bills circulated through ALEC and SPN, as well as policy blueprints such as Heritage’s Mandate for Leadership and Project 2025.9

A third channel connects think tanks → movement organizations and influencers. This includes training activists such as the Free Brazil Movement, Latin American youth organizations, and Tea Party or AFP activists,5 as well as supplying talking points and “studies” to pastors, YouTubers, and movement media.

Finally, there is the think tank → public channel, in which organizations communicate directly through branded concepts such as “Tax Freedom Day,” “economic freedom” rankings, and “welfare dependency” narratives that become part of popular political vocabulary. From a systems perspective, think tanks therefore operate as information hubs with high betweenness centrality: they sit between billionaire donors, politicians, media organizations, and grassroots supporters, shaping the “expert knowledge” that flows among them.

Cultural and institutional diffusion

Market fundamentalism also spreads beyond the narrowly economic domain and into culture and public institutions. McCright, Dunlap, and others describe conservative think tanks as a “denial machine” that transforms a narrower economic skepticism of regulation into full-spectrum environmental skepticism encompassing climate, pollution, and environmental health, often reframed in libertarian language about property rights.3

Atlas has historically helped partner think tanks oppose tobacco controls and, later, vape regulation by aligning corporate interests with generic rhetoric about freedom, consumer choice, and the “nanny state.”1 Climate-denial messaging has also diffused transnationally, moving from U.S. think tanks to the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and beyond through networks such as Atlas and SPN.15

The same logic appears in other policy areas. SPN and Atlas partners promote school vouchers and charters as “choice,” thereby marketizing education;2 healthcare privatization and opposition to Medicaid expansion as a market approach to health;2 and attacks on unions and collective bargaining as “workplace freedom.”2

Anti-DEI and anti-ESG campaigns are similarly reframed as efforts to “protect shareholders” or “keep politics out of business.” This is what Wendy Brown describes as neoliberalism as a political rationality: schools, families, democracy, and identity are increasingly reframed in market terms such as competition, choice, and human capital, with think tanks acting as important engineers of that reframing.

3. From network to feedback system

Two reinforcing mechanisms

MechanismSequence
Wealth → influence → policy → more wealth

From a systems-dynamics perspective, the first mechanism is a classic positive feedback loop: wealth → influence → policy → more wealth. Wealthy donors and corporations fund think tanks and networks; those organizations design and promote policies that increase returns to capital, including tax cuts, deregulation, and weakened labor or environmental rules; those policies further concentrate wealth among the same elites; and the resulting wealth increases the capacity to fund and expand the network.

Narrative → belief → support → capacity

The second mechanism runs through narrative and social reinforcement: narrative → belief → support → capacity. Think tanks generate claims such as “tax cuts grow the economy,” “regulation kills jobs,” or “climate policies hurt the poor.” Those narratives spread through media, churches, schools, and activist networks, shaping public and elite beliefs. Believers then donate, vote, and consume aligned media, increasing both demand for think-tank content and the political clout of aligned politicians. Publicized victories—“we defeated X bill” or “we elected Y”—can further strengthen credibility and in-group loyalty, producing still more support and narrative production.

Epistemic closure and self-reinforcement

Over time, these mechanisms can produce epistemic closure. Within the movement’s information network, in-network “experts” such as Heritage, AEI, and Atlas partners become trusted authorities, while critical scholarship is dismissed as partisan or “globalist.” This is the “apologetics” dynamic noted earlier: the reasoning becomes conclusion-first, community-reinforced, and increasingly self-sealing.

Several structural features help keep the system relatively closed to outside influence:

  • Donor anonymity (via DonorsTrust, etc.) breaks the accountability loop between source and public; you see the message, not the money.11
  • Network density and homophily: Atlas/SPN affiliates mostly cross-reference each other, invite each other to conferences, and publicize each others’ work, increasing perceived “consensus” within the bubble.1
  • Parallel media ecosystem: when your “experts” appear constantly on Fox, talk radio, YouTube, aligned newspapers, there is little incentive for your audience to ever check what, say, the IMF or mainstream econ journals are saying.

In systems terms, this creates a self-reinforcing attractor: once funders, think tanks, politicians, activists, or citizens enter the basin, a combination of incentives and identity can keep them circulating within it even when external evidence conflicts with the core beliefs.

Scale and reach

The scale of the network is broad. A few indicators illustrate its reach:

  • Atlas: ~580–590 partner organizations in 100+ countries; multi-million-dollar grant-making and thousands trained annually (we will look at map plots of this later).1
  • SPN: at least one affiliate in each U.S. state; combined revenues of network orgs in the tens of millions; active on tax, labor, education, welfare, environment.2
  • DonorsTrust / Donors Capital: almost $200M in 2024 alone to 300+ right-wing groups; tens of millions annually for more than a decade prior.10
  • Climate denial: conservative think-tank climate output has grown rapidly, and think-tank-linked denial books form a key spine of the global denial movement.14

Latin America offers a particularly concrete example. Investigations describe Atlas-affiliated think tanks as helping shape party platforms, advise ministers, and coordinate campaigns against left governments using imported “free-market,” anti-corruption, and anti-statist narratives.5 The broader point is that this infrastructure is not marginal; it operates as a globalized ideological supply chain.

With that background in place, we can now build the systems-dynamics model. The next sections define the main variables and then trace the reinforcing (R) and balancing (B) loops through which supply-side and market-fundamentalist ideas spread across think-tank networks such as Atlas, SPN, and the broader Heritage/AEI orbit.

5. Reinforcing loops (the “engine” of propagation)

R1 – Wealth → funding → policy → more wealth

The classic plutocratic growth loop.

StepCausal relationshipMechanism and explanation
1
WEALTH (+) → FUNDING

More concentrated wealth among economic elites creates more surplus resources that can be directed into Atlas-, SPN-, and Heritage-type networks.

2
FUNDING (+) → TT_CAP & INFRA

More funding supports additional staff, stronger communications teams, more training programs, and more organizational spin-offs, including new think tanks, front groups, and student organizations.

3
TT_CAP & INFRA (+) → MF_CONTENT

Greater capacity and infrastructure support a higher volume of reports, op-eds, “indexes,” YouTube content, training sessions, and school curricula.

4
MF_CONTENT (+) → MEDIA_AMP & PERCEIVED_CONSENSUS

Journalists and platforms operating under time pressure can repeatedly turn to readily available “experts.” Repetition across many separately branded organizations can create the illusion of a broad expert consensus around market fundamentalism.

5
MEDIA_AMP & PERCEIVED_CONSENSUS (+) → ELITE_BELIEF & PUBLIC_BELIEF

Politicians, judges, and voters may internalize the idea that “everyone knows” tax cuts and deregulation are the route to growth and freedom.

6
ELITE_BELIEF (+) → POLITICAL_POWER & POLICY_ADOPTION

Market-fundamentalist elites win elections, staff agencies, and implement supply-side, austerity, privatization, and deregulatory policies.

7
POLICY_ADOPTION (+) → INEQUALITY & WEALTH

These policies can increase profits, asset prices, and top-end incomes, further concentrating WEALTH among the same class that funds the network.

8
INEQUALITY (+) → WEALTH

Greater inequality concentrates more wealth that can be converted into political resources, feeding back into FUNDING.

Loop summary

WEALTH → FUNDING → TT_CAP → MF_CONTENT → BELIEFS → POLICY → WEALTH

6. Balancing loops (countervailing forces and potential failure modes)

Even powerful ideological systems face countervailing forces. These balancing loops (B) can slow, constrain, or reverse the expansion of market fundamentalism.

B1 – Empirical reality and policy failure

StepCausal relationshipMechanism and explanation
1
POLICY_ADOPTION (extreme supply-side/austerity) (−) → POLICY_PERFORMANCE

Extreme policy adoption can contribute to deep inequality, fiscal crises, financial crashes, deteriorating public services, and visible climate harms.

2
POLICY_PERFORMANCE (−) → STATE_LEGITIMACY & PUBLIC_BELIEF

As people experience precarity, crises, and state failure, both state legitimacy and belief in the governing ideology can weaken.

3Poor PERFORMANCE (+) → SHOCKS & CRITICAL_PUBLIC_DEBATE

Poor outcomes can generate visible shocks and critical public debate.

4SHOCKS & DEBATE (+) → COUNTER_MVT & CRITICAL_SCHOLARSHIP visibility

Those shocks and debates can create an opening for progressive think tanks, unions, climate and justice movements, and heterodox economists to gain an audience.

5COUNTER_MVT (+) → COMPETING_NARRATIVES (−) → PUBLIC_BELIEF in market fundamentalism

Counter-movements can strengthen competing narratives, weakening public belief in market fundamentalism.

6Lower PUBLIC_BELIEF (−) → POLITICAL_POWER → less POLICY_ADOPTION

Lower public belief can reduce the political power available to sustain further policy adoption.

Loop summary

Extreme POLICY_ADOPTION → bad OUTCOMES → SHOCKS → COUNTER_MOVEMENT → reduced BELIEF → reduced POLICY_ADOPTION

This balancing effect can arrive with a substantial delay. Short-term booms may conceal long-run fragility—as in pre-2008 financialization or pre-crisis austerity—so the feedback from poor outcomes may emerge only after the reinforcing dynamics have operated for some time.

B2 – Credibility and scandal loop

StepCausal relationshipMechanism and explanation
1
MF_CONTENT BIAS & LOW RIGOR (+) → EVIDENCE-REALITY GAP

The more propagandistic the “research” becomes, the larger the gap can grow between its claims and mainstream academic or empirical findings.

2
EVIDENCE-REALITY GAP (+) → EXPOSÉS & REPUTATIONAL HITS

Investigative journalism, leaked documents, and academic critiques can make that evidence-reality gap visible, including through reporting on climate-denial funding or tobacco links.

3
REPUTATIONAL_HITS (+) → CREDIBILITY_LOSS (−) PERCEIVED_CONSENSUS & ELITE_BELIEF

Repeated reputational hits can lead some elites and media organizations to view the think tanks as partisan advocacy shops rather than neutral experts.

4
CREDIBILITY_LOSS (−) → MEDIA_AMP & POLICY_ADOPTION

As credibility falls, media amplification and policy influence can also decline.

Loop summary

Propaganda excess → credibility crisis → reduced influence

This loop may be relatively weak inside a core right-wing media ecosystem that protects its own institutions, but stronger in more pluralistic or technocratic settings such as courts, bureaucracies, and some mainstream media organizations.

B3 – Regulation and transparency loop

StepCausal relationshipMechanism and explanation
1
REVELATIONS about dark money & influence (+) → PUBLIC_CONCERN

Leaks, academic research, and advocacy campaigns can expose donor networks, conflicts of interest, and forms of corporate capture.

2PUBLIC_CONCERN (+) → REGULATORY_PUSH

Public concern can create pressure for disclosure, lobbying restrictions, and campaign-finance rules.

3REGULATORY_PUSH (+) → TRANSPARENCY & FUNDING_CONSTRAINTS (−) → FUNDING

Greater transparency or tighter funding constraints can make anonymous donor-advised funding more difficult and may deter or alter donor behavior.

4
Lower FUNDING (−) → TT_CAP & INFRA → weaker MF_CONTENT & MEDIA_AMP

Lower funding reduces think-tank capacity and movement infrastructure, weakening the production and amplification of market-fundamentalist content.

Loop summary

Exposure → regulation → funding limits → reduced capacity

B4 – Institutional capture → institutional collapse

This balancing mechanism is subtler because it arises when market-fundamentalist policies weaken the institutional foundations on which functioning markets themselves depend.

StepCausal relationshipMechanism and explanation
1
POLICY_ADOPTION (anti-union, anti-public investment, deregulatory) (−) → STATE_CAPACITY

Anti-union, anti-public-investment, and deregulatory policies can degrade public services, regulatory agencies, and civil-service capacity.

2
STATE_CAPACITY (−) → ABILITY_TO_DELIVER_BASIC_GOODS

Weaker state capacity reduces the ability to deliver high-quality schools, infrastructure, healthcare, and disaster response.

3
FAILURE_TO_DELIVER (+) → LEGITIMACY_CRISIS & POLITICAL_VOLATILITY

Persistent failure to deliver basic goods can erode trust not only in “big government,” but in institutions overall, including property rights and the rule of law.

4
VOLATILITY (−) → FAVORABILITY_FOR PREDICTABLE MARKETS

Even many capitalists prefer predictability. Severe volatility can drive investors away or create demand for a different governing model, whether authoritarian or social democratic.

5
DEMAND_FOR_ALTERNATIVES (+) → COUNTER_MVT & POLICY_REORIENTATION (−) POLITICAL_POWER of supply-side ideologues

A legitimacy crisis can therefore increase demand for alternatives and policy reorientation, weakening the political power of supply-side ideologues.

Loop summary

Market fundamentalism undermines the very institutional foundations markets need, eventually reducing its own appeal.

7. Other causal influences

InfluenceVariable(s)Causal pathwayDiagram interpretation

Religious / moral framing

RELIGIOUS_ALIGNMENT

MF_CONTENT → RELIGIOUS_ALIGNMENT (via Christian free enterprise narratives) → IDENTITY_ALIGNMENT → PUBLIC_BELIEF.

mf_content → religious_align → identity_align → public_belief

Market-fundamentalist ideas can be wrapped in religious language—Christian free enterprise, prosperity-gospel themes, and related frames—strengthening the link between ideology, identity, and belief.

International institutions

IFIs & TRADE_REGIMES (IMF, World Bank, WTO)

Atlas/think tanks → influence IFI discourse → embed market-fundamentalist conditionalities → POLICY_ADOPTION in debtor countries → SUCCESS_STORIES.

mf_content → atlas_tt → ifi_trade → cond_policies → policy_adopt → success_stories → public_belief / elite_belief

Think tanks can shape discourse within international financial institutions; conditionalities and policy advice can then embed market-fundamentalist reforms in debtor countries, while selected “success stories” are circulated as evidence for the model elsewhere.

Academic capture

ACADEMIC_ECON_ORTHODOXY

FUNDING (via chairs, centers) → selective support for pro-market scholars → more market-friendly curricula → ELITE_BELIEF among future policymakers → POLICY_ADOPTION.

f_funding_acad → funding_chairs → econ_orthodoxy → curricula → elite_belief → policy_adopt

Targeted funding for chairs, centers, and institutes can shape economics departments and curricula. That, in turn, affects what future policymakers learn and can feed into elite belief and policy choices.

Social media & algorithms

ALGO_ENGAGEMENT_BIAS, POLARIZATION

Polarizing, identity-laden market-fundamentalist content performs well → ALGO amplifies → MEDIA_AMP → PUBLIC_BELIEF & IDENTITY_ALIGNMENT → more polarizing content → etc. (another reinforcing loop).

mf_content + identity_align → polarizing_content → algo_bias → media_amp → public_belief & identity_align

Polarizing, identity-laden content often performs well on engagement-driven platforms. Algorithmic amplification can therefore strengthen both public belief and identity alignment, while polarization generates still more content suited to the same incentives.
