Absolutely. For a 4–5 page blog, I’d keep it tightly organized around one central claim: **GDP growth is a useful indicator, but political discourse routinely turns it into something it is not—a proxy for welfare, a measure of governmental success, and even evidence that business success translates into governing competence.**

## Working title

**GDP, Growth, and the Myth of the President as Economic Manager**

### 1. Introduction: the category error at the center of economic discourse

Open with the familiar political ritual: presidents are judged by GDP growth, employment, markets, inflation, and other headline indicators, often as though they were CEOs reporting quarterly earnings.

Then introduce the core problem: several distinct concepts are being collapsed together.

Key points:

* GDP measures production, not welfare.
* Economic growth is not synonymous with economic success.
* Government does not have a single objective comparable to firm profit.
* Presidents do not directly “manage” the economy.
* Business competence, macroeconomic competence, and governing competence are distinct.

Possible thesis:

> GDP is an extremely useful accounting measure, but much of our political discourse rests on a chain of conceptual errors: we confuse production with welfare, growth with success, macroeconomic outcomes with presidential management, and private business success with competence in governing a state.

---

## 2. GDP is an accounting measure, not a social objective

Briefly explain what GDP actually does well: it measures the market value of final production over a given period.

Then emphasize what it does **not** contain.

Discuss:

* distribution of output;
* quality of products and services;
* composition of production;
* environmental externalities;
* resilience and reliability;
* leisure and unpaid production;
* durability of goods;
* regional disparities;
* whether production actually satisfies important needs.

A useful illustrative comparison:

Two countries could have similar GDP per capita while differing enormously in housing affordability, commute times, public infrastructure, health, pollution, leisure, inequality, and economic security.

You can also use the “pathological expenditure” point:

* disaster reconstruction raises measured activity;
* illness generates medical spending;
* crime generates spending on security;
* congestion generates fuel consumption.

The point is **not** that GDP counts these incorrectly. The point is that GDP was never designed to distinguish desirable from undesirable states of the world.

Concluding line for the section:

> The mistake is not using GDP. The mistake is turning a measure of throughput into a measure of system success.

---

## 3. Why “growth” itself is not an obvious end

Move from the measurement problem to the deeper conceptual issue.

Introduce the economy as a **system of institutions for satisfying human needs and coordinating production and exchange**, rather than as a machine whose purpose is simply to expand its output every year.

Then develop the systems analogy:

* systems grow when additional capacity is needed;
* mature systems also maintain, repair, adapt, become more efficient, and improve quality;
* indefinite expansion is not inherently evidence of health.

Questions to pose:

* Why should an economy that adequately satisfies needs necessarily need rapid aggregate growth?
* If technology improves quality while reducing material throughput, why should slower measured growth be undesirable?
* Why value national growth if almost all gains accrue to one region or group?
* What if growth degrades the environmental or institutional systems on which future production depends?

Then acknowledge the strongest case **for** growth.

Growth is instrumentally valuable because it:

* raises fiscal capacity;
* makes debt easier to service;
* permits rising incomes without explicit redistribution;
* reduces distributional conflict;
* creates room for new investment and consumption.

But these are arguments that growth **helps accomplish other objectives**.

The distinction:

> Growth can be a means without being the end.

This section could finish by asking an institutional question:

> If social and financial stability require perpetual increases in throughput, perhaps that dependence itself deserves examination.

---

## 4. Government is not a corporation

This is where the blog pivots into politics.

Contrast the objective functions.

A stylized firm may pursue:

* profits;
* enterprise value;
* survival;
* market share.

Government instead pursues multiple, often conflicting objectives:

* security;
* rule of law;
* provision of public goods;
* social stability;
* rights;
* redistribution;
* infrastructure;
* responsiveness to citizens;
* environmental stewardship;
* economic resilience.

There is no single governmental “bottom line.”

Good examples:

* A fire department does not maximize revenue.
* A court does not maximize trials.
* A public-health system may succeed by preventing economic activity in the form of medical treatment.
* A military may succeed precisely because its capabilities are never used.

This gives you one of the strongest conceptual lines in the piece:

> Many government outputs are not transactions at all; they are desirable states of the world.

---

## 5. The president is not the economy’s CEO

Now address the common political interpretation of economic statistics.

Even if GDP growth were our preferred economic objective, attributing GDP performance to a president remains difficult.

Economic outcomes reflect:

* inherited economic conditions;
* monetary policy;
* Congress and fiscal policy;
* technological change;
* global demand;
* energy prices;
* financial conditions;
* demographics;
* private investment;
* wars, pandemics, and natural disasters;
* policies whose effects unfold over many years.

A president can matter substantially without being the sole or even dominant cause of every short-run outcome.

Introduce the causal-inference problem in plain English:

What voters really want to know is not:

> What happened to GDP while this president was in office?

but:

> What would have happened under a different administration facing the same conditions?

That counterfactual is unobservable.

Therefore, presidential economic evaluation should focus more heavily on:

* policies chosen;
* institutional competence;
* management of shocks;
* trade-offs;
* distributional effects;
* long-run consequences;

rather than mechanically assigning credit or blame for every movement in aggregate statistics.

---

## 6. The billionaire-to-president fallacy

This can be the sharpest section of the essay.

Lay out the intuitive chain:

> wealthy → good at business → good with money → understands the economy → can produce economic growth → will govern well.

Then dismantle each step.

First distinction:

**Business competence ≠ macroeconomic competence**

A business can improve profits through layoffs, wage reductions, outsourcing, price increases, market power, or closing unprofitable operations.

Those strategies cannot simply be generalized to the entire economy.

The useful fallacy-of-composition example:

> One firm's wage bill is a cost. Across the economy, wages are also household income.

Likewise:

> One firm can improve its finances by firing workers. An entire economy cannot enrich everyone by eliminating everyone's income.

Then:

**Macroeconomic competence ≠ governing competence**

Even a brilliant economist is not automatically good at:

* diplomacy;
* administration;
* coalition building;
* constitutional governance;
* crisis management;
* law;
* security policy.

So the argument fails at several separate levels.

You could present the complete chain visually in the prose:

**wealth → business skill → macroeconomic skill → GDP growth → economic welfare → governmental success**

and say:

> Nearly every arrow in this chain requires a separate argument. Political discourse often treats the entire sequence as self-evident.

---

## 7. Why the misconception persists

A short section explaining why these errors are attractive would give the piece more depth.

The “country as corporation” model is cognitively simple.

It gives people:

* GDP as revenue;
* the president as CEO;
* the national debt as a credit-card balance;
* elections as shareholder votes;
* billionaires as proven managers.

The alternative is much harder:

An economy is a decentralized system involving millions of agents, while government is a multi-objective institutional system operating inside it.

That explanation lacks the simplicity of the corporate analogy.

You can also make the point that **measurement convenience becomes normative importance**:

> Because GDP is standardized, frequent, comparable, and quantifiable, we gradually move from “this is something we can measure” to “this is what we should maximize.”

---

## 8. Conclusion: put GDP back in its proper place

Return to a moderate rather than anti-GDP conclusion.

GDP is:

* useful;
* informative;
* indispensable for many macroeconomic questions.

But it is one indicator of system activity, not the system's objective function.

End with the broader principle:

> A healthy economy should ultimately be judged by how effectively, sustainably, and robustly it enables people to live the lives they value—not simply by how rapidly the monetary value of final production increases.

And politically:

> A government should be evaluated by whether it fulfills the many functions entrusted to it, while a president should be judged by decisions, institutions, and outcomes over which the office can plausibly exercise influence—not as the CEO of a national corporation whose quarterly earnings happen to be called GDP.
