    • The gift economy and patronage ¶

    In monarchies (and court politics generally), valuable gifts aren’t “free”; they’re signals of allegiance and tools for access. The ruler dispenses protection, exemptions, offices, or favorable treatment; elites compete to be seen as loyal and useful. The key behavioral markers are: public display, personalization (the ruler, not an institution, is treated as the decision node), and discretionary reward (benefits granted case-by-case rather than by neutral rule).

    1. The “royal gift” object: the Qatari jet as court-style tribute: Multiple outlets have reported that the U.S. Air Force accepted a Boeing 747-8 donated by Qatar (often described as a ~$400M jet), intended as an interim Air Force One after retrofits, with delivery discussed for summer 2026.

      • Tribute logic: A foreign ruling family gives an extraordinary, prestige object to the ruler’s system. In court politics, that’s classic: a lavish gift communicates “we honor you; we seek favor/standing.”
      • Spectacle + symbolism: A “palace in the sky” plays directly into regal aesthetics—power made visible through opulence—rather than republican modesty norms.
      • Personalized authority: Even if lawyers argue the jet is “to the U.S. Air Force” (institutional), the political meaning is still about proximity to Trump and what Qatar might gain by pleasing him. That’s the court dynamic: gifts function as relationship instruments, not just assets.

      A defender can argue that the recipient is the U.S. government, not Trump personally; it’s a procurement/stopgap decision; and the gift is being security-modified under U.S. control but even if legal on paper, the court-style meaning still attaches when a head of state is seen as the reason the gift exists and the main beneficiary of the upgraded prestige.

    2. The “favor pipeline” - corporate money + tariff relief as patronage politics: There’s reporting and analysis about tariff exemptions being sought by thousands of firms and concerns about opacity and political connection advantages. This maps well onto monarchic behavior because Tariffs become discretionary favors (exemptions granted to some, not others). Elites behave like courtiers: they hire lobbyists, cultivate relationships, and make donations to improve their odds.

      • A Cato research brief (summarizing academic work) reports that campaign contributions and lobbying increased the likelihood of tariff exemptions on Chinese imports during Trump’s first administration.
      • Related summaries (and university writeups of peer-review–bound research) likewise describe politically connected firms being more likely to receive exemptions.

      At Davos (Jan 2026), Reuters and Business Insider reported Trump said he agreed to lower tariffs on Swiss imports (from a previously discussed 39% down to 15%) after meetings with Swiss business leaders, and BI reports he acknowledged receiving a Rolex desk clock as a gesture. This is basically a modernized “audience with the king”: elites travel, plead their case, offer a prestige gift, and the ruler announces a discretionary tariff change. You don’t need to allege illegality. The monarchic mapping is about the performance of personalized power and gift symbolism. Lobbying exists everywhere, but monarchic behavior is when it’s performed as personal loyalty + gift exchange + discretionary mercy, rather than rule-governed administration.

    The Board of Peace ¶

    1. The price of entry is effectively a “tribute tier”: Reporting on the draft charter and the launch describes $1B in cash as the price for membership that lasts longer than a standard term (and in some accounts, for “permanent” status). So even if you don’t call it “entry” (since there are also shorter terms), the monarchic part is the buy-in for longevity.

    2. The chair (Trump) has sweeping, centralized power—including veto: A key difference from most multilateral bodies is that the Board is described as chairman-centered, where Trump (as Chairman) can invite/exclude members and approve or veto board resolutions. This is not normally how international organizations work; in most international organizations, authority is distributed across members, secretariats, committees, and formal voting rules—no single leader has blanket personal veto over everything.

    3. It’s structured outside the UN system (and the UN is limiting engagement): Reuters reports the Board was launched with a widened mandate and sparked concerns it could undercut the UN; the UN spokesperson is quoted as saying UN engagement would only be within the Gaza-resolution context. That maps neatly onto the “parallel court” thesis: an alternative venue where the “king” convenes the willing.

    Reuters explicitly notes that few sign-ups for the borard of peace are democracies. Belarus as an example is potent. Reuters reports Belarus’s Alexander Lukashenko signed on, notes he’s been in power since 1994, and that the exiled opposition describes him as a dictator. Better World Campaign’s analysis says the Board relies on voluntary contributions and describes governance as centralized around the Chairman. A published copy/summary of the charter (CIE) states the Executive Board manages finances with the Chairman’s approval, implying Trump can control how funds are allocated. Trump would have significant control over membership and funding distribution with essentially zero accountability. The design creates a pay-to-play incentive structure: states that pay more can secure longer status, and a chairman with broad discretion can reward compliant members—an institutional setup that resembles court patronage even if no illegal quid pro quo is proven.

    International organizations typically dilute power through rules, committees, and member votes. The Board of Peace does the opposite: it concentrates authority in a single chairman, who can invite and eject members and reportedly approve or veto all resolutions. Membership itself is stratified by money—states can buy longer or permanent status with a $1 billion cash commitment—making participation look less like rule-based multilateralism and more like court politics. Unsurprisingly, many early participants are non-democracies, including Belarus’s Alexander Lukashenko, a long-ruling strongman whom opponents describe as a dictator. In a monarchic system, tribute purchases proximity; proximity purchases discretion. The Board’s design imports that logic into international governance.

    No concern for DOJ contempt of court ¶

    The DOJ missed the statutory deadline in the Epstein Files Transparency Act (Dec 19, 2025) and, as of mid-to-late January, has released only a small fraction of the identified corpus (e.g., ~125,000 pages out of 2+ million potentially responsive documents, with heavy redactions). Lawmakers and victims’ advocates have accused DOJ of stalling and are pushing for outside oversight, but a federal judge declined to appoint an independent monitor/special master in the Maxwell case—reporting frames that as a jurisdictional/vehicle issue, not the court holding DOJ in contempt. Some reporting says members of Congress are exploring tools like contempt of Congress or lawsuits to force compliance—again, that’s different from a judge already finding DOJ in contempt.

    A theme of monarchy is disdain for accountability and control of information, this episode fits well via three court-politics markers:

    1. Secrecy as power: Monarchic courts treat information as a controlled resource. Delays + mass redactions function like gatekeeping: disclosure happens at the ruler’s pace, in the ruler’s preferred form.
    2. Executive supremacy posture: Even when the obligation comes from a bipartisan statute, the practical message becomes: the executive decides what the public gets to see, when. That’s less “rule of law” and more “the center decides.”
    3. Deflection to procedural complexity: Courts/monarchies often justify non-transparency by invoking process (“it’s complicated,” “it takes time,” “we must protect X”). That may be legally arguable, but it’s also a classic technique for avoiding accountability while appearing compliant.

    The behavior tracks a monarchic pattern: the Justice Department missed a congressional transparency deadline on the Epstein files and has released only a sliver of the identified records, heavily redacted, while efforts to impose outside oversight have stalled. Whatever the formal legal posture, the practical effect is familiar to court politics: information is treated as a discretionary asset controlled from the center, not as a public right enforceable on schedule.

    Ballrooms... ¶

    This fits the “monarchic priorities” theme really well because it’s less about the policy details and more about the performance of rule: opulence, personal legacy-building, and the idea that the center of the state should look and feel like a court.

    • Palace logic (spectacle over service): A massive state ballroom is a classic “court” investment—ceremony, banquets, chandeliers, the ruler hosting the elite—rather than a project aimed at ordinary people’s material problems. PBS framed the scale and symbolism as unusually large and controversial.
    • Personal legacy project: Reporting describes Trump having wanted a White House ballroom for years and treating it as a signature addition. That “I will build” vibe is very monarch-coded: the ruler reshapes national space to reflect their taste and permanence.
    • Rule-bending / bypassing norms: A big part of the controversy is process: lawsuits and court hearings over whether approvals were bypassed, whether advisory review is being skirted, and whether Congress should be involved. Monarchs do not care for accountability.
    • Court patronage vibes via funding: The privately funded angle is where the “court” analogy gets sharp: if wealthy donors and corporations help fund a presidential vanity project, it resembles old patronage systems where elites bankroll the palace and receive access/status in return (even if no explicit quid pro quo is proven). Concerns about “pay-to-play” have been raised by lawmakers and in coverage.

    The ballroom is a priority signal: investing political energy in courtly spectacle and personal legacy while Americans struggle with everyday costs. That’s a monarchic pattern—state power expressed through pageantry, funded and shaped by elites, with transparency and public consent treated as optional.

    Territory Conquering ¶

    We are essentially observing a shift from treaties, institutions, and equal sovereignty toward personalized great-power prerogative: major states get “neighborhoods,” smaller states get managed. That’s basically how pre-20th-century empires and kingdoms talked:

    • A core realm that must be “secured”
    • Borderlands where the stronger power claims special rights
    • Client relationships (protection in exchange for obedience/limits on outside ties)

    When modern leaders talk this way, it’s not literally “crowns and castles,” but it’s the same logic of dominion.

    1. A revived Monroe Doctrine / “Trump Corollary”: Reuters reported that the administration’s 2025 National Security Strategy revives the Monroe Doctrine and frames a “Trump Corollary” emphasizing U.S. dominance in the Americas. Atlantic Council and Chatham House describe the NSS as centering U.S. protection of the Western Hemisphere and explicitly carving out this “Trump Corollary.” PBS similarly summarized the strategy as asserting U.S. power in the Western Hemisphere under that corollary framing. It’s an official, doctrinal version of “this hemisphere is our backyard,” i.e., the king’s realm has privileged status and outsiders must keep out.

    2. “Dominance” rhetoric in the Western Hemisphere: Reuters has also reported Trump “vowed to restore U.S. dominance” in the Western Hemisphere and signaled willingness to intervene again there. Reuters’ “Donroe Doctrine” piece frames the NSS as “cementing U.S. dominance in the Western Hemisphere by pushing out competitors.” Other news orgs have similarly describes the “Donroe Doctrine” as seeking influence over the hemisphere, with critics calling it imperial. The administration explicitly defines the hemisphere as a special U.S. zone where outside powers must be pushed back which is the sphere-of-influence idea in plain English.

    Trump’s worldview leans toward a pre-modern ‘realm’ model of international politics: great powers get neighborhoods, and the strongest power claims special rights inside its sphere. The administration’s 2025 National Security Strategy explicitly revives the Monroe Doctrine through a ‘Trump Corollary,’ prioritizing U.S. dominance in the Western Hemisphere and treating extra-hemispheric influence as a threat to be pushed out. That’s less a rules-based order than a court-and-dominion order: the center sets terms, the periphery complies, and sovereignty becomes conditional on staying within the ruler’s acceptable bounds. While it is true Monroe Doctrine ideas predate Trump and spheres of influence are realist geopolitics; that’s why the monarchic mapping works. The point isn’t uniqueness; it’s the explicitness and entitlement language (dominance, pushing others out, special hemispheric prerogatives) and the downgrading of multilateral constraints, which looks more like 19th-century great-power management than 21st-century institutionalism. This is precisely what is meant by monarchic behavior.
