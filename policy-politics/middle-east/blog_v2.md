## 1. A conversation that stopped being about the evidence

The other day, I found myself in a political conversation that left me thinking less about the disagreement itself than about the way the disagreement unfolded. We were discussing Israel’s military campaign in Gaza, particularly the enormous disparity between Palestinian and Israeli deaths. I was troubled by the scale of Palestinian casualties and wanted to understand whether the military decisions producing so much destruction could be justified. The person I was speaking with appeared broadly supportive of Israel’s conduct. That difference in perspective could have provided the basis for a serious conversation, but something else happened.

When I raised the death toll, I was told: “Things happen in war. Would you rather have Hamas or Israel? Yeah, Israel has done some bad stuff, but Iran and all of that is way worse.” Each part of that response seemed to point in a different direction. Some were relevant to the wider conflict, but none directly addressed the question I had raised. Rather than argue over which state or armed group was morally worse, I tried to make the discussion more specific.

I asked whether a death ratio on the order of thirty Palestinians for every Israeli should at least command our attention. Who were the Palestinians counted in that total? How many were civilians, and how many were combatants? What was being achieved militarily, and at what cost? Most importantly, were Israeli operations being conducted with adequate regard for minimizing civilian harm, or might different choices have achieved legitimate security objectives with fewer deaths? The figure was one I invoked for the sake of conversation, and is roughly consistent with what I've read. 

I did not expect either of us to resolve these questions on the spot. They were invitations to examine evidence: casualty data, military objectives, methods of attack, operational constraints, humanitarian findings, and the legal standards governing civilian protection. I was not claiming to possess a better campaign plan, and I was not suggesting that a disparity in deaths, by itself, proved that every attack was indiscriminate or unlawful. My point was more modest and, I thought, more productive: consequences this grave deserve scrutiny rather than an automatic appeal to the difficulties of war.

The response was pure brain rot: “Well, you tell me what they should be doing then.” When I acknowledged that I could not personally devise a comprehensive alternative military strategy, the answer came back: “Yeah, see, you don’t know.” This argumentative turn is the part that stayed with me. I had asked for an examination of a strategy’s justification; I was now being tested on my ability to replace it.

That shift matters. My inability to command a military campaign established nothing about whether the existing campaign was necessary, lawful, effective, or conducted with feasible precautions. It established a limit to my knowledge. Yet in the conversation, that admission was treated as though it had settled the larger question. The appearance of a rebuttal had taken the place of a rebuttal itself.

This essay uses that exchange as a case study in a broader problem: the tendency to transform criticism of a consequential decision into a demand that the critic solve the entire problem. I want to examine how this maneuver works, why it can be persuasive, when a request for alternatives is entirely reasonable, and how a discussion can be redirected toward genuine inquiry. The question beneath all of it is simple: **when did not knowing the best replacement become evidence that the existing choice is justified?**



<a id="two-questions"></a>
## 2. Two different questions—and a burden that moved

The central confusion is the tendency to treat questioning a decision as though it were a claim to know how to replace it. Those activities are related, but they impose different intellectual responsibilities. **One question asks whether the existing strategy was justified, necessary, effective, and appropriately attentive to civilian life. Another asks whether I can personally design a superior strategy that achieves the same objectives with fewer casualties.**

Evaluating a strategy may require examining alternatives, operational limits, and foreseeable consequences. Yet the relationship between the questions does not make them interchangeable. Someone can have reasonable grounds to scrutinize a decision while lacking the expertise and information required to devise or implement its replacement.

The first question concerns the justification of an existing decision. The second concerns my ability to formulate a replacement. I can have legitimate reasons to question the former without possessing the technical expertise to accomplish the latter. This distinction becomes particularly important when the decision being scrutinized involves specialized knowledge. Military operations, like medical procedures, engineering projects, and economic policies, require expertise that most ordinary citizens do not possess. But expertise is not a prerequisite for recognizing evidence that something may be wrong, nor does its absence eliminate the legitimacy of asking questions. Suppose someone claims that a particular military operation was necessary, that its objectives justified its consequences, and that reasonable measures were taken to minimize civilian harm.

Those are substantive claims about the operation. They can be examined through evidence: the objectives pursued, the intelligence available, the expected and actual consequences, the precautions undertaken, and the alternatives reasonably available at the time. Now suppose I question those claims. I may be uncertain whether adequate precautions were taken. I may see evidence suggesting that civilian harm was foreseeable or potentially avoidable. I may ask whether different tactics, timing, or targeting decisions were considered. None of this requires that I already know which alternative strategy would have been superior. In fact, **the purpose of asking these questions is precisely to determine what the evidence supports.**

Demanding that I supply the answer before the investigation can begin defeats the purpose of asking the question.

### Where the inference breaks

We can express the reasoning underlying my interlocutor's response as a simple argument:

1. A critic questions whether Strategy A is justified.
2. The critic cannot provide a complete alternative, Strategy B.
3. Therefore, the critic's objection to Strategy A is invalid.

The problem is that the conclusion does not follow from the premises. My inability to demonstrate that Strategy B would be better tells us something about the limits of my knowledge. It tells us nothing definitive about the merits of Strategy A. Strategy A might be the best available option. It might be deeply flawed. It might be effective at achieving one objective while causing unnecessary harm in pursuit of another. Or it might involve tradeoffs that require more evidence before any responsible judgment can be made. All of these possibilities remain open regardless of whether I can personally design its replacement.

**The absence of a demonstrated alternative is not the presence of a justification.** There is also an important epistemological distinction between *not knowing that a better alternative exists* and *knowing that no better alternative exists*. These statements may sound similar, but they represent radically different claims. The first acknowledges uncertainty. The second asserts a conclusion about the available possibilities. When someone responds to criticism by demanding an alternative and then treats the critic's inability to provide one as proof that the current approach is justified, they effectively convert the first statement into the second. But that conversion is unwarranted.

To establish that no feasible superior alternative existed would require examining the relevant constraints, evidence, and options. It cannot be established merely by demonstrating that one participant in a conversation does not know what those alternatives might have been.

### What the burden of proof actually requires

This error becomes even more significant when we consider the burden of proof. Suppose someone defends a military campaign on the grounds that its destructive consequences were necessary to achieve legitimate security objectives. That position carries an evidentiary burden. The person defending it should be able to offer reasons for believing that the objectives, methods, and consequences were justified. When I question whether those reasons are sufficient, I am not necessarily advancing a competing military strategy. I am examining the justification already being offered. Yet the response "Well, what would you do instead?" changes the terms of the discussion.

Rather than explaining why the existing strategy was justified, my interlocutor demands that I establish the superiority of an alternative. The original claim is no longer being subjected to scrutiny. Instead, the critic is now required to solve an extraordinarily complex problem before the original claim receives any further examination. The effect is to give the existing strategy a presumption of correctness it has not necessarily earned. And if the critic cannot produce a comprehensive alternative, the defender can declare victory without having demonstrated anything about the strategy being defended. This is not to suggest that critics have no evidentiary responsibilities.

If I positively assert that a particular Israeli military operation was indiscriminate, that a specific alternative would have saved lives, or that a particular strategy was militarily unnecessary, those are claims requiring evidence. I cannot simply assume them to be true. Likewise, a defender of Israeli military conduct is entitled to question my assumptions, challenge my evidence, and point out operational constraints I may have overlooked. That is what substantive disagreement should involve. But there is a fundamental difference between challenging the evidence for a claim and imposing an entirely new obligation on the person raising a question.

**The burden of substantiating a claim should correspond to the claim actually being made, not to a more demanding claim substituted by one's opponent.** My claim was not that I possessed the solution to Israel's security challenges. My position was that the scale of destruction and loss of life warranted serious examination of the decisions producing those outcomes. Perhaps you should second guess blind support of policies supporting such decisions. Those are not equivalent propositions, and they do not carry equivalent burdens of proof.

## 3. Five ways to change the subject without answering it

What made this conversation particularly interesting was not simply the final demand that I produce an alternative military strategy. It was the sequence of responses that preceded it. As I reflected on the exchange, I noticed that each response seemed to redirect the discussion away from the specific questions I was raising. Rather than examining the evidence concerning Israel's military conduct, the conversation repeatedly shifted toward broader, more emotionally charged, or less directly relevant considerations. The sequence went something like this: "Things happen in war." "Would you rather have Hamas or Israel?" "Iran is much worse."

"Well, tell me what Israel should do instead." "Yeah, see, you don't know." These statements are not all logically equivalent, nor is every one inherently fallacious in every context. Some contain legitimate considerations that could contribute to a serious discussion. Wars do produce casualties. Hamas represents a real security threat. Military alternatives must be evaluated against practical constraints. And comparisons between governments can sometimes help illuminate broader geopolitical questions. The problem arises when these observations are deployed not to advance an investigation, but to replace it. So what follows is an examination of five distinct rhetorical moves, the reasoning errors they can involve, and the way their cumulative effect can obstruct meaningful inquiry.

### “Things happen in war”: normalization

The first response was perhaps the most familiar: "Things happen in war." On its face, this statement is uncontroversial. Wars are destructive. Civilians are killed. Military operations involve uncertainty, imperfect intelligence, and consequences that cannot always be anticipated or prevented. Nobody needs to dispute these realities. But acknowledging that civilian casualties occur in warfare does not answer whether *particular* casualties were avoidable, whether specific military operations were justified, or whether adequate precautions were taken to protect civilian life. This is the distinction between recognizing a general feature of a phenomenon and explaining a particular instance of it.

Imagine investigating an industrial accident in which dozens of workers were killed. A company representative responds, "Well, industrial work is dangerous. Accidents happen." The statement is true, but its truth tells us almost nothing about the accident being investigated. Were safety protocols followed? Was equipment properly maintained? Were warnings ignored? Could different procedures have prevented the deaths? The general fact that accidents occur does not establish that this accident was unavoidable. Likewise, the general fact that wars produce civilian casualties does not establish that any particular military operation, targeting decision, or level of civilian harm was necessary.

What matters is not merely whether harm is possible within an activity, but whether the specific harm in question was justified, foreseeable, preventable, or the result of decisions that warrant criticism. In the context of my conversation, "things happen in war" functioned as a form of normalization. It placed the extraordinary scale of death within a familiar category—war—and then seemed to treat that categorization as sufficient explanation. Yet the very questions I was asking concerned the extent to which those deaths could be explained by unavoidable military realities rather than avoidable decisions. **To say that something commonly happens is not to establish that it had to happen, or that it was permissible for it to happen.**

The statement might serve as the beginning of an explanation. It cannot substitute for the explanation itself.

### “Would you rather have Hamas or Israel?”: the false choice

The next move was to introduce a choice: "Would you rather have Hamas or Israel?" Here, the subject changed in a more significant way. I had been questioning the conduct of a particular military campaign. The response instead asked me to express a preference between two political and military actors. This is an example of a false dichotomy when the implied choice is presented as though it exhausts the positions available. The suggestion is that one must either defend Israel's actions or align oneself, implicitly or explicitly, with Hamas. But there is no logical reason that these positions must be mutually exclusive.

One can condemn Hamas's attacks against Israeli civilians, recognize Israel's legitimate security concerns, and simultaneously question whether Israel's military response has complied with international humanitarian law or adequately protected Palestinian civilians. These positions are entirely compatible. In fact, consistently applying the principle that civilian lives deserve protection requires evaluating the conduct of all parties rather than granting immunity from criticism to whichever party one regards as more legitimate. What interests me about this particular maneuver is how it transforms an analytical question into a question of allegiance. Rather than asking, "Were these military decisions justified?" the conversation becomes, "Whose side are you on?"

That change matters because questions of allegiance can carry emotional and social consequences that questions of evidence do not necessarily carry. If a person can be made to feel that criticizing Israel implies defending Hamas, the conversation no longer needs to examine whether the criticism is accurate. The criticism itself becomes suspect because of the political identity supposedly associated with it. This can create pressure to retreat from legitimate questions merely to avoid being associated with an objectionable group. But an argument's validity does not depend on whether its conclusion is politically convenient or whether someone fears the associations attached to it.

**Condemning one party's wrongdoing does not confer moral or legal immunity upon another.** And preferring one political actor over another does not settle whether the preferred actor's particular decisions are justified. The original inquiry was about actions, evidence, and consequences. The response attempted to transform it into a choice between identities. These are fundamentally different subjects.

### “Iran is worse”: changing the comparison

The next response introduced another comparison: "Iran and all of that is way worse." This move resembles *whataboutism*: responding to criticism of one actor by directing attention toward the wrongdoing of another. Closely related is the fallacy of relative privation, in which a concern is treated as less deserving of attention because some other problem is supposedly more serious. There are circumstances in which comparisons are useful. If the discussion concerned regional security threats, the relative conduct of Israel, Iran, and other governments might be directly relevant. But that was not the question being asked.

I was not attempting to rank Middle Eastern governments by moral legitimacy or compare every instance of violence in the region. I was questioning the conduct and consequences of a particular military campaign. Even assuming, for the sake of argument, that Iran's government were substantially worse by every relevant measure, what would that establish about Israel's targeting decisions, civilian casualties, or available military alternatives? Nothing directly. The conduct of one government cannot be justified merely by identifying another government whose conduct may be worse. Consider the reasoning in an ordinary context. A company is accused of serious workplace safety violations. Its representative responds by pointing out that a competing company has an even worse safety record.

That comparison might be factually accurate. It might reveal something important about industry-wide conditions. But it does not establish that the original company's practices are acceptable. The relevant safety questions remain unanswered. The same logic applies here. If the claim is that another government is more dangerous, more repressive, or more responsible for regional instability, that claim can be investigated on its own merits. But it does not resolve whether Israel's military operations were necessary, proportionate, discriminate, or conducted with adequate precautions. The rhetorical effect is to expand the discussion until the original issue becomes just one concern among many, potentially more alarming concerns.

Instead of assessing a particular action against relevant standards, the participants are encouraged to assess whether the actor responsible compares favorably with someone else. **But being less objectionable than another actor is not equivalent to being justified in one's own conduct.** A comparison can provide context. It cannot replace direct evaluation.

### “Tell me what they should do”: moving the burden

Having moved through the general inevitability of war, the preference between Israel and Hamas, and the comparative wrongdoing of Iran, the conversation eventually reached the response that most clearly exposed the underlying problem: "Well, you tell me what they should be doing then." This was the decisive shift. Until this point, the responses had offered broad contextual considerations without directly examining the original question. Now, the responsibility for providing an explanation was transferred to me. I had asked whether the military campaign was being conducted in a way that adequately minimized civilian casualties. My interlocutor responded by demanding that I devise a better campaign.

Notice the change in what must now be established. Originally, the question was whether there was sufficient evidence to justify the decisions being made. Now, the question was whether I could demonstrate the superiority of decisions that had not been made. The existing strategy was effectively granted a presumption of justification unless I could personally produce a superior alternative. This is a form of burden shifting. In a serious discussion, the person making a substantive claim bears responsibility for supporting that claim. If someone maintains that a military strategy is necessary, effective, and appropriately protective of civilians, those conclusions require evidence.

Likewise, if I assert that a particular operation was unlawful or that an alternative strategy would definitely have produced better results, I have a responsibility to substantiate those assertions. But my original question did not claim to establish the superiority of some alternative. It asked whether the existing approach had been adequately examined and justified. The demand for a replacement therefore imposed an obligation that did not follow from the position I was actually advancing. It also created an extraordinarily high threshold for criticism. Military strategy involves intelligence assessments, logistical constraints, operational planning, adversarial behavior, and technical considerations unavailable to most ordinary citizens. Producing a comprehensive alternative would require expertise and information that neither participant necessarily possessed.

### “You don’t know”: dismissing the critic

The final response completed the shift: "Yeah, see, you don't know." This was perhaps the most revealing moment in the conversation. Strictly speaking, my interlocutor had identified something true: I did not possess a complete alternative military strategy. I had not claimed otherwise. But the statement was delivered as though acknowledging that limitation resolved the entire disagreement. Here, the discussion ceased to concern the validity of the original questions and became a judgment about the questioner's competence. The implied reasoning was simple: because I could not provide the requested technical solution, I lacked sufficient standing to criticize the existing one.

This resembles an appeal to expertise used in reverse—not an appeal to a particular expert's evidence, but an attempt to disqualify a nonexpert from participating in the evaluation of a consequential decision. Expertise undoubtedly matters. A person who has studied military operations may understand constraints that I do not. Their knowledge can expose weaknesses in my assumptions and help explain why certain seemingly attractive alternatives are infeasible. But expertise must contribute evidence or reasoning to the discussion. Simply pointing out that someone lacks expertise does not establish that their concerns are unfounded. A person can be mistaken about the explanation for a problem while still correctly identifying that the problem deserves investigation.

A patient may not understand surgical techniques but can reasonably question why a procedure is necessary. A homeowner may not understand structural engineering but can ask whether a major renovation is justified. A citizen may not understand military planning but can question whether civilian-protection obligations have been met. In all three cases, specialized knowledge may be necessary to answer the question properly. It is not necessary to establish that the question can legitimately be asked.

Taken together, the replies had a striking cumulative effect. The subject moved from the circumstances of Palestinian deaths to the inevitability of war, then to political allegiance, comparative wrongdoing, and finally my presumed incompetence. None of those moves established whether Israel’s decisions were justified. The sequence illustrates how a conversation can end with the appearance of an answer even when its central question has gone unexamined.

## 4. The leaking roof: why expertise is not immunity

One of the most useful ways to evaluate an argument is to remove it from the emotionally charged context in which it originally appeared and apply the same reasoning to a more ordinary situation. Political discussions are particularly vulnerable to distorted reasoning because our judgments are often entangled with ideological commitments, group loyalties, emotional associations, and deeply held moral convictions. Arguments that might appear persuasive when defending a favored political position can become obviously unreasonable when transferred to circumstances in which those commitments are absent. Consider a simple hypothetical. Suppose a leak appears in your roof. A contractor inspects the property and recommends removing and replacing the entire roof at a cost of $45,000. The leak seems localized, and while you recognize that hidden deterioration could justify extensive work, you want to know whether a targeted repair might be sufficient. You raise the concern with another stakeholder: “Do we really need to replace everything? Shouldn’t we ask for an explanation or get another estimate?”

Instead of considering the question, the stakeholder asks whether you are a professional roofer. When you admit that you are not, the response is effectively: “Then explain exactly how you would repair it. You can’t? That’s what I thought.” The contractor may turn out to be right about the replacement, but most people would recognize that this reply fails to establish it. And what makes it inadequate is not that the contractor must necessarily be wrong. The contractor might be entirely correct. There might be extensive structural damage, hidden deterioration, or serious problems that make a complete replacement the safest and most economical option.

The homeowner's suspicion might ultimately prove unfounded. But none of that changes the fact that the questions themselves are legitimate. The homeowner does not need to know how to install roofing materials, repair structural components, or calculate drainage requirements to ask whether the recommended work is necessary. They need not arrive with a complete engineering plan before requesting an explanation, obtaining another estimate, or asking the contractor to demonstrate why a less extensive repair would be inadequate. **The inability to perform a specialized task does not eliminate the ability—or the right—to critically evaluate whether that task is necessary.**

### Expertise should supply reasons, not immunity

At the center of this analogy is a distinction between possessing specialized knowledge and possessing unquestionable authority. The roofing contractor has expertise the homeowner lacks. That expertise should matter. It gives the contractor a stronger basis for identifying structural problems, evaluating repair options, and recommending appropriate interventions. But it does not make the contractor infallible. Expertise can be exercised competently or incompetently. Recommendations can be based on accurate or inaccurate assessments. Specialists can overlook relevant considerations, disagree with one another, or make judgments influenced by incomplete information. In some circumstances, professionals may also have financial, institutional, or reputational incentives that affect their recommendations.

None of these possibilities proves that any particular expert is mistaken or acting dishonestly. They merely establish why expertise does not eliminate the need for scrutiny. The correct relationship between expert judgment and critical inquiry is therefore not one of unconditional obedience. It is one in which expertise provides reasons, evidence, and informed evaluations that can be examined. If the contractor explains that water damage extends throughout the roof's supporting structure, produces photographs documenting the deterioration, identifies applicable building standards, and demonstrates why a localized repair would not solve the problem, the homeowner now has substantive reasons to accept the recommendation.

The homeowner may still seek a second opinion, but the discussion has advanced. The contractor has done something fundamentally different from simply declaring, "I'm the expert, and you're not." He has provided an explanation. **Expertise becomes intellectually valuable when it helps us understand why a conclusion is justified, not merely when it is invoked to discourage us from questioning that conclusion.**

### Delegation and accountability

The roof-repair example also reveals a broader institutional problem. When we hire specialists, elect representatives, or authorize institutions to make decisions on our behalf, we delegate certain responsibilities because those tasks require knowledge, resources, or capabilities we do not personally possess. But delegation is not the same as surrendering the right to oversight. In economics and organizational theory, this relationship is often examined through the *principal-agent problem*. A principal entrusts an agent with responsibility for pursuing certain objectives. The agent may possess information and expertise unavailable to the principal, creating the possibility that the principal cannot easily determine whether the agent's decisions are appropriate.

The homeowner and contractor provide a simple example. The homeowner wants a safe, functional roof at a reasonable cost. The contractor possesses the specialized knowledge necessary to perform the work. Because the homeowner cannot independently verify every technical recommendation, some mechanism of accountability becomes necessary. That might involve competing estimates, independent inspections, contractual guarantees, professional standards, or documentation explaining the proposed work. These safeguards do not exist because every contractor is presumed dishonest. They exist because expertise and delegated decision-making create information asymmetries that make independent scrutiny valuable. The same underlying problem becomes considerably more consequential when applied to government and military institutions.

Military commanders possess operational knowledge that ordinary citizens generally lack. Governments control intelligence, resources, and decision-making processes that cannot always be independently examined by the public. But the decisions made within these institutions can have enormous consequences, including the loss of human life. The need for specialized expertise does not dissolve the need for accountability. If anything, the combination of concentrated authority, incomplete public information, and potentially irreversible consequences makes meaningful oversight especially important. There is also an important difference between the homeowner analogy and warfare: the people bearing the gravest consequences are often not the people who authorized the decisions.

In Gaza, Palestinian civilians bear the direct consequences of military operations in ways that cannot be adequately captured by an analogy about financial stakeholders or property owners. Their lives are not simply costs within someone else's strategic calculation. They are individuals entitled to protection under international humanitarian law. Similarly, Israeli civilians have legitimate interests in protection against armed attacks. The relevant questions of accountability therefore extend beyond whether taxpayers approve of their government's military policies. They concern the obligations of institutions wielding lethal force and the rights of the people affected by their decisions. **The greater the power delegated to an institution, and the more consequential its decisions, the stronger the justification for demanding reasons, evidence, and independent scrutiny.**

### Where the analogy stops working

There is an important objection worth considering. Military strategy is not roof repair. A contractor operates in a relatively controlled environment. A military commander confronts an adversary who adapts, conceals information, and actively attempts to undermine the commander's objectives. Military operations may involve incomplete intelligence, urgent decisions, uncertain outcomes, and information that cannot safely be disclosed to the public. An alternative that appears obvious to an outside observer may be operationally infeasible. A less destructive tactic in one circumstance might increase risks elsewhere or fail to accomplish a legitimate military objective. These distinctions matter. It would be intellectually careless to treat military operations as though they could be evaluated with the same straightforward cost comparisons used in ordinary contracting decisions.

But notice what these objections actually establish. They establish that assessing military conduct is difficult. They do not establish that assessing military conduct is illegitimate. In fact, the existence of such complexities is a reason for more careful investigation, greater intellectual humility, and appropriate forms of independent oversight. The difficulty of obtaining complete information should make us more cautious about confidently declaring that a particular strategy was unnecessary. But it should also make us cautious about confidently declaring that the strategy was necessary. Uncertainty cuts both ways. A person defending a military decision cannot simply invoke complexity as evidence that the decision was justified, any more than a critic can ignore complexity when alleging that a better option was readily available.

Both conclusions require supporting evidence. The responsible response to limited knowledge is neither automatic condemnation nor automatic deference. It is a recognition of what remains unknown, alongside a willingness to investigate what can be established.

The analogy is not meant to show that military strategy is as simple as roof repair. It isolates a narrower point: there are circumstances in which a layperson may reasonably demand a justification without being able to implement the decision under scrutiny. If complexity limits what the public can know, it should make judgments more careful on both sides—not give the existing decision an automatic presumption of correctness. **Expertise should be a source of explanation, not a substitute for one.**


<a id="gaza"></a>
## 5. What the original question about Gaza actually required

Having established the logical problems with my interlocutor's responses, it is worth returning to the question that prompted the conversation in the first place. Was there anything unreasonable about questioning Israel's military conduct in light of the extraordinary number of Palestinians killed? More specifically, was it unreasonable to ask whether the military campaign could have been conducted differently, in ways that achieved legitimate security objectives while reducing civilian deaths? I would argue that these are not merely reasonable questions. They are among the most important questions one can ask when evaluating the conduct of any military campaign.

These questions do not arise merely from moral intuition or discomfort with the scale of destruction. They are similar to those military analysts, humanitarian investigators, and legal authorities must consider when evaluating necessity, effectiveness, and lawfulness.

### A disparity is a reason to investigate, not a legal verdict

The immediate concern I raised during the conversation was the enormous disparity between Palestinian and Israeli deaths. I referred to a ratio of approximately thirty Palestinians killed for every Israeli killed, using it to illustrate the scale of the imbalance and to question whether that disparity deserved serious investigation. For a published analysis, such a ratio must be tied to a clearly defined period, consistent casualty categories, and identifiable sources. Different periods of the conflict produce different ratios, and different datasets may include or exclude particular types of deaths. The [United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs](https://www.ochaopt.org/data/casualties?tab=palestinianfatalities), for example, maintains historical casualty records with specific definitions and verification standards. Its records for the hostilities beginning October 7, 2023, are handled differently from its older verified casualty database.

Nevertheless, the underlying observation remains: the scale of Palestinian deaths and destruction in Gaza is extraordinary, and the consequences warrant serious scrutiny. But what exactly does that disparity establish? Taken alone, it establishes a difference in the number of people killed. It does not tell us everything necessary to determine whether the military operations responsible were lawful, indiscriminate, proportionate, or conducted with sufficient precautions. An aggregate death ratio does not distinguish civilians from combatants. It does not identify individual military objectives. It does not tell us what information commanders possessed when deciding to attack, what alternatives were available, or what civilian harm was anticipated.

Nor does the difference in casualties, considered in isolation, establish that the party inflicting more deaths necessarily violated international humanitarian law. These distinctions matter, and a serious analysis should acknowledge them. But there is an equally important point: **the fact that a statistic does not independently prove a conclusion does not mean it provides no legitimate reason to investigate that conclusion.** Suppose a hospital reports an unusually high mortality rate among patients undergoing a particular procedure. That statistic alone would not establish medical malpractice. Perhaps the hospital treats unusually severe cases. Perhaps its patients have additional risk factors. Perhaps the procedures are performed under circumstances that make adverse outcomes more likely.

All of these explanations might be relevant. But nobody reasoning responsibly would conclude that the mortality rate therefore deserves no investigation. On the contrary, the unusual outcome would provide a reason to examine the underlying causes. What kinds of patients were being treated? What procedures were performed? What decisions contributed to the outcomes? Were accepted standards followed? Were preventable deaths occurring? The questions are justified by the need to understand the observed results, even before the answers are known. The same general reasoning applies to military casualties. A substantial disparity in deaths does not settle the question of wrongdoing. But it can provide an important reason to investigate the circumstances under which people were killed.

### What the laws of war ask us to examine

One of the most significant problems with the response "things happen in war" is that it treats civilian casualties as though their occurrence largely removes them from further scrutiny. But international humanitarian law operates on a very different premise. It recognizes that armed conflicts may involve military necessity and that attacks against legitimate military objectives can result in incidental civilian harm. At the same time, it imposes restrictions on how military operations may be conducted. The existence of a legitimate military objective does not provide unlimited permission to pursue it through any available means. According to the [International Committee of the Red Cross](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/FAQ-icrc-work-Israel-and-occupied-territories), three foundational principles govern the conduct of hostilities: distinction, proportionality, and precautions.

Each is directly relevant to the questions I raised. **The principle of distinction** requires parties to an armed conflict to distinguish between civilians and combatants, and between civilian objects and military objectives. Civilians may not be deliberately targeted as such. Neither may civilian objects, unless their actual use or other relevant circumstances make them legitimate military objectives under the applicable rules. Attacks that cannot be directed at a specific military objective, or whose effects cannot be limited as required by international humanitarian law, are prohibited as indiscriminate. This immediately introduces questions that cannot be answered by observing that war is inherently destructive.

Were the people or objects attacked legitimate military targets? What evidence established their status? Were the weapons and methods used capable of distinguishing those targets from surrounding civilians?

**The principle of proportionality** imposes another essential restriction. Even when attacking a legitimate military objective, the expected incidental civilian harm must not be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. This is an important distinction because proportionality, in its legal sense, is not determined by comparing the total number of Palestinians and Israelis killed. It is not a requirement that casualties be numerically equal between opposing sides. Nor is it a calculation in which the number of civilian deaths is compared directly with the number of enemy combatants killed.

Instead, proportionality concerns the anticipated civilian harm and military advantage associated with an attack, assessed using the information reasonably available at the time. A military operation can be directed at a legitimate target and still violate the principle of proportionality if the expected incidental civilian harm is excessive relative to the anticipated concrete and direct military advantage. Conversely, a high number of casualties does not automatically establish that the legal proportionality test was violated. The relevant circumstances must be examined. That is precisely why investigation is necessary.

**The principle of precautions** is perhaps the most directly relevant to the argument I was making during the conversation.

International humanitarian law requires parties to take all feasible precautions to avoid, and in any event minimize, incidental civilian deaths, injuries, and damage to civilian objects. This includes verifying military targets, evaluating anticipated civilian harm, considering the weapons and methods used, providing effective warnings when required, and canceling or suspending attacks when it becomes apparent that the relevant legal requirements would be violated. Where a choice is possible between military objectives offering a similar military advantage, the objective expected to present less danger to civilians must be selected. These are not abstract standards invented by critics of a particular military campaign.

They are established requirements of the laws governing armed conflict. And they lead directly to the kinds of questions I asked. Could the military objective have been achieved through different tactics? Could different weapons have reduced civilian harm? Could the operation have been conducted at another time? Was sufficient intelligence available to verify the intended target? Were civilian casualties anticipated, and what precautions were undertaken to minimize them? These are not peripheral considerations. They are central to determining whether military operations were conducted in accordance with applicable legal obligations. In other words, **my original question about whether alternative methods could have reduced civilian deaths was not an unreasonable demand for perfect warfare. It was closely related to the actual standards governing how warfare must be conducted.**

### The limits of military necessity

A possible response to this argument is that Israel faced a genuine security threat following the Hamas-led attacks of October 7, 2023. That threat should not be dismissed. Hamas and other armed groups killed Israeli civilians, took hostages, and committed serious violations of international humanitarian law. Israel has legitimate security interests, including protecting its population against further armed attacks. But acknowledging those interests does not answer whether every method employed to pursue them was lawful or justified. The distinction is essential. A legitimate objective does not automatically justify every action taken in pursuit of that objective.

Consider a police operation intended to apprehend a dangerous armed suspect. The objective may be legitimate and urgent. The suspect may pose a serious threat to public safety. Officers may face difficult decisions under uncertain conditions. But none of that establishes that every possible use of force would be justified. We would still ask whether the force used was necessary under the circumstances, whether the operation endangered uninvolved people, and whether reasonable protective measures were available. The analogy is imperfect because policing and armed conflict operate under different legal frameworks. Nevertheless, the general distinction remains useful: the legitimacy of an objective and the legitimacy of the methods used to achieve it are separate questions.

The same principle applies to military operations. Even where an armed group operates within densely populated civilian areas, the presence of military targets does not eliminate civilian protections. Civilians do not automatically lose their protection merely because fighters or military assets are present nearby.

### The practical question of feasible precautions

This brings us to the question my interlocutor refused to engage: could alternative military decisions have achieved the relevant objectives while reducing civilian casualties? There is an important distinction between asking whether the military acted *optimally* and asking whether it took *all feasible precautions* to minimize civilian harm. Optimality is an exceptionally demanding standard. In complex and uncertain environments, identifying the single best possible strategy may be impossible, even with extensive information and hindsight. A military operation might be imperfect without necessarily being unlawful. But the fact that perfect outcomes are impossible does not eliminate the obligation to take feasible precautions.

The word *feasible* is important. It recognizes that military decisions occur under constraints. Commanders may face urgent threats, incomplete intelligence, limited resources, and competing risks. A theoretical alternative that could not reasonably have been implemented under the circumstances would not establish that the original decision was unjustified. But where less harmful alternatives were practically available, their existence can be relevant to assessing the precautions taken. This creates an identifiable subject for investigation. What information was available before the attack? What military advantage was expected? Were alternative targets, weapons, methods, or timings considered? Would those alternatives have offered a comparable military advantage with less expected danger to civilians?

These questions need not be answered through speculation alone. They can be examined through operational records, available intelligence, witness testimony, weapons analysis, military expertise, forensic evidence, and independent investigations. Some relevant information may be classified or otherwise inaccessible, making complete public assessment difficult. But the appropriate conclusion from that difficulty is that certain questions remain unresolved—not that the existing military decisions should automatically be presumed justified. It is also important to distinguish between evaluating individual attacks and evaluating the broader military campaign. A strategy might succeed in degrading an armed group's capabilities while simultaneously causing devastating civilian harm, creating long-term instability, or failing to produce sustainable security.

These outcomes raise legitimate strategic and political questions even when they do not, by themselves, determine the legality of individual attacks. Did the campaign achieve its stated objectives? Were the consequences anticipated? Were the long-term security effects considered? Were approaches involving different operational methods or combinations of military, diplomatic, and humanitarian measures adequately evaluated? These are questions of effectiveness and public accountability. They do not require pretending that an obvious or painless alternative was available. They require taking the consequences of military decisions seriously enough to investigate whether those decisions were warranted.


<a id="psychology"></a>
## 6. Why a challenge to a belief can feel like a threat

Up to this point, I have focused primarily on the logical structure of the conversation: how questions were redirected, how the burden of justification was shifted, and how my inability to provide a complete alternative military strategy was treated as grounds for dismissing my concerns. But identifying these reasoning errors raises another question. Why do people reason this way? Why would someone respond to an invitation to investigate a consequential decision by repeatedly redirecting the conversation away from the evidence? Why does a request for explanation sometimes seem to provoke defensiveness rather than curiosity? And why might a person regard their ability to dismiss a question as equivalent to having answered it?

It would be tempting to conclude that such responses simply reflect intellectual dishonesty, an unwillingness to think critically, or a deliberate attempt to manipulate the discussion.

### Motivated reasoning: when an answer comes before the inquiry

One of the most useful concepts for understanding this phenomenon is *motivated reasoning*. In her influential 1990 paper, *[The Case for Motivated Reasoning](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.108.3.480)*, psychologist Ziva Kunda examined how people's motivations can influence the cognitive processes they use to evaluate information and construct judgments. The central insight is that reasoning is not always directed exclusively toward discovering what is true. Sometimes people are also motivated to arrive at a particular conclusion. These motivations can influence which evidence they notice, which explanations they find persuasive, which objections they consider important, and how rigorously they scrutinize information that challenges their existing beliefs.

This does not necessarily involve consciously deciding to disregard the truth. A person may genuinely believe they are evaluating evidence fairly while applying different standards to information that supports or threatens their preferred conclusion. Consider two different approaches to a controversial question. In the first, someone begins by asking: "What does the available evidence suggest, and what conclusion should I draw from it?" In the second, someone begins with an established conclusion and, perhaps without consciously realizing it, approaches the evidence through a different question: "How can I reconcile this information with what I already believe?"

These approaches can produce very different reasoning processes. The first invites the possibility that the evidence may require a change in belief. The second encourages the preservation of an existing belief, even if doing so requires introducing qualifications, exceptions, or alternative explanations that would receive much greater scrutiny in another context. This distinction is especially relevant to the conversation I experienced. My questions about Palestinian casualties did not require my interlocutor to immediately conclude that Israel's military conduct was unjustified. They simply invited examination of that possibility. Yet the responses consistently moved away from the proposed investigation.

Rather than examining whether civilian deaths were avoidable, the conversation shifted toward the inevitability of casualties in war. Rather than evaluating the conduct of particular military operations, it shifted toward whether Israel or Hamas was preferable. Rather than addressing the evidence concerning Israel's decisions, it shifted toward Iran's wrongdoing. And when I continued to ask whether the strategy had been adequately justified, the discussion shifted toward whether I possessed the expertise to devise a replacement. Each response had the potential to preserve the original favorable assessment of Israel's conduct without requiring that assessment to be subjected to the scrutiny I was proposing.

This is consistent with motivated reasoning, although the exchange alone cannot establish that motivated reasoning was the cause. The distinction matters. A series of weak arguments does not reveal a person's internal psychological state. But it can reveal a pattern of reasoning whose effects resemble those studied by psychologists. **The central danger of motivated reasoning is not that people necessarily invent evidence. It is that they may evaluate evidence according to standards influenced by the conclusions they hope to preserve.**

### Motivated skepticism and unequal standards

A particularly revealing form of motivated reasoning involves applying different standards of skepticism to claims depending on whether those claims support or challenge an existing position. Political scientists Charles Taber and Milton Lodge examined this phenomenon in their 2006 study, *[Motivated Skepticism in the Evaluation of Political Beliefs](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5907.2006.00214.x)*. Their research found that participants evaluating politically controversial arguments tended to assess information more favorably when it supported their existing attitudes and to scrutinize opposing arguments more critically. Participants also showed tendencies to seek out confirming information and generate counterarguments against information that challenged their positions. The broader implication is that people can approach the same subject with substantially different evidentiary expectations depending on which conclusion is being defended.

This suggests an especially interesting way to interpret the final exchange in my conversation. When I questioned whether Israel's military strategy adequately minimized civilian harm, I was effectively asked to establish a highly demanding counterfactual claim: that I could devise a better military strategy. But my interlocutor did not appear to apply an equally demanding standard to his own position. He did not demonstrate that Israel's existing strategy was the least harmful feasible option. He did not provide evidence establishing that alternative approaches had been considered and reasonably rejected. He did not explain how the expected civilian harm of particular operations had been evaluated.

Instead, my inability to demonstrate a superior alternative was treated as sufficient reason to dismiss the concerns. This reveals an asymmetry in evidentiary expectations. The existing strategy was defended without a detailed demonstration of its necessity, while criticism of that strategy was expected to satisfy a much more demanding test. Of course, neither participant should be required to prove more than their actual claims entail. The person defending Israel's conduct need not establish that every military decision was perfect, just as I need not demonstrate that every military decision was wrong. But when someone confidently claims that an existing strategy was justified, while requiring a critic to eliminate every possible justification before questioning it, the two positions are no longer being evaluated under equivalent standards.

The problem is not skepticism itself. Skepticism is valuable. The problem is *selective skepticism*: demanding rigorous evidence for conclusions one dislikes while accepting much weaker support for conclusions one prefers. **A commitment to critical thinking requires that our standards of evidence remain consistent even when the conclusions threaten our existing beliefs.** Otherwise, skepticism ceases to function as a method of discovering truth and becomes an instrument for defending conclusions.

### When factual criticism becomes a loyalty test

Motivated reasoning helps explain how people may selectively evaluate evidence. But it does not fully explain why some topics provoke such intense resistance to reconsideration. For that, another concept is useful: *identity-protective cognition*. Researchers studying cultural cognition, including Dan Kahan and his colleagues, have explored how people's judgments can be influenced by the relationship between information and their social identities or cultural commitments. The basic proposition is that beliefs are not always held as isolated assessments of factual evidence. Sometimes they are embedded within a broader understanding of who we are, which communities we belong to, and which values we regard as fundamental.

When this happens, questioning a belief can feel like questioning something much larger than a factual proposition. A political position may become intertwined with loyalty, moral character, social belonging, historical memory, or a person's understanding of justice. Under those circumstances, information that threatens the belief may also seem to threaten the identity connected to it. Consider someone for whom support for Israel is closely associated with concern for Jewish security, the historical persecution of Jewish people, or the importance of a Jewish national homeland. Those concerns may be deeply sincere, historically grounded, and morally significant.

But it is possible for a person to become so attached to defending a particular political or military institution that criticism of the institution is experienced as a challenge to the underlying values associated with it. A question about the proportionality of a military operation can then be heard not simply as a question about military conduct, but as an attack on Israel's legitimacy, its population's security, or the moral commitments of the person defending it. This does not mean that Zionism necessarily produces defensive reasoning. Nor does it mean that criticism of Israel is always free from prejudice, misinformation, or hostility toward Jewish people.

Those possibilities must be assessed on their own merits. The relevant psychological point is more general: when political judgments become closely connected to identity, the perceived stakes of changing one's mind can become much greater. This dynamic is not limited to supporters of Israel. A Palestinian nationalist might defensively reject credible evidence of wrongdoing by a Palestinian armed group. A committed supporter of a political party might minimize misconduct by its leaders. A person deeply invested in a social movement might resist legitimate criticism of its tactics. The mechanism is not defined by the political position involved, but by the relationship between that position and the person's sense of identity.

In my conversation, the question "Would you rather have Hamas or Israel?" was particularly interesting in this respect. I had raised concerns about specific military conduct. The response transformed those concerns into a question about which actor I preferred. In effect, the conversation moved from evaluating a decision to declaring an allegiance. This is precisely the kind of shift that can occur when questions about conduct become entangled with questions of identity. If criticism is interpreted as evidence that someone belongs to an opposing camp, its factual content can become secondary to its perceived social meaning.

Rather than asking whether the criticism is well supported, the listener may begin asking what the criticism implies about the critic. **The question changes from "Is this claim true?" to "What does accepting this claim mean about whose side I am on?"** And once that transformation occurs, rational disagreement becomes considerably more difficult.

### Cognitive dissonance and defensive explanations

Another relevant concept is *cognitive dissonance*, first developed by psychologist Leon Festinger. Cognitive dissonance refers to the psychological discomfort that can arise when a person holds conflicting beliefs, attitudes, or commitments, or encounters information that creates a meaningful inconsistency within their existing understanding. People may respond by revising their beliefs, changing their behavior, introducing additional explanations, or reducing the perceived importance of the conflicting information. Not every encounter with contrary evidence produces cognitive dissonance. Nor does dissonance necessarily lead to irrationality. It can sometimes motivate genuine reflection and a productive reconsideration of existing beliefs. But it can also encourage attempts to preserve psychological consistency without fully confronting the underlying contradiction.

Consider a hypothetical person who holds two important beliefs: "Israel's military campaign is fundamentally necessary and morally defensible." And: "The large-scale, avoidable killing of civilians is morally unacceptable." These beliefs are not inherently contradictory. A person might reasonably distinguish between unavoidable incidental civilian harm during lawful military operations and harm resulting from unlawful or unnecessarily destructive decisions. But suppose that person encounters substantial evidence suggesting that some civilian deaths may have been foreseeable, excessive, or preventable. The evidence could create tension between their favorable assessment of the military campaign and their commitment to civilian protection.

One response would be to examine the evidence and determine whether their assessment of the campaign should change. Another would be to preserve the original assessment by introducing explanations that make reconsideration seem unnecessary. "War is terrible." "Hamas is worse." "Iran is worse." "You don't understand military strategy." Again, these statements may contain truths or raise considerations worthy of examination. But when they substitute for evaluating the disturbing evidence, they can also serve to reduce the pressure to reconsider an established conclusion. The person need not consciously recognize this process. They may simply experience the defensive responses as more persuasive or relevant than the original questions.

What makes cognitive dissonance an especially useful concept here is that it provides a possible explanation for why an analytical question can provoke a response that seems disproportionately defensive. If the question is understood only as an invitation to evaluate evidence, the reasonable response is to examine the evidence. If the question is experienced as a threat to a valued belief, however, the immediate psychological priority may shift toward resolving that threat. But I want to emphasize the limits of applying this explanation to my particular interlocutor. I cannot know whether he experienced cognitive dissonance. I cannot know how closely his views about Israel were connected to his identity, or whether his responses were motivated by discomfort, limited information, genuine strategic disagreement, or some combination of factors.

## 7. The strongest objection: alternatives really do matter

Up to this point, I have examined the ways in which my interlocutor's responses redirected the conversation away from the questions I was raising. I have argued that demanding a comprehensive alternative military strategy as a prerequisite for criticism is logically unjustified, and that such demands can function as a mechanism for protecting existing beliefs from scrutiny. But there is an important possibility that deserves more serious consideration. What if my interlocutor was attempting, however imperfectly, to raise a legitimate objection? What if his demand that I propose an alternative was not entirely disconnected from the question I was asking, but instead reflected a real difficulty inherent in evaluating military decisions?

After all, it is easy to criticize the consequences of a strategy. It is considerably more difficult to establish what should have been done differently, particularly when the alternatives involve competing risks, incomplete information, and an adversary actively attempting to frustrate one's objectives. If I am going to criticize someone's reasoning for failing to engage with opposing arguments, I should be willing to subject my own position to the strongest version of the objection being raised.

### A stronger version of the objection

Imagine that my interlocutor had said something closer to the following:

> “I understand why the scale of Palestinian casualties concerns you. But Israel faced a real security problem after Hamas killed civilians and took hostages. Hamas fighters operated through tunnels and in densely populated areas, which complicated the military response. Airstrikes risk civilian casualties; ground operations risk civilians and soldiers in other ways; targeted raids may be insufficient for some objectives; and ceasefires or negotiations raise their own unresolved security questions. Before concluding that Israel’s chosen strategy was unnecessarily destructive, shouldn’t we examine which alternatives were realistically available and what their consequences might have been?”

This is a substantially stronger argument. It acknowledges the humanitarian concern rather than dismissing it, identifies relevant strategic constraints, and recognizes that alternatives must be evaluated not merely by their theoretical appeal, but by their practical feasibility and likely consequences. Most importantly, it asks a question that is directly relevant to the original inquiry. And my answer would be straightforward: **Yes. We absolutely should examine those alternatives. That was precisely what I was proposing.** The strength of this reconstructed objection is that it invites an investigation rather than attempting to terminate one. It identifies areas of uncertainty that both participants should take seriously. It also exposes a genuine weakness that can arise in criticism of military operations: evaluating a strategy entirely by its undesirable consequences, without adequately considering the consequences of feasible alternatives.

That weakness deserves attention, because the quality of a decision cannot be read directly from its eventual outcome.

### A tragic outcome is not automatically a bad decision

One of the most important principles in evaluating decisions is that an undesirable outcome does not necessarily establish that the decision producing it was unreasonable. This is sometimes described as the problem of *outcome bias*. We may judge a decision harshly because we know that its consequences were tragic, even when the decision was made under conditions where those consequences could not reasonably have been anticipated or avoided. Conversely, we may regard a decision favorably because it produced a good result, even when the reasoning behind it was reckless or poorly informed. But the quality of a decision and the quality of its eventual outcome are not identical.

Consider an emergency physician treating a critically injured patient. The physician must choose among several interventions, each carrying risks. Some may be more aggressive, others more conservative. The patient's condition may be deteriorating, and the available information may be incomplete. Suppose the physician chooses an intervention that carries substantial risks, and the patient dies. That tragic outcome does not, by itself, establish that the physician made the wrong decision. A responsible evaluation would examine what the physician knew, what alternatives were available, what risks were reasonably foreseeable, and whether the decision complied with accepted professional standards.

Likewise, if a patient survives despite a reckless decision, the favorable outcome does not automatically justify the physician's conduct. The relevant question is whether the decision was reasonable in light of the information and alternatives available at the time. The same principle applies to military operations. The fact that a military campaign causes enormous destruction does not independently establish that every decision contributing to that destruction was strategically irrational or legally prohibited. War involves circumstances in which even carefully planned operations can cause grave harm. However, this principle also works in the opposite direction. The fact that commanders faced difficult decisions does not establish that the decisions they made were reasonable.

The existence of operational constraints does not prove that every method employed was necessary. And the possibility that some civilian casualties were unavoidable does not establish that all civilian casualties were unavoidable. **A difficult decision is not automatically a justified decision, just as a tragic outcome is not automatically proof of an unjustified one.** The purpose of investigation is to determine which conclusion the available evidence supports.

### Comparisons must be with feasible options

Here the request for alternatives becomes genuinely relevant. When evaluating a proposed course of action, it often makes sense to compare it against the other options reasonably available. A decision cannot be fully understood in isolation from the constraints under which it was made. Suppose military commanders face a choice between several methods of pursuing a legitimate objective. One option involves an airstrike. Another involves a ground operation. A third involves delaying the attack while gathering additional intelligence. Each option may involve different risks. An airstrike might reduce exposure of soldiers but increase risks to surrounding civilians. A ground operation might permit closer target verification in some circumstances while also exposing civilians to prolonged fighting. Delaying an operation might create an opportunity to obtain better information, but could also allow an adversary to escape or prepare further attacks.

There is no reason to assume in advance that one method is always superior. The correct choice depends on the circumstances. And those circumstances are exactly what a serious investigation should examine. But this reveals the crucial difference between two claims. The first is: "We cannot establish that the existing strategy was inferior without considering feasible alternatives." The second is: "Because you cannot personally identify a superior alternative, the existing strategy should be presumed justified." The first is a legitimate analytical consideration when making a comparative claim. The second is not. It substitutes a limitation in the critic's knowledge for evidence about the available alternatives.

If someone asserts that there was no feasible way to reduce civilian casualties while achieving a particular military objective, that is itself a substantive claim about the circumstances. It requires support. One must examine what alternatives were available, what tradeoffs they involved, and why they were considered inadequate. The fact that an ordinary citizen cannot immediately identify a better strategy does not answer those questions. Nor does the inability to establish a superior alternative eliminate other grounds for criticism. An attack can be unlawful because it deliberately targets civilians, for example, regardless of whether a critic has devised an alternative way to accomplish the broader campaign's objectives.

So the legitimate demand to consider alternatives must be kept within its proper scope. **Alternatives matter when they are relevant to the claim being evaluated. They do not become a universal prerequisite for questioning any existing decision.**

### Military effectiveness, morality, and law are distinct

There is another qualification that strengthens the opposing argument. When I asked whether Israel could have pursued its military objectives while minimizing civilian casualties, I was implicitly describing a problem involving more than one objective. Military operations are not ordinarily designed to minimize civilian casualties in isolation from every other consideration. They also involve legitimate military objectives, operational constraints, force protection, intelligence uncertainty, and the need to respond to threats. A strategy that produces fewer civilian casualties but completely fails to accomplish a necessary military objective may not be a straightforwardly superior strategy. Conversely, a strategy that achieves substantial military objectives while causing avoidable or excessive civilian harm may be strategically successful in one respect while remaining morally or legally indefensible in another.

This distinction is important because different standards are being applied. **Strategic effectiveness** asks whether an operation achieved its intended military or security objectives. **Moral justification** asks whether the action and its consequences can be defended according to relevant ethical principles. **Legal compliance** asks whether the operation satisfied the applicable rules of international humanitarian law. These questions overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A strategically effective operation can violate international law. A lawful operation may fail to achieve its broader strategic objectives. A decision might satisfy a particular legal requirement while still raising serious moral or political objections.

This is why the phrase "Was Israel minimizing civilian deaths?" requires some precision. If minimizing deaths means achieving the lowest possible casualty figure regardless of military circumstances, that is not an accurate statement of the applicable legal standard. International humanitarian law does not require military commanders to abandon every legitimate objective whenever civilian casualties are possible. But it does impose binding restrictions on attacks, including the principles of distinction and proportionality and the obligation to take all feasible precautions to avoid or minimize incidental civilian harm. It prohibits certain actions outright, regardless of their strategic utility.

And it requires that decisions be assessed in light of the relevant information and circumstances. These obligations are not suspended merely because an adversary operates in civilian areas or commits its own violations of international law.

### Avoiding imaginary easy solutions

A serious critique must also avoid treating hypothetical alternatives as though their feasibility were self-evident. It is easy to suggest that Israel should have relied more heavily on targeted raids, intelligence operations, negotiations, different targeting methods, or other approaches. But identifying a conceivable alternative is not the same as demonstrating that it was feasible or superior under the circumstances. A targeted raid may reduce collateral damage in one situation while creating greater danger in another. An intelligence operation may require information that was unavailable. A negotiated arrangement may depend on the willingness of other parties to cooperate.

Delaying an attack may reduce some risks while creating others. There are also questions of scale. An approach capable of neutralizing a small number of specific threats may not necessarily achieve the broader military objectives associated with a sustained campaign. These difficulties cannot simply be ignored. If I claimed that a particular alternative would definitely have achieved Israel's stated objectives with substantially fewer civilian deaths, I would have an obligation to demonstrate why that claim was credible. And if I could not provide supporting evidence, my confidence should be reduced accordingly. This is a legitimate demand for intellectual discipline.

But again, it does not establish that the existing military strategy was justified. It merely establishes that evaluating alternatives requires serious analysis. There is a symmetrical error worth identifying here.

### Reciprocity in the demands we make

I think this is where the most productive lesson emerges. The best response to my interlocutor would not have been to insist that alternatives were irrelevant. They were relevant. Nor would it have been to claim that the scale of Palestinian deaths conclusively established that Israel's strategy was unlawful or unnecessarily destructive. The aggregate casualty disparity alone cannot establish those conclusions. Instead, I could have acknowledged the legitimate concern embedded within his objection while refusing the unreasonable burden he attempted to impose. I might have said: "You're right that military alternatives have to be evaluated realistically. I'm not assuming that there was some obvious strategy that would have eliminated Hamas's threat without substantial risk or civilian harm.

"But that's exactly why I think this deserves investigation. "If you believe Israel's strategy was necessary, what evidence supports that conclusion? What alternatives were available? What constraints made less destructive approaches infeasible? How were civilian casualties considered in those decisions? "I'm open to the possibility that some operations were justified under circumstances I don't fully understand. But are you open to the possibility that some were unnecessarily destructive or failed to meet the relevant legal requirements? "Because if neither of us knows enough to answer those questions confidently, shouldn't that make us more interested in investigating them, rather than less?"

That is the difference between the strongest form of the alternative-strategy objection and the actual rejoinder I received. The strong version names constraints that deserve investigation; the weak version treats my lack of an immediate solution as grounds to stop. A demand for alternatives is constructive when it helps us compare real options. It becomes a deflection when it is used to make criticism contingent on personally solving the whole military problem.

## 8. What a productive disagreement might look like

Having examined the logical failures, psychological mechanisms, and legitimate counterarguments involved in my original exchange, it is worth considering a different possibility. What would the conversation have looked like if both participants had approached it as an opportunity to investigate a difficult question rather than defend their respective positions? This question matters because identifying defective reasoning is only part of the challenge. It is relatively easy to recognize when someone has changed the subject, shifted the burden of proof, or substituted an attack on a critic's qualifications for an answer to their argument. It is considerably more difficult to establish a conversational framework that encourages better reasoning in the first place.

And that distinction is important. The goal of a productive discussion should not simply be to become more skilled at identifying an opponent's fallacies. Doing so may help us recognize when an argument fails, but it does not necessarily bring us closer to understanding the underlying issue.

### Define the question before debating it

The first requirement of productive disagreement is conceptual clarity. Before evaluating a claim, both participants should understand what the claim actually is. This may sound obvious, but much of the dysfunction in my original conversation arose because the subject repeatedly changed without either participant explicitly recognizing the shift. I began by asking whether the enormous scale of Palestinian casualties warranted scrutiny of Israel's military decisions. My interlocutor responded as though I were questioning Israel's right to protect its population, expressing sympathy for Hamas, or asserting that I possessed a superior military strategy. These were not the positions I had advanced.

A more productive exchange would have begun by clarifying the precise question under consideration. I might have said: "I'm not arguing that Israel has no legitimate security concerns, and I'm not defending Hamas's attacks against civilians. I'm asking whether Israel's military response has been conducted in a way that adequately protects civilian life, and whether less destructive methods were feasible." This clarification would serve two purposes. First, it would distinguish criticism of military conduct from broader judgments about political legitimacy or allegiance. Second, it would establish that my position was investigative rather than conclusory. I was not claiming to have already established that every military operation was unlawful or that a particular alternative would necessarily have produced better results.

I was asking what evidence would be necessary to determine whether the existing strategy was justified. Once this distinction is established, a productive interlocutor can respond to the actual question. They might say: "I think the military constraints were more severe than you're accounting for." Or: "I agree the scale of civilian harm deserves investigation, but I don't think the casualty ratio tells us enough to judge the campaign." Either response would create an opportunity for substantive discussion. The first introduces a factual claim about operational constraints. The second introduces an evidentiary challenge concerning how casualty statistics should be interpreted.

Both can be examined. Neither requires abandoning the original inquiry. **Productive disagreement begins by ensuring that both participants are evaluating the same proposition.** Without that foundation, a conversation can generate considerable argument without ever establishing what the disagreement is actually about.

### Separate factual, legal, strategic, and moral issues

The second improvement would be to distinguish the different kinds of questions involved. Discussions about warfare frequently combine factual claims, strategic assessments, moral judgments, and legal conclusions. These dimensions are related, but they are not interchangeable. Consider the statement: "Israel's military campaign has caused an unacceptable level of civilian harm." This contains several questions that should be distinguished. There is an empirical question: How many civilians have been killed or injured, and under what circumstances? There is a causal question: Which military decisions contributed to those outcomes? There is a strategic question: What objectives were being pursued, and were the methods effective?

There is a legal question: Did particular attacks comply with the applicable principles of international humanitarian law? And there is a moral question: Even where conduct might satisfy minimum legal requirements, should it nevertheless be condemned as unjustified or excessively destructive? Each question may require different evidence and different standards of evaluation. For example, determining the number of people killed requires reliable casualty records and an understanding of their limitations. Determining whether particular attacks were disproportionate under international humanitarian law requires examining anticipated civilian harm and concrete military advantage in the circumstances of those attacks.

Determining whether a broader military strategy was effective requires examining its stated objectives and actual consequences. And determining whether a campaign was morally justified requires ethical judgments that cannot be reduced to casualty statistics alone. A discussion becomes confused when conclusions from one category are treated as though they automatically settle another. A high death ratio does not independently establish that every military operation was unlawful. But neither does the existence of a legitimate military objective establish that all methods employed to pursue it were morally or legally acceptable. Likewise, the fact that Hamas committed atrocities against Israeli civilians is relevant to understanding Israel's security concerns, but does not determine whether Israel's subsequent conduct complied with its own obligations.

By separating these questions, participants can identify more precisely where they agree and disagree. Perhaps both agree that the civilian death toll is catastrophic but disagree about how much of that harm was avoidable. Perhaps both agree that Hamas posed a serious threat but disagree about whether Israel's chosen response was strategically effective. Perhaps both agree on the factual circumstances of a particular attack but disagree about its moral implications.

### Turn challenges into investigable questions

The most practical improvement would be to transform rhetorical challenges into questions that can actually be investigated. Consider the difference between the following responses: "Well, what would you do instead?" And: "What alternatives were realistically available, and what evidence suggests they would have reduced civilian harm without undermining legitimate military objectives?" The first question may be legitimate in some contexts, but when delivered as a challenge to the critic's qualifications, it can become a conversational dead end. The second identifies a subject for investigation. It asks about feasible options, likely consequences, and the evidence needed to compare competing decisions.

It also permits a perfectly reasonable answer: "I don't know yet. Let's examine what the available evidence shows." That answer should not be treated as a defeat. It is often the appropriate starting point for serious inquiry. In the context of Israel's military campaign, we could have identified several concrete areas for investigation. We could examine the distinction between civilian and combatant casualties, the reliability of available estimates, and the circumstances in which deaths occurred. We could investigate particular military operations, including their intended objectives, anticipated civilian harm, and the precautions undertaken. We could consider what alternative tactics were realistically available, what limitations applied to those alternatives, and whether the decisions made were consistent with international humanitarian law.

We could also examine the broader effectiveness of the campaign, including whether its military achievements translated into sustainable security or whether its humanitarian and political consequences undermined those objectives.

### Ask what evidence would change each person’s mind

Perhaps the most revealing improvement would be to ask a question that is rarely heard in contentious political discussions: "What evidence would make you reconsider your position?" This question shifts attention from the conclusions people currently defend to the process through which those conclusions might change. It asks whether their beliefs are genuinely responsive to evidence. Suppose my interlocutor maintains that Israel's military campaign was conducted with appropriate precautions and that its civilian casualties were largely unavoidable given the operational circumstances. I might ask: "What kind of evidence would lead you to conclude that some military operations were unnecessarily destructive or failed to adequately protect civilians?"

A serious answer could identify several possibilities. Credible evidence that commanders knowingly disregarded foreseeable civilian harm might change his assessment. Evidence that attacks were conducted without adequate target verification could raise concerns. Evidence that less harmful military methods were feasible but were not considered might undermine confidence in the precautions taken. He might also identify evidence that would strengthen his current position, such as credible documentation of legitimate military targets, targeting procedures, operational constraints, and precautions implemented before particular attacks. These answers would provide something valuable: a clearer understanding of the standards by which he is evaluating the conduct.

But the question must also be applied to me. What evidence would lead me to reconsider my own assessment? I should be prepared to acknowledge that some apparently troubling outcomes may have explanations I do not presently understand. Evidence that a particular attack was directed at a legitimate military objective, that the anticipated civilian harm was not excessive relative to the concrete and direct military advantage, and that feasible precautions were taken would be relevant to my assessment of that operation. Evidence demonstrating that a proposed alternative was operationally infeasible could weaken a claim that the alternative should have been used.

And evidence revealing serious limitations in casualty estimates or other factual assumptions should prompt me to revise the conclusions that depend on them. This does not mean I must abandon a broader criticism of the campaign whenever evidence supports the lawfulness of one particular operation. Nor does it mean that every piece of contrary evidence requires treating the overall issue as evenly balanced.

### An alternative version of the conversation

Consider how a more productive version of the original exchange might have unfolded. It would not require either participant to abandon a conviction, only to make the claims and uncertainties explicit.

> **Me:** “The disparity between Palestinian and Israeli deaths troubles me. I know that an aggregate ratio cannot determine whether a specific attack was unlawful, but shouldn’t we investigate who was killed and whether the harm could have been reduced?”
>
> **Interlocutor:** “Yes, but we also need to account for the fact that Hamas operated in densely populated areas. Some alternatives may not have been feasible.”
>
> **Me:** “That’s fair. What evidence would help us understand those constraints and the precautions that were taken?”
>
> **Interlocutor:** “Some of the military information might be inaccessible to outsiders.”
>
> **Me:** “Then we should be careful in both directions. We cannot assume that a better strategy existed, but neither can we assume that the chosen one was the best feasible approach. What can independent investigations tell us?”

At this point, the conversation has become meaningfully different. Neither participant has abandoned their original concerns. Neither has been required to accept the other's conclusions. But the disagreement now concerns identifiable claims and evidence. We might investigate those claims and discover that we disagree about the reliability of particular sources. We might find evidence supporting some military decisions and undermining others. We might conclude that the information available is insufficient for confident judgment about certain operations.

Or we might eventually reach different conclusions about the broader campaign despite agreeing on substantial portions of the evidence. Any of these outcomes would represent progress. The purpose of a productive conversation is not necessarily to eliminate disagreement. It is to improve the quality of the understanding from which disagreement proceeds.

### Know when inquiry is no longer possible

There is, however, a practical limitation to this approach. A productive discussion requires some degree of reciprocal willingness to engage. One participant cannot single-handedly transform every argument into a sincere investigation. If someone repeatedly changes the subject, dismisses questions without addressing them, or refuses to apply consistent standards of evidence, there may be little value in continuing indefinitely. This raises an important question: how can we distinguish a difficult but productive disagreement from one that has ceased to advance understanding? I think several indicators are useful. A productive disagreement generally becomes more precise over time.

Participants begin with broad claims, identify the specific points of contention, clarify relevant evidence, and discover which uncertainties actually matter. Even when they remain opposed, they should become better able to explain what the other person believes and why. An unproductive disagreement often moves in the opposite direction. The subject becomes increasingly diffuse. New accusations replace unresolved questions. Participants become preoccupied with exposing each other's ignorance, motives, or political affiliations. Rather than clarifying the disagreement, the conversation generates additional opportunities for misunderstanding. One useful test is to ask whether the original question has actually been addressed.

If I raise concerns about civilian casualties and the response becomes a discussion about Iran, political allegiance, or my personal military expertise, I can reasonably attempt to return to the subject. For example: "I understand those considerations may be relevant to other questions, but they don't answer what I'm asking. What evidence supports your conclusion that the civilian harm resulting from these operations was adequately minimized?" If the person engages that question, the discussion may move forward. If the response is simply another demand that I produce a military strategy, I can identify the distinction once more.

"I'm not claiming to know the best alternative. I'm asking how you know the existing approach was justified."

Disagreement need not end in consensus to be valuable. Even when we disagree, we can finish with a clearer account of which propositions are supported, which remain disputed, and what evidence would resolve the uncertainty. That is more productive than treating a concession of incomplete knowledge as a rhetorical victory.

## 9. From a conversational tactic to institutional deference

Although this case study began with a conversation about Israel's military conduct, the reasoning pattern it illustrates is neither unique to that conflict nor confined to political disagreements. In fact, one of the most revealing aspects of the exchange is how easily its underlying logic can be transferred to other areas of life. A person questions a consequential decision. Someone defending the decision invokes the complexity of the problem or the expertise of those responsible. The critic is then challenged to produce a superior solution. When they cannot, their original concern is dismissed. This pattern can appear in discussions about healthcare, economic policy, business management, technological safety, and government decision-making.

And in each setting, it raises the same fundamental question: **Does the inability to personally solve a complex problem invalidate one's ability to recognize, question, or demand an explanation for the decisions being made about it?** The answer has implications extending far beyond the quality of individual arguments. It concerns the relationship between expertise and authority, the distribution of responsibility within institutions, and the conditions necessary for meaningful accountability.

### A patient is not required to become a surgeon

Consider a patient who has been advised to undergo an invasive medical procedure. The physician recommends surgery, explaining that the patient's condition requires intervention. The patient, concerned about the risks, asks whether less invasive treatments might be appropriate. "Is surgery absolutely necessary? Are there alternative treatments? What are the relative risks? Should I seek another opinion?" Suppose the physician responds: "Are you a surgeon?" "No." "Then tell me how you would treat the condition." "I don't know exactly how to treat it. That's why I'm consulting a medical professional." "Well, then you aren't qualified to question my recommendation."

The inadequacy of this response should be obvious. The patient's inability to practice medicine does not establish that the recommended surgery is necessary. Nor does the physician's expertise, however genuine, eliminate the responsibility to explain the diagnosis, available treatment options, associated risks, and reasons for recommending one course of action over another. Indeed, informed consent depends in part on patients receiving sufficient information to make meaningful decisions about their care. A patient need not possess the knowledge required to perform a procedure in order to evaluate whether they are willing to undergo it. This does not mean the patient is equally qualified to determine which treatment is medically appropriate.

Expertise matters enormously. A physician's professional assessment should generally carry much more weight than a patient's uninformed speculation about medical procedures. But placing appropriate weight on expertise is not the same as treating expert judgment as beyond examination. A patient may reasonably ask for supporting evidence, explanations of uncertainty, alternative treatment options, or a second professional opinion.

### A taxpayer is not required to design the economy

The same pattern appears in economic and political debates. Suppose a government introduces a major economic policy that imposes substantial costs on ordinary citizens. Perhaps the policy involves new taxes, significant reductions in public services, extensive subsidies, or an expensive industrial initiative. A citizen questions whether the policy is effective. "Are the projected benefits supported by evidence? Were less costly alternatives considered? Who bears the financial burden? What happens if the policy fails?" A defender responds: "Well, what's your economic plan? How would you manage the entire economy?" The implication is familiar. Unless the citizen can formulate a comprehensive alternative economic program, their criticism of the existing policy is supposedly invalid.

But this demand once again substitutes a different question. The citizen may be questioning the assumptions underlying a particular policy, the reliability of its projected benefits, or the fairness of its consequences. Those questions do not require designing an entire economic system. A person can reasonably question whether a government expenditure is wasteful without knowing how to allocate every dollar in the national budget. They can ask whether a tax policy is regressive without possessing a complete alternative tax code. They can identify contradictions in an economic forecast without knowing how to construct a superior macroeconomic model.

Of course, if the person claims that a specific alternative policy would produce better outcomes, they should provide evidence supporting that claim.

### An employee is not required to run the company

Similar dynamics appear within organizations. Imagine an employee who notices that a company is committing substantial resources to a project that appears increasingly unlikely to succeed. Costs are rising, deadlines are repeatedly missed, and the projected benefits remain uncertain. The employee raises concerns. "Are we confident this project is still viable? Have we reconsidered the assumptions behind it? Would it make sense to evaluate whether continuing is the best use of our resources?" A manager responds: "Well, if you think you know better, why don't you come up with a complete business strategy?" The employee does not possess the authority, information, or resources needed to formulate and implement a company-wide alternative.

But that limitation has no direct bearing on whether the concerns about the existing project deserve examination. The project might be entirely justified. The rising costs might be anticipated, the delayed benefits might be substantial, and management might possess information unavailable to the employee. But those possibilities would need to be explained or examined. They are not established by the employee's inability to design a superior corporate strategy. This example introduces another dimension of the problem: the relationship between criticism and organizational loyalty. Within hierarchical institutions, questioning a decision may be interpreted as questioning the competence or authority of those responsible.

A manager who closely identifies with a project may experience criticism of the project as criticism of their own judgment. Under those circumstances, the conversation can shift from evaluating the project's merits to evaluating the employee's standing, experience, or qualifications. "Do you understand how complicated this is?" "Have you ever managed a project this large?"

### A user is not required to build a safer algorithm

The same reasoning becomes particularly relevant in technological development, where specialized knowledge is often highly concentrated. Consider a company deploying an automated system that will influence consequential decisions. Perhaps the system helps determine access to financial services, evaluates job applicants, or supports decisions in healthcare. Researchers, affected individuals, or members of the public raise concerns about its reliability, fairness, or safety. A defender responds: "Do you know how to build a better algorithm?" The critic may not. But that is not the question. The question is whether the system performs as claimed, whether its limitations are adequately understood, whether its outcomes have been appropriately evaluated, and whether its use creates unacceptable risks.

Those questions can be investigated through testing, independent audits, error analysis, documentation, and examination of real-world consequences. A person harmed by an automated decision should not need to understand the system's architecture before being entitled to question the decision. Likewise, a researcher identifying a serious safety vulnerability need not be capable of constructing an entirely new technological platform before the vulnerability deserves attention. There is a broader principle here. The complexity of a system does not eliminate the need for accountability. It often increases it. As systems become more complicated, outsiders may become less able to independently determine whether the claims made about them are accurate.

This creates a greater need for meaningful testing, transparency where appropriate, independent evaluation, and mechanisms for correcting errors. The alternative is to permit complexity itself to become a form of insulation. The more difficult a technology is to understand, the easier it becomes to dismiss questions by appealing to the critic's lack of expertise.

### The common structure

These examples share a recognizable structure. First, a consequential decision is made by someone possessing specialized knowledge, institutional authority, or access to information unavailable to the critic. Second, an observer identifies a possible problem and raises questions about the decision's justification, effectiveness, or consequences. Third, the defender invokes the complexity of the problem or the expertise of the decision-maker. Fourth, the observer is challenged to demonstrate the ability to produce a superior solution. Finally, when the observer cannot satisfy that demand, the original concern is dismissed. The result is an inversion of accountability. Instead of requiring those exercising authority to provide adequate justification for their decisions, the critic is required to demonstrate competence to replace them.

But the ability to question a decision and the ability to implement an alternative involve different kinds of responsibility. The person making a decision is responsible for exercising their authority appropriately. The person evaluating that decision is responsible for making accurate claims, presenting relevant evidence, and acknowledging the limits of their knowledge. Neither role automatically requires possessing all the capabilities of the other. A citizen questioning military conduct need not be a commander. A patient questioning a recommendation need not be a surgeon. A shareholder questioning financial management need not be a chief executive. And a person identifying a technological risk need not be capable of developing a complete replacement system.

In each case, the critic's obligation is to provide adequate support for the particular claims they are making. Their obligation is not to establish that they could personally assume responsibility for the entire institution. **The logical error is to confuse the qualifications necessary to make a decision with the qualifications necessary to demand that the decision be justified.**

### Accountability without universal access

An important qualification is necessary here. Demanding accountability does not mean that every individual is entitled to immediate access to every piece of information underlying a consequential decision. Some forms of expertise depend on confidential information. Medical decisions involve patient privacy. Businesses may possess proprietary information. Military operations may involve intelligence that cannot safely be disclosed. Nor does accountability require that every technical decision be submitted to public approval. Institutions often need specialized personnel who can act efficiently within established responsibilities. The relevant principle is not that expertise should be replaced by direct public control over every decision.

It is that expertise and authority should operate within systems capable of evaluating whether they are being exercised responsibly. Those systems may include professional standards, independent review, judicial oversight, external audits, legislative scrutiny, investigative journalism, internal reporting mechanisms, or other forms of institutional accountability. Different settings require different mechanisms. In medicine, professional guidelines, second opinions, and informed consent help patients evaluate recommendations. In business, financial reporting, independent audits, and governance processes provide opportunities to examine management decisions. In technology, testing, documentation, safety evaluations, and independent assessments can help identify weaknesses that developers may have overlooked.

In military affairs, compliance review, independent investigation, legislative oversight, and applicable international legal processes can help assess decisions that ordinary citizens are not equipped to evaluate directly. These mechanisms are imperfect. They can fail, be underfunded, encounter conflicts of interest, or lack access to necessary information. But their importance arises from a basic recognition: those exercising specialized authority should not be the sole judges of whether they have exercised it appropriately. The absence of individual expertise does not require blind trust. It requires appropriate methods of verification. And where adequate verification is unavailable, the responsible response may be to acknowledge uncertainty rather than adopt an unjustified presumption of correctness.

### The democratic cost of silencing nonexperts

The broader implications become especially serious in democratic political life. Modern governments exercise powers that no ordinary citizen could independently administer. They make decisions about national defense, public health, economic policy, infrastructure, intelligence, and countless other complex matters. Citizens generally lack the technical knowledge necessary to personally design complete alternatives in each domain. But democracy does not rest on the assumption that every citizen is an expert in government administration. It rests, in part, on the principle that political authority must remain subject to public evaluation and institutional accountability. Citizens may reasonably scrutinize government decisions, demand explanations, question official claims, support independent investigations, and advocate changes in policy.

They can do so without being qualified to personally perform the duties of every official whose decisions they question. Imagine a political system in which criticism of a military campaign were permitted only from those capable of designing superior military operations. Or one in which economic policies could be questioned only by individuals able to produce complete national budgets. Or one in which public concerns about a healthcare system could be dismissed unless the critics could independently design and administer its replacement. Such a system would dramatically narrow the scope of legitimate public participation. Expertise would cease to function primarily as a resource informing democratic judgment.

It would become a condition controlling who was permitted to exercise judgment at all. That would be a profound inversion of accountability. The public delegates authority because specialized decisions must be made. It does not follow that the public has surrendered its legitimate interest in how that authority is exercised.

The point is not that an outsider’s judgment should routinely outweigh expert knowledge. It is that expert knowledge should inform evaluation rather than prohibit it. When a critic presents a poorly supported claim, it can be challenged on its merits. When a decision-maker has a powerful justification, it can be offered through appropriate evidence or review. But the inability to design a replacement policy cannot, on its own, explain why the policy in place deserves confidence.

## 10. What uncertainty should make us do

What stayed with me from that conversation was not simply that someone disagreed with my assessment of Israel’s military conduct. What troubled me was how easily a question about evidence became a contest over whether I had earned the right to ask it. We moved from the scale of Palestinian deaths, civilian protection, military objectives, and feasible alternatives to a single admission: I could not personally design a better campaign. The admission was true; the conclusion drawn from it was not.

The lesson is not that critics are entitled to assume their suspicions correct, or that expert decisions should always be distrusted. In the case of Gaza, the death toll deserves serious examination. The relevant inquiries require evidence about the circumstances, the expected military advantage, foreseeable civilian harm, and feasible precautions. The same intellectual standard should govern anyone defending the operations and anyone criticizing them. Neither can turn what they do not know into proof of what they would prefer to believe.

There is a more demanding way to disagree. We can respect expertise without treating it as infallibility, recognize real constraints without assuming they justified every choice, and hold serious moral concerns without pretending they settle every empirical or legal question. We can ask what would change our minds and be willing to answer that question ourselves. A productive disagreement need not produce consensus; it should at least leave us clearer about which claims are supported, which are disputed, and what remains to be investigated.

The question I wanted to ask in that original conversation remains worth asking: *What evidence would show that the conduct under examination was justified, and what evidence would show that it was not?* Someone who believes Israel’s actions were necessary should be able to explain the grounds for that belief. Someone who believes they were unnecessarily destructive should also be prepared to substantiate the claim. Neither person has to arrive as a military commander, equipped with a complete alternative war plan, before the discussion can begin.

**The absence of a demonstrated alternative is not the presence of a justification.** When we respond to uncertainty by demanding better evidence, we make inquiry possible. When we use uncertainty to stop questions from being asked, we are no longer examining a belief. We are protecting it.
