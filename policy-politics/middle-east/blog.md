# When Criticism Requires a Replacement Plan: A Case Study in Defensive Reasoning

## I. Introduction: A Conversation That Stopped Being About the Evidence

Recently, I found myself in a political discussion that illustrated a peculiar and troubling pattern of reasoning—one that I suspect many people have encountered, particularly when discussing emotionally charged or ideologically divisive subjects.

The conversation concerned Israel's military campaign in Gaza, specifically the extraordinary disparity between Palestinian and Israeli deaths. As the discussion progressed, I became increasingly interested not only in the disagreement itself, but in the way my interlocutor responded to questions that challenged his position.

When I raised concerns about the scale of Palestinian casualties, the response was familiar:

"Things happen in war. Would you rather have Hamas or Israel? Yeah, Israel has done some bad stuff, but Iran and all of that is way worse."

There are several distinct arguments embedded in that response, each deserving its own examination. But rather than pursue those tangents, I attempted to redirect the conversation toward something more concrete: an inquiry into the military conduct itself.

I asked, in essence: *Doesn't a death ratio on the order of thirty Palestinians to one Israeli warrant serious scrutiny? Who, exactly, are the people being killed? How many are combatants, and how many are civilians? Is the military campaign being conducted in a way that adequately minimizes civilian casualties? Could alternative strategies achieve legitimate security objectives while producing less destruction and loss of life?*

These seemed to me reasonable questions. I wasn't claiming to possess military expertise, nor was I asserting that the casualty disparity, by itself, established the illegality of particular military operations. I was suggesting that such an extraordinary disparity should provoke investigation rather than casual dismissal.

More importantly, the questions were open to examination. We could investigate casualty data, the distinction between civilian and combatant deaths, military objectives, targeting decisions, international humanitarian law, and the feasibility of less destructive approaches. We could examine evidence supporting Israel's decisions, evidence challenging them, and the practical constraints involved in fighting an armed adversary in a densely populated environment.

In other words, there was an opportunity for substantive disagreement—one in which both participants might have learned something.

Instead, I received a different response:

"Well, you tell me what they should be doing then."

And when I acknowledged that I couldn't personally prescribe a comprehensive alternative military strategy, the reply was immediate:

"Yeah, see, you don't know."

It was this exchange that caught my attention.

Consider what had happened. I had questioned whether a military strategy was justified by its objectives, execution, and consequences. Rather than address those questions, my interlocutor demanded that I produce an alternative strategy. My inability to do so was then treated as though it invalidated the criticism itself.

Yet nothing about that exchange established whether the existing strategy was necessary, effective, lawful, or appropriately attentive to civilian life. Nothing established that less destructive alternatives were unavailable. Nothing even established that the original questions were misguided.

**The subject of the conversation had shifted from the justification of a military campaign to the qualifications of the person questioning it.**

And this shift was treated as a decisive argumentative victory.

What makes this interaction interesting is that the underlying pattern is not unique to debates about Israel and Palestine. Similar reasoning appears in discussions about government policy, healthcare, economics, corporate decision-making, and virtually any situation in which someone questions the judgment of an institution or authority.

A person identifies a potentially serious problem. Rather than examining the evidence, someone defending the existing decision demands a fully developed alternative. When the critic cannot immediately provide one, the original concern is dismissed.

There is an important distinction between reasonably asking someone to consider practical alternatives and insisting that criticism is illegitimate unless the critic can personally solve the problem. The former can deepen an investigation. The latter can prevent one from occurring.

That distinction is the subject of this essay.

What interests me is not simply the presence of a logical error, but the broader pattern of reasoning it reveals: how conversations can be redirected away from uncomfortable evidence, how the burden of justification can be transferred to the person asking questions, and how these maneuvers may function—consciously or otherwise—to protect established beliefs from scrutiny.

The central question, then, is not whether I could have devised a better military strategy.

It is this:

**Since when does the inability to provide a better solution constitute evidence that the existing solution is justified?**

## II. Two Fundamentally Different Questions

At the center of this exchange is a deceptively simple logical error: the conflation of questioning an existing course of action with claiming to know how to replace it.

These are two fundamentally different intellectual activities, and the distinction between them is essential to rational inquiry.

Consider the two questions at issue:

**Question A:** Is the existing military strategy justified, effective, necessary, and being conducted with adequate regard for civilian life?

**Question B:** Can I personally devise a superior military strategy that achieves the same objectives with fewer casualties?

At first glance, these questions may seem closely related. And indeed, they are. Evaluating a strategy often involves considering available alternatives, operational constraints, and the consequences of different decisions. But their relationship does not make them logically equivalent.

The first question concerns the justification of an existing decision. The second concerns my ability to formulate a replacement.

I can have legitimate reasons to question the former without possessing the technical expertise to accomplish the latter.

This distinction becomes particularly important when the decision being scrutinized involves specialized knowledge. Military operations, like medical procedures, engineering projects, and economic policies, require expertise that most ordinary citizens do not possess. But expertise is not a prerequisite for recognizing evidence that something may be wrong, nor does its absence eliminate the legitimacy of asking questions.

Suppose someone claims that a particular military operation was necessary, that its objectives justified its consequences, and that reasonable measures were taken to minimize civilian harm.

Those are substantive claims about the operation. They can be examined through evidence: the objectives pursued, the intelligence available, the expected and actual consequences, the precautions undertaken, and the alternatives reasonably available at the time.

Now suppose I question those claims.

I may be uncertain whether adequate precautions were taken. I may see evidence suggesting that civilian harm was foreseeable or potentially avoidable. I may ask whether different tactics, timing, or targeting decisions were considered.

None of this requires that I already know which alternative strategy would have been superior.

In fact, **the purpose of asking these questions is precisely to determine what the evidence supports.**

Demanding that I first supply the answer defeats the purpose of the inquiry.

### The logical error

We can express the reasoning underlying my interlocutor's response as a simple argument:

1. A critic questions whether Strategy A is justified.
2. The critic cannot provide a complete alternative, Strategy B.
3. Therefore, the critic's objection to Strategy A is invalid.

The problem is that the conclusion does not follow from the premises.

My inability to demonstrate that Strategy B would be better tells us something about the limits of my knowledge. It tells us nothing definitive about the merits of Strategy A.

Strategy A might be the best available option. It might be deeply flawed. It might be effective at achieving one objective while causing unnecessary harm in pursuit of another. Or it might involve tradeoffs that require more evidence before any responsible judgment can be made.

All of these possibilities remain open regardless of whether I can personally design its replacement.

**The absence of a demonstrated alternative is not the presence of a justification.**

There is also an important epistemological distinction between *not knowing that a better alternative exists* and *knowing that no better alternative exists*.

These statements may sound similar, but they represent radically different claims.

The first acknowledges uncertainty. The second asserts a conclusion about the available possibilities.

When someone responds to criticism by demanding an alternative and then treats the critic's inability to provide one as proof that the current approach is justified, they effectively convert the first statement into the second.

But that conversion is unwarranted.

To establish that no feasible superior alternative existed would require examining the relevant constraints, evidence, and options. It cannot be established merely by demonstrating that one participant in a conversation does not know what those alternatives might have been.

### The shifting burden of justification

This error becomes even more significant when we consider the burden of proof.

Suppose someone defends a military campaign on the grounds that its destructive consequences were necessary to achieve legitimate security objectives.

That position carries an evidentiary burden. The person defending it should be able to offer reasons for believing that the objectives, methods, and consequences were justified.

When I question whether those reasons are sufficient, I am not necessarily advancing a competing military strategy. I am examining the justification already being offered.

Yet the response "Well, what would you do instead?" changes the terms of the discussion.

Rather than explaining why the existing strategy was justified, my interlocutor demands that I establish the superiority of an alternative.

The original claim is no longer being subjected to scrutiny. Instead, the critic is now required to solve an extraordinarily complex problem before the original claim receives any further examination.

The effect is to give the existing strategy a presumption of correctness it has not necessarily earned.

And if the critic cannot produce a comprehensive alternative, the defender can declare victory without having demonstrated anything about the strategy being defended.

This is not to suggest that critics have no evidentiary responsibilities.

If I positively assert that a particular Israeli military operation was indiscriminate, that a specific alternative would have saved lives, or that a particular strategy was militarily unnecessary, those are claims requiring evidence. I cannot simply assume them to be true.

Likewise, a defender of Israeli military conduct is entitled to question my assumptions, challenge my evidence, and point out operational constraints I may have overlooked.

That is what substantive disagreement should involve.

But there is a fundamental difference between challenging the evidence for a claim and imposing an entirely new obligation on the person raising a question.

**The burden of substantiating a claim should correspond to the claim actually being made, not to a more demanding claim substituted by one's opponent.**

My claim was not that I possessed the solution to Israel's security challenges.

My position was that the scale of destruction and loss of life warranted serious examination of the decisions producing those outcomes.

Those are not equivalent propositions, and they do not carry equivalent burdens of proof.

### When requesting an alternative is reasonable

There is, however, an important qualification.

Asking someone what they would do differently is not inherently fallacious. In many circumstances, it is an entirely reasonable and productive question.

For example, someone might respond to my concerns by saying:

"I understand your criticism, but Hamas operates in densely populated areas, making military operations exceptionally difficult. What alternative approaches do you think could realistically reduce civilian casualties while addressing the security threat?"

That question introduces legitimate considerations. It asks about feasibility, military constraints, and the relationship between objectives and consequences. It could lead to a constructive examination of available options.

I might acknowledge that I do not know which alternative would be most effective. We could then investigate what military analysts, humanitarian organizations, or independent investigators have found.

That would be a productive conversation.

But notice how different this is from:

"Well, you tell me what they should do. You don't know? Then you can't criticize."

The first question invites further investigation. The second uses the absence of an immediate answer to terminate it.

The distinction lies not simply in the words used, but in the role the question plays within the argument.

Is the request for an alternative being used to investigate the practical constraints of the problem? Or is it being used to disqualify the person raising concerns?

In the conversation I experienced, the dismissive follow-up—"Yeah, see, you don't know"—made the function of the question particularly revealing.

My interlocutor did not offer evidence that less harmful strategies were infeasible. He did not explain the operational necessity of the existing approach. He did not address the distinction between civilian and combatant casualties or examine the principles governing the conduct of military operations.

He simply treated my inability to produce an alternative as sufficient grounds to dismiss the inquiry.

### The deeper consequence

There is a broader danger in accepting this kind of reasoning.

If criticism of specialized decisions were legitimate only when the critic could personally design a superior replacement, enormous areas of human activity would become effectively insulated from public scrutiny.

Most citizens cannot design a national healthcare system, formulate a comprehensive military strategy, engineer a commercial aircraft, or manage a national economy.

Yet we routinely and legitimately question whether these systems are safe, effective, efficient, equitable, and accountable.

The fact that expertise is needed to implement complex solutions does not mean that only experts can identify problems or demand explanations.

Indeed, the greater the consequences of a decision, the more important it becomes to distinguish technical expertise from the authority to escape criticism.

None of this establishes that a critic's concerns are automatically correct. Questions can be poorly informed, evidence can be misleading, and an apparently troubling outcome may have explanations that are not immediately obvious.

But the appropriate response to those possibilities is to investigate them.

The inability to answer a difficult question does not eliminate the question's legitimacy.

And this returns us to the central problem illustrated by the exchange: **rather than demonstrating that the existing military strategy was justified, my interlocutor attempted to demonstrate that I was unqualified to question it.**

Those are fundamentally different arguments.

Even if he had succeeded in establishing my ignorance of military strategy, the original questions about necessity, effectiveness, civilian harm, and available alternatives would have remained unanswered.

That is what makes this particular form of reasoning so consequential. It allows a discussion to end with the appearance of a rebuttal, even though the central issue has never been addressed.

## III. The Anatomy of the Deflection: Five Conversational Moves

What made this conversation particularly interesting was not simply the final demand that I produce an alternative military strategy. It was the sequence of responses that preceded it.

As I reflected on the exchange, I noticed that each response seemed to redirect the discussion away from the specific questions I was raising. Rather than examining the evidence concerning Israel's military conduct, the conversation repeatedly shifted toward broader, more emotionally charged, or less directly relevant considerations.

The sequence went something like this:

"Things happen in war."

"Would you rather have Hamas or Israel?"

"Iran is much worse."

"Well, tell me what Israel should do instead."

"Yeah, see, you don't know."

These statements are not all logically equivalent, nor is every one inherently fallacious in every context. Some contain legitimate considerations that could contribute to a serious discussion. Wars do produce casualties. Hamas represents a real security threat. Military alternatives must be evaluated against practical constraints. And comparisons between governments can sometimes help illuminate broader geopolitical questions.

The problem arises when these observations are deployed not to advance an investigation, but to replace it.

What follows is an examination of five distinct rhetorical moves, the reasoning errors they can involve, and the way their cumulative effect can obstruct meaningful inquiry.

### A. "Things happen in war" — Normalization as a Substitute for Explanation

The first response was perhaps the most familiar:

"Things happen in war."

On its face, this statement is uncontroversial. Wars are destructive. Civilians are killed. Military operations involve uncertainty, imperfect intelligence, and consequences that cannot always be anticipated or prevented.

Nobody needs to dispute these realities.

But acknowledging that civilian casualties occur in warfare does not answer whether particular casualties were avoidable, whether specific military operations were justified, or whether adequate precautions were taken to protect civilian life.

This is the distinction between recognizing a general feature of a phenomenon and explaining a particular instance of it.

Imagine investigating an industrial accident in which dozens of workers were killed. A company representative responds, "Well, industrial work is dangerous. Accidents happen."

The statement is true, but its truth tells us almost nothing about the accident being investigated.

Were safety protocols followed? Was equipment properly maintained? Were warnings ignored? Could different procedures have prevented the deaths?

The general fact that accidents occur does not establish that this accident was unavoidable.

Likewise, the general fact that wars produce civilian casualties does not establish that any particular military operation, targeting decision, or level of civilian harm was necessary.

What matters is not merely whether harm is possible within an activity, but whether the specific harm in question was justified, foreseeable, preventable, or the result of decisions that warrant criticism.

In the context of my conversation, "things happen in war" functioned as a form of normalization. It placed the extraordinary scale of death within a familiar category—war—and then seemed to treat that categorization as sufficient explanation.

Yet the very questions I was asking concerned the extent to which those deaths could be explained by unavoidable military realities rather than avoidable decisions.

**To say that something commonly happens is not to establish that it had to happen, or that it was permissible for it to happen.**

The statement might serve as the beginning of an explanation. It cannot substitute for the explanation itself.

### B. "Would you rather have Hamas or Israel?" — The False Dichotomy

The next move was to introduce a choice:

"Would you rather have Hamas or Israel?"

Here, the subject changed in a more significant way.

I had been questioning the conduct of a particular military campaign. The response instead asked me to express a preference between two political and military actors.

This is an example of a false dichotomy when the implied choice is presented as though it exhausts the positions available.

The suggestion is that one must either defend Israel's actions or align oneself, implicitly or explicitly, with Hamas.

But there is no logical reason that these positions must be mutually exclusive.

One can condemn Hamas's attacks against Israeli civilians, recognize Israel's legitimate security concerns, and simultaneously question whether Israel's military response has complied with international humanitarian law or adequately protected Palestinian civilians.

These positions are entirely compatible.

In fact, consistently applying the principle that civilian lives deserve protection requires evaluating the conduct of all parties rather than granting immunity from criticism to whichever party one regards as more legitimate.

What interests me about this particular maneuver is how it transforms an analytical question into a question of allegiance.

Rather than asking, "Were these military decisions justified?" the conversation becomes, "Whose side are you on?"

That change matters because questions of allegiance can carry emotional and social consequences that questions of evidence do not necessarily carry.

If a person can be made to feel that criticizing Israel implies defending Hamas, the conversation no longer needs to examine whether the criticism is accurate. The criticism itself becomes suspect because of the political identity supposedly associated with it.

This can create pressure to retreat from legitimate questions merely to avoid being associated with an objectionable group.

But an argument's validity does not depend on whether its conclusion is politically convenient or whether someone fears the associations attached to it.

**Condemning one party's wrongdoing does not confer moral or legal immunity upon another.**

And preferring one political actor over another does not settle whether the preferred actor's particular decisions are justified.

The original inquiry was about actions, evidence, and consequences. The response attempted to transform it into a choice between identities.

These are fundamentally different subjects.

### C. "Iran Is Much Worse" — Whataboutism and Relative Privation

The next response introduced another comparison:

"Iran and all of that is way worse."

This move resembles what is commonly called *whataboutism*: responding to criticism of one actor by directing attention toward the wrongdoing of another.

Closely related is the fallacy of relative privation, in which a concern is treated as less deserving of attention because some other problem is supposedly more serious.

There are circumstances in which comparisons are useful. If the discussion concerned regional security threats, the relative conduct of Israel, Iran, and other governments might be directly relevant.

But that was not the question being asked.

I was not attempting to rank Middle Eastern governments by moral legitimacy or compare every instance of violence in the region.

I was questioning the conduct and consequences of a particular military campaign.

Even assuming, for the sake of argument, that Iran's government were substantially worse by every relevant measure, what would that establish about Israel's targeting decisions, civilian casualties, or available military alternatives?

Nothing directly.

The conduct of one government cannot be justified merely by identifying another government whose conduct may be worse.

Consider the reasoning in an ordinary context.

A company is accused of serious workplace safety violations. Its representative responds by pointing out that a competing company has an even worse safety record.

That comparison might be factually accurate. It might reveal something important about industry-wide conditions.

But it does not establish that the original company's practices are acceptable.

The relevant safety questions remain unanswered.

The same logic applies here.

If the claim is that another government is more dangerous, more repressive, or more responsible for regional instability, that claim can be investigated on its own merits.

But it does not resolve whether Israel's military operations were necessary, proportionate, discriminate, or conducted with adequate precautions.

The rhetorical effect is to expand the discussion until the original issue becomes just one concern among many, potentially more alarming concerns.

Instead of assessing a particular action against relevant standards, the participants are encouraged to assess whether the actor responsible compares favorably with someone else.

**But being less objectionable than another actor is not equivalent to being justified in one's own conduct.**

A comparison can provide context. It cannot replace direct evaluation.

### D. "Tell Me What They Should Do Instead" — Shifting the Burden of Proof

Having moved through the general inevitability of war, the preference between Israel and Hamas, and the comparative wrongdoing of Iran, the conversation eventually reached the response that most clearly exposed the underlying problem:

"Well, you tell me what they should be doing then."

This was the decisive shift.

Until this point, the responses had offered broad contextual considerations without directly examining the original question. Now, the responsibility for providing an explanation was transferred to me.

I had asked whether the military campaign was being conducted in a way that adequately minimized civilian casualties.

My interlocutor responded by demanding that I devise a better campaign.

Notice the change in what must now be established.

Originally, the question was whether there was sufficient evidence to justify the decisions being made.

Now, the question was whether I could demonstrate the superiority of decisions that had not been made.

The existing strategy was effectively granted a presumption of justification unless I could personally produce a superior alternative.

This is a form of burden shifting.

In a serious discussion, the person making a substantive claim bears responsibility for supporting that claim. If someone maintains that a military strategy is necessary, effective, and appropriately protective of civilians, those conclusions require evidence.

Likewise, if I assert that a particular operation was unlawful or that an alternative strategy would definitely have produced better results, I have a responsibility to substantiate those assertions.

But my original question did not claim to establish the superiority of some alternative.

It asked whether the existing approach had been adequately examined and justified.

The demand for a replacement therefore imposed an obligation that did not follow from the position I was actually advancing.

It also created an extraordinarily high threshold for criticism.

Military strategy involves intelligence assessments, logistical constraints, operational planning, adversarial behavior, and technical considerations unavailable to most ordinary citizens. Producing a comprehensive alternative would require expertise and information that neither participant necessarily possessed.

By making that task a prerequisite for questioning the existing strategy, my interlocutor had established a condition that I was unlikely to satisfy.

And because I was unlikely to satisfy it, the original criticism could be dismissed without substantive engagement.

There is a revealing asymmetry here.

The defender does not have to demonstrate that the existing strategy was the best feasible option. The critic, however, is expected to demonstrate that another strategy would be better.

The defender can rely on uncertainty to protect the existing approach, while the critic must overcome that uncertainty to question it.

But uncertainty does not automatically favor the status quo.

**Not knowing whether a better alternative exists does not establish that no better alternative exists.**

Nor does it establish that the existing decisions complied with the standards by which they should be evaluated.

A legitimate response would be to examine the actual constraints and available evidence together.

A deflective response is to turn the critic's lack of a complete solution into a reason to abandon the investigation.

### E. "Yeah, See, You Don't Know" — Disqualifying the Critic Instead of Answering the Criticism

The final response completed the shift:

"Yeah, see, you don't know."

This was perhaps the most revealing moment in the conversation.

Strictly speaking, my interlocutor had identified something true: I did not possess a complete alternative military strategy.

I had not claimed otherwise.

But the statement was delivered as though acknowledging that limitation resolved the entire disagreement.

Here, the discussion ceased to concern the validity of the original questions and became a judgment about the questioner's competence.

The implied reasoning was simple: because I could not provide the requested technical solution, I lacked sufficient standing to criticize the existing one.

This resembles an appeal to expertise used in reverse—not an appeal to a particular expert's evidence, but an attempt to disqualify a nonexpert from participating in the evaluation of a consequential decision.

Expertise undoubtedly matters. A person who has studied military operations may understand constraints that I do not. Their knowledge can expose weaknesses in my assumptions and help explain why certain seemingly attractive alternatives are infeasible.

But expertise must contribute evidence or reasoning to the discussion.

Simply pointing out that someone lacks expertise does not establish that their concerns are unfounded.

A person can be mistaken about the explanation for a problem while still correctly identifying that the problem deserves investigation.

A patient may not understand surgical techniques but can reasonably question why a procedure is necessary. A homeowner may not understand structural engineering but can ask whether a major renovation is justified. A citizen may not understand military planning but can question whether civilian-protection obligations have been met.

In all three cases, specialized knowledge may be necessary to answer the question properly.

It is not necessary to establish that the question can legitimately be asked.

This is where my interlocutor's response became particularly unproductive.

If he had said, "Neither of us knows enough to determine whether a better strategy was possible, so we should be careful about our conclusions," I would have considered that a reasonable acknowledgment of uncertainty.

But that was not the substance of the response.

The lack of knowledge was attributed to me and treated as an argumentative defeat, while the justification for the existing military strategy remained unexamined.

The result was a peculiar reversal: the person expressing uncertainty was discredited, while the person expressing confidence was not required to explain the basis for that confidence.

**Confidence had effectively been substituted for justification.**

And with that substitution, the conversation ended—not because the original questions had been answered, but because the person raising them had been declared unqualified to continue.

### The Cumulative Effect: Five Moves, One Unanswered Question

Consider the sequence as a whole.

First, civilian casualties were situated within the general destructiveness of war.

Then, the discussion shifted toward whether Hamas or Israel was preferable.

Next, attention was redirected toward Iran's wrongdoing.

When I continued to question the military campaign, I was required to propose an alternative strategy.

Finally, my inability to do so was treated as evidence that my criticism lacked legitimacy.

Each move changed the immediate subject of discussion.

And each move created an opportunity to avoid the question that had prompted the exchange.

At no point did my interlocutor explain what evidence justified the extent of civilian harm, what precautions were taken, or why less harmful alternatives were considered infeasible.

This does not prove that he was consciously attempting to deceive or manipulate me. It is entirely possible that he sincerely regarded these responses as persuasive. He might have believed that military constraints were self-evident, that Israel's security concerns outweighed the objections I raised, or that my questions reflected insufficient appreciation of the difficulties involved.

Those possibilities deserve acknowledgment.

But the psychological intention behind a response is separate from its logical function.

A person can sincerely believe they are defending a position while repeatedly avoiding the evidence necessary to justify it.

And a conversation can become structurally evasive even without a deliberate intention to evade.

That is what I find most important about this exchange.

The problem was not simply that my interlocutor disagreed with me. Disagreement would have created an opportunity to examine competing evidence, challenge assumptions, and potentially revise our conclusions.

The problem was that **the responses repeatedly changed the conditions of the discussion rather than addressing the questions under examination.**

By the end, we were no longer discussing whether Israel's military conduct was justified. We were discussing whether I knew how to command a military campaign.

The original questions remained exactly where they had been at the beginning.

This reveals a broader failure of reasoning: the appearance of winning an argument can be achieved without actually answering it.

A response may sound decisive, forceful, or rhetorically clever while doing nothing to establish the truth of the conclusion it is supposed to defend.

That distinction is essential.

**A successful deflection can end a conversation. It cannot, by itself, establish that the position being defended is correct.**

And perhaps the clearest way to understand why is to remove the political context entirely and consider the same reasoning in a much more ordinary situation: a homeowner questioning an expensive roof-repair recommendation.

In that setting, the absurdity of requiring a complete replacement plan before permitting criticism becomes much harder to overlook.

## IV. The Roof-Repair Analogy: Why This Reasoning Fails Outside Politics

One of the most useful ways to evaluate an argument is to remove it from the emotionally charged context in which it originally appeared and apply the same reasoning to a more ordinary situation.

Political discussions are particularly vulnerable to distorted reasoning because our judgments are often entangled with ideological commitments, group loyalties, emotional associations, and deeply held moral convictions. Arguments that might appear persuasive when defending a favored political position can become obviously unreasonable when transferred to circumstances in which those commitments are absent.

Consider a simple hypothetical.

Suppose your roof develops a leak. You contact a roofing contractor, who inspects the property and informs you that the entire roof must be removed and replaced. The work will cost $45,000.

You find this recommendation surprising. The leak appears relatively localized, and although you recognize that the underlying damage might be more extensive than it appears, you are not convinced that replacing the entire roof is necessary.

You raise your concerns with another stakeholder in the property.

"Doesn't that seem excessive? I understand that the roof needs work, but do we really need to replace the entire thing? Could a more targeted repair solve the problem? Shouldn't we investigate whether there's a less expensive way to achieve the same result?"

Your stakeholder responds:

"Well, are you a professional roofer?"

"No."

"Then tell me exactly how you would fix it."

"I don't know exactly how to repair a roof. That's why we hired a professional. I'm just questioning whether the proposed work is necessary."

"Yeah, see, you don't know. The contractor is the expert."

Most people would immediately recognize the inadequacy of this response.

And what makes it inadequate is not that the contractor must necessarily be wrong. The contractor might be entirely correct. There might be extensive structural damage, hidden deterioration, or serious problems that make a complete replacement the safest and most economical option.

The homeowner's suspicion might ultimately prove unfounded.

But none of that changes the fact that the questions themselves are legitimate.

The homeowner does not need to know how to install roofing materials, repair structural components, or calculate drainage requirements to ask whether the recommended work is necessary.

They need not arrive with a complete engineering plan before requesting an explanation, obtaining another estimate, or asking the contractor to demonstrate why a less extensive repair would be inadequate.

**The inability to perform a specialized task does not eliminate the ability—or the right—to critically evaluate whether that task is necessary.**

### Expertise Is Not the Same as Infallibility

At the center of this analogy is a distinction between possessing specialized knowledge and possessing unquestionable authority.

The roofing contractor has expertise the homeowner lacks. That expertise should matter. It gives the contractor a stronger basis for identifying structural problems, evaluating repair options, and recommending appropriate interventions.

But it does not make the contractor infallible.

Expertise can be exercised competently or incompetently. Recommendations can be based on accurate or inaccurate assessments. Specialists can overlook relevant considerations, disagree with one another, or make judgments influenced by incomplete information.

In some circumstances, professionals may also have financial, institutional, or reputational incentives that affect their recommendations.

None of these possibilities proves that any particular expert is mistaken or acting dishonestly. They merely establish why expertise does not eliminate the need for scrutiny.

The correct relationship between expert judgment and critical inquiry is therefore not one of unconditional obedience.

It is one in which expertise provides reasons, evidence, and informed evaluations that can be examined.

If the contractor explains that water damage extends throughout the roof's supporting structure, produces photographs documenting the deterioration, identifies applicable building standards, and demonstrates why a localized repair would not solve the problem, the homeowner now has substantive reasons to accept the recommendation.

The homeowner may still seek a second opinion, but the discussion has advanced.

The contractor has done something fundamentally different from simply declaring, "I'm the expert, and you're not."

He has provided an explanation.

**Expertise becomes intellectually valuable when it helps us understand why a conclusion is justified, not merely when it is invoked to discourage us from questioning that conclusion.**

### Identifying a Problem and Solving It Are Different Forms of Knowledge

The analogy also illustrates an important distinction between recognizing evidence of a possible problem and possessing the knowledge required to resolve it.

These abilities can overlap, but they are not identical.

A homeowner may notice that the proposed repair costs dramatically more than comparable work. They may observe that other contractors have recommended less extensive interventions. They may recognize inconsistencies in the contractor's explanation.

These observations can provide legitimate grounds for further inquiry, even if the homeowner cannot determine the technically correct repair.

Indeed, the homeowner may have good reasons to believe that something is wrong without yet knowing precisely what the problem is.

The same principle appears throughout ordinary life.

A patient need not be a physician to question why an unusually invasive procedure has been recommended. A shareholder need not be a corporate executive to ask whether a company's expenditures are justified. A passenger need not understand aircraft engineering to raise concerns about an apparent safety problem.

In each instance, the observer's lack of technical expertise may limit the conclusions they can responsibly draw.

But that limitation does not turn every concern into an illegitimate one.

There is a difference between saying, "I have established that this recommendation is wrong," and saying, "I have identified reasons to question this recommendation, and I want to understand whether it is justified."

The former asserts a conclusion. The latter initiates an investigation.

Confusing them allows defenders of an existing decision to demand proof of something the critic has not claimed.

And that is precisely what happened in my conversation about Israel's military conduct.

I was not presenting a completed military strategy and declaring it superior to the one being employed.

I was questioning whether the existing strategy had been adequately justified in light of its consequences.

The appropriate response would have been to examine the considerations relevant to that question—not to demand that I demonstrate how to conduct a military campaign myself.

### The Problem of Delegated Authority

The roof-repair example also reveals a broader institutional problem.

When we hire specialists, elect representatives, or authorize institutions to make decisions on our behalf, we delegate certain responsibilities because those tasks require knowledge, resources, or capabilities we do not personally possess.

But delegation is not the same as surrendering the right to oversight.

In economics and organizational theory, this relationship is often examined through the *principal-agent problem*.

A principal entrusts an agent with responsibility for pursuing certain objectives. The agent may possess information and expertise unavailable to the principal, creating the possibility that the principal cannot easily determine whether the agent's decisions are appropriate.

The homeowner and contractor provide a simple example.

The homeowner wants a safe, functional roof at a reasonable cost. The contractor possesses the specialized knowledge necessary to perform the work. Because the homeowner cannot independently verify every technical recommendation, some mechanism of accountability becomes necessary.

That might involve competing estimates, independent inspections, contractual guarantees, professional standards, or documentation explaining the proposed work.

These safeguards do not exist because every contractor is presumed dishonest.

They exist because expertise and delegated decision-making create information asymmetries that make independent scrutiny valuable.

The same underlying problem becomes considerably more consequential when applied to government and military institutions.

Military commanders possess operational knowledge that ordinary citizens generally lack. Governments control intelligence, resources, and decision-making processes that cannot always be independently examined by the public.

But the decisions made within these institutions can have enormous consequences, including the loss of human life.

The need for specialized expertise does not dissolve the need for accountability. If anything, the combination of concentrated authority, incomplete public information, and potentially irreversible consequences makes meaningful oversight especially important.

There is also an important difference between the homeowner analogy and warfare: the people bearing the gravest consequences are often not the people who authorized the decisions.

In Gaza, Palestinian civilians bear the direct consequences of military operations in ways that cannot be adequately captured by an analogy about financial stakeholders or property owners.

Their lives are not simply costs within someone else's strategic calculation. They are individuals entitled to protection under international humanitarian law.

Similarly, Israeli civilians have legitimate interests in protection against armed attacks.

The relevant questions of accountability therefore extend beyond whether taxpayers approve of their government's military policies. They concern the obligations of institutions wielding lethal force and the rights of the people affected by their decisions.

**The greater the power delegated to an institution, and the more consequential its decisions, the stronger the justification for demanding reasons, evidence, and independent scrutiny.**

### Where the Analogy Has Limits

There is an important objection worth considering.

Military strategy is not roof repair.

A contractor operates in a relatively controlled environment. A military commander confronts an adversary who adapts, conceals information, and actively attempts to undermine the commander's objectives.

Military operations may involve incomplete intelligence, urgent decisions, uncertain outcomes, and information that cannot safely be disclosed to the public.

An alternative that appears obvious to an outside observer may be operationally infeasible. A less destructive tactic in one circumstance might increase risks elsewhere or fail to accomplish a legitimate military objective.

These distinctions matter.

It would be intellectually careless to treat military operations as though they could be evaluated with the same straightforward cost comparisons used in ordinary contracting decisions.

But notice what these objections actually establish.

They establish that assessing military conduct is difficult.

They do not establish that assessing military conduct is illegitimate.

In fact, the existence of such complexities is a reason for more careful investigation, greater intellectual humility, and appropriate forms of independent oversight.

The difficulty of obtaining complete information should make us more cautious about confidently declaring that a particular strategy was unnecessary.

But it should also make us cautious about confidently declaring that the strategy was necessary.

Uncertainty cuts both ways.

A person defending a military decision cannot simply invoke complexity as evidence that the decision was justified, any more than a critic can ignore complexity when alleging that a better option was readily available.

Both conclusions require supporting evidence.

The responsible response to limited knowledge is not automatic condemnation or automatic deference. It is a recognition of what remains unknown, alongside a willingness to investigate what can be established.

### When Deference Becomes a Substitute for Reasoning

The deeper issue illustrated by the roof-repair analogy is the difference between respecting expertise and using expertise as a substitute for justification.

There is nothing irrational about placing greater weight on the judgments of people with relevant knowledge and experience.

Indeed, refusing to acknowledge genuine expertise would create its own serious problems.

But expertise cannot do the argumentative work of evidence merely by being invoked.

Consider two possible explanations for the contractor's recommendation.

The first is:

"The entire roof needs replacing because the underlying structure is extensively damaged. Here is the inspection report, here are the photographs, and here is why localized repairs would not adequately address the problem."

The second is:

"The entire roof needs replacing because I'm the professional. If you don't know how to fix it yourself, you have no basis to question me."

Both statements appeal to expertise.

Only the first uses expertise to provide an explanation.

The second uses expertise to establish a hierarchy of conversational authority: the expert may assert conclusions, while the nonexpert must accept them or demonstrate equivalent technical competence.

That is not a sound model of rational inquiry.

And it becomes especially troubling when the supposed expert is not even participating in the conversation.

In my original exchange, neither participant was presenting a detailed military assessment. My interlocutor was not demonstrating that Israel had considered and rejected less harmful alternatives. He was not identifying specific operational constraints supported by evidence.

He was appealing to the complexity of military strategy to dismiss my questions about its execution.

But if neither of us possessed the information needed to determine whether a better strategy existed, then neither of us was entitled to treat that uncertainty as proof of our preferred conclusion.

My inability to formulate a replacement did not establish that Israel's strategy was unjustified.

But it certainly did not establish that the strategy was justified, either.

That distinction is the entire point.

### The Principle Beneath the Analogy

Returning to the homeowner, imagine that the conversation had unfolded differently.

The homeowner questions the proposed replacement. The stakeholder acknowledges the concern but points out that substantial structural deterioration may be hidden beneath the visible surface. They agree to ask the contractor for supporting evidence and seek an independent assessment before deciding whether the full replacement is necessary.

Neither person needs to become an expert roofer.

Neither needs to assume the contractor is dishonest.

Neither needs to know the correct answer before beginning the investigation.

They simply recognize that an expensive and consequential recommendation deserves scrutiny, and that uncertainty should motivate further inquiry rather than terminate it.

This is the same orientation I was attempting to bring to the discussion of Israel's military campaign.

I was not claiming that I could resolve every strategic difficulty facing the Israeli military. I was not claiming that the casualty figures alone answered every question about military necessity or legal responsibility.

I was proposing that the consequences warranted a serious examination of the decisions producing them.

The response I received treated the absence of a ready-made alternative as a reason to abandon that examination.

And this is where the analogy reveals its most important lesson:

**We do not need to possess the knowledge required to solve a problem before we are entitled to ask whether those entrusted with solving it are doing so responsibly.**

Recognizing that distinction does not undermine expertise.

It places expertise within a framework of reasoning and accountability, where decisions must ultimately be evaluated by the evidence supporting them rather than by the authority of those making them.

That principle becomes particularly important when the consequences are not merely financial, but human.

And it brings us back to the original question that the conversation never answered: given the extraordinary scale of death and destruction in Gaza, what evidence would allow us to determine whether Israel's military conduct was necessary, proportionate, and conducted with all feasible precautions to minimize civilian harm?

## V. Why the Original Question Was Legitimate

Having established the logical problems with my interlocutor's responses, it is worth returning to the question that prompted the conversation in the first place.

Was there anything unreasonable about questioning Israel's military conduct in light of the extraordinary number of Palestinians killed?

More specifically, was it unreasonable to ask whether the military campaign could have been conducted differently, in ways that achieved legitimate security objectives while reducing civilian deaths?

I would argue that these are not merely reasonable questions. They are among the most important questions one can ask when evaluating the conduct of any military campaign.

And the reason is not simply moral intuition or personal discomfort with the scale of destruction. The questions closely resemble those that military analysts, humanitarian investigators, and legal authorities must consider when assessing the necessity, effectiveness, and lawfulness of military operations.

Far from being evidence of ignorance, asking whether civilian harm was avoidable is an essential part of evaluating the decisions that produced it.

### A. The Casualty Disparity: An Investigative Starting Point, Not a Complete Conclusion

The immediate concern I raised during the conversation was the enormous disparity between Palestinian and Israeli deaths.

I referred to a ratio of approximately thirty Palestinians killed for every Israeli killed, using it to illustrate the scale of the imbalance and to question whether that disparity deserved serious investigation.

For a published analysis, such a ratio must be tied to a clearly defined period, consistent casualty categories, and identifiable sources. Different periods of the conflict produce different ratios, and different datasets may include or exclude particular types of deaths.

The [United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs](https://www.ochaopt.org/data/casualties?tab=palestinianfatalities), for example, maintains historical casualty records with specific definitions and verification standards. Its records for the hostilities beginning October 7, 2023, are handled differently from its older verified casualty database.

Nevertheless, the underlying observation remains: the scale of Palestinian deaths and destruction in Gaza is extraordinary, and the consequences warrant serious scrutiny.

But what exactly does that disparity establish?

Taken alone, it establishes a difference in the number of people killed. It does not tell us everything necessary to determine whether the military operations responsible were lawful, indiscriminate, proportionate, or conducted with sufficient precautions.

An aggregate death ratio does not distinguish civilians from combatants. It does not identify individual military objectives. It does not tell us what information commanders possessed when deciding to attack, what alternatives were available, or what civilian harm was anticipated.

Nor does the difference in casualties, considered in isolation, establish that the party inflicting more deaths necessarily violated international humanitarian law.

These distinctions matter, and a serious analysis should acknowledge them.

But there is an equally important point: **the fact that a statistic does not independently prove a conclusion does not mean it provides no legitimate reason to investigate that conclusion.**

Suppose a hospital reports an unusually high mortality rate among patients undergoing a particular procedure.

That statistic alone would not establish medical malpractice. Perhaps the hospital treats unusually severe cases. Perhaps its patients have additional risk factors. Perhaps the procedures are performed under circumstances that make adverse outcomes more likely.

All of these explanations might be relevant.

But nobody reasoning responsibly would conclude that the mortality rate therefore deserves no investigation.

On the contrary, the unusual outcome would provide a reason to examine the underlying causes.

What kinds of patients were being treated? What procedures were performed? What decisions contributed to the outcomes? Were accepted standards followed? Were preventable deaths occurring?

The questions are justified by the need to understand the observed results, even before the answers are known.

The same general reasoning applies to military casualties.

A substantial disparity in deaths does not settle the question of wrongdoing. But it can provide an important reason to investigate the circumstances under which people were killed.

Who were the people counted in the total? How many were civilians? How many were members of armed groups? What weapons and targeting methods were used? What was the anticipated military advantage? What measures were taken to prevent civilian harm?

These are not rhetorical questions whose answers must necessarily condemn Israel.

They are factual questions whose answers are necessary for an informed assessment.

To dismiss them merely because "things happen in war" is to mistake the existence of a possible explanation for evidence that the explanation is sufficient.

### B. Civilian Casualties Are Not Simply an Unavoidable Background Condition of Warfare

One of the most significant problems with the response "things happen in war" is that it treats civilian casualties as though their occurrence largely removes them from further scrutiny.

But international humanitarian law operates on a very different premise.

It recognizes that armed conflicts may involve military necessity and that attacks against legitimate military objectives can result in incidental civilian harm.

At the same time, it imposes restrictions on how military operations may be conducted.

The existence of a legitimate military objective does not provide unlimited permission to pursue it through any available means.

According to the [International Committee of the Red Cross](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/FAQ-icrc-work-Israel-and-occupied-territories), three foundational principles govern the conduct of hostilities: distinction, proportionality, and precautions.

Each is directly relevant to the questions I raised.

**The principle of distinction** requires parties to an armed conflict to distinguish between civilians and combatants, and between civilian objects and military objectives.

Civilians may not be deliberately targeted as such. Neither may civilian objects, unless their actual use or other relevant circumstances make them legitimate military objectives under the applicable rules.

Attacks that cannot be directed at a specific military objective, or whose effects cannot be limited as required by international humanitarian law, are prohibited as indiscriminate.

This immediately introduces questions that cannot be answered by observing that war is inherently destructive.

Were the people or objects attacked legitimate military targets? What evidence established their status? Were the weapons and methods used capable of distinguishing those targets from surrounding civilians?

**The principle of proportionality** imposes another essential restriction.

Even when attacking a legitimate military objective, the expected incidental civilian harm must not be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated.

This is an important distinction because proportionality, in its legal sense, is not determined by comparing the total number of Palestinians and Israelis killed.

It is not a requirement that casualties be numerically equal between opposing sides. Nor is it a calculation in which the number of civilian deaths is compared directly with the number of enemy combatants killed.

Instead, proportionality concerns the anticipated civilian harm and military advantage associated with an attack, assessed using the information reasonably available at the time.

A military operation can be directed at a legitimate target and still violate the principle of proportionality if the expected incidental civilian harm is excessive relative to the anticipated concrete and direct military advantage.

Conversely, a high number of casualties does not automatically establish that the legal proportionality test was violated.

The relevant circumstances must be examined.

That is precisely why investigation is necessary.

**The principle of precautions** is perhaps the most directly relevant to the argument I was making during the conversation.

International humanitarian law requires parties to take all feasible precautions to avoid, and in any event minimize, incidental civilian deaths, injuries, and damage to civilian objects.

This includes verifying military targets, evaluating anticipated civilian harm, considering the weapons and methods used, providing effective warnings when required, and canceling or suspending attacks when it becomes apparent that the relevant legal requirements would be violated.

Where a choice is possible between military objectives offering a similar military advantage, the objective expected to present less danger to civilians must be selected.

These are not abstract standards invented by critics of a particular military campaign.

They are established requirements of the laws governing armed conflict.

And they lead directly to the kinds of questions I asked.

Could the military objective have been achieved through different tactics? Could different weapons have reduced civilian harm? Could the operation have been conducted at another time? Was sufficient intelligence available to verify the intended target? Were civilian casualties anticipated, and what precautions were undertaken to minimize them?

These are not peripheral considerations.

They are central to determining whether military operations were conducted in accordance with applicable legal obligations.

In other words, **my original question about whether alternative methods could have reduced civilian deaths was not an unreasonable demand for perfect warfare. It was closely related to the actual standards governing how warfare must be conducted.**

### C. The Difference Between Military Necessity and Unlimited Military Discretion

A possible response to this argument is that Israel faced a genuine security threat following the Hamas-led attacks of October 7, 2023.

That threat should not be dismissed.

Hamas and other armed groups killed Israeli civilians, took hostages, and committed serious violations of international humanitarian law. Israel has legitimate security interests, including protecting its population against further armed attacks.

But acknowledging those interests does not answer whether every method employed to pursue them was lawful or justified.

The distinction is essential.

A legitimate objective does not automatically justify every action taken in pursuit of that objective.

Consider a police operation intended to apprehend a dangerous armed suspect.

The objective may be legitimate and urgent. The suspect may pose a serious threat to public safety. Officers may face difficult decisions under uncertain conditions.

But none of that establishes that every possible use of force would be justified.

We would still ask whether the force used was necessary under the circumstances, whether the operation endangered uninvolved people, and whether reasonable protective measures were available.

The analogy is imperfect because policing and armed conflict operate under different legal frameworks. Nevertheless, the general distinction remains useful: the legitimacy of an objective and the legitimacy of the methods used to achieve it are separate questions.

The same principle applies to military operations.

Even where an armed group operates within densely populated civilian areas, the presence of military targets does not eliminate civilian protections. Civilians do not automatically lose their protection merely because fighters or military assets are present nearby.

Likewise, violations of international humanitarian law by one party do not suspend the obligations of the other.

This is not a special standard applied to Israel. It applies to all parties engaged in armed conflict.

And this is precisely why the response "Would you rather have Hamas or Israel?" fails to address the original concern.

Even if I regard Hamas as responsible for grave atrocities, that conclusion does not determine whether Israel's subsequent military operations met their own legal requirements.

Both propositions must be evaluated independently.

**The existence of a genuine threat can help explain why military action was undertaken. It does not, by itself, establish that every military action undertaken was justified.**

### D. Were Less Destructive Alternatives Feasible?

This brings us to the question my interlocutor refused to engage: could alternative military decisions have achieved the relevant objectives while reducing civilian casualties?

There is an important distinction between asking whether the military acted *optimally* and asking whether it took *all feasible precautions* to minimize civilian harm.

Optimality is an exceptionally demanding standard. In complex and uncertain environments, identifying the single best possible strategy may be impossible, even with extensive information and hindsight.

A military operation might be imperfect without necessarily being unlawful.

But the fact that perfect outcomes are impossible does not eliminate the obligation to take feasible precautions.

The word *feasible* is important.

It recognizes that military decisions occur under constraints. Commanders may face urgent threats, incomplete intelligence, limited resources, and competing risks.

A theoretical alternative that could not reasonably have been implemented under the circumstances would not establish that the original decision was unjustified.

But where less harmful alternatives were practically available, their existence can be relevant to assessing the precautions taken.

This creates an identifiable subject for investigation.

What information was available before the attack? What military advantage was expected? Were alternative targets, weapons, methods, or timings considered? Would those alternatives have offered a comparable military advantage with less expected danger to civilians?

These questions need not be answered through speculation alone.

They can be examined through operational records, available intelligence, witness testimony, weapons analysis, military expertise, forensic evidence, and independent investigations.

Some relevant information may be classified or otherwise inaccessible, making complete public assessment difficult.

But the appropriate conclusion from that difficulty is that certain questions remain unresolved—not that the existing military decisions should automatically be presumed justified.

It is also important to distinguish between evaluating individual attacks and evaluating the broader military campaign.

A strategy might succeed in degrading an armed group's capabilities while simultaneously causing devastating civilian harm, creating long-term instability, or failing to produce sustainable security.

These outcomes raise legitimate strategic and political questions even when they do not, by themselves, determine the legality of individual attacks.

Did the campaign achieve its stated objectives? Were the consequences anticipated? Were the long-term security effects considered? Were approaches involving different operational methods or combinations of military, diplomatic, and humanitarian measures adequately evaluated?

These are questions of effectiveness and public accountability.

They do not require pretending that an obvious or painless alternative was available.

They require taking the consequences of military decisions seriously enough to investigate whether those decisions were warranted.

### E. What Would a Serious Investigation Actually Examine?

If my interlocutor had genuinely wanted to examine the question, rather than dismiss it, there were several directions the conversation could have taken.

We could have begun by distinguishing the different categories of people killed.

Aggregate casualty figures often combine combatants and civilians, and classification can be complicated by incomplete records, disputed affiliations, and the destruction of institutions responsible for documenting deaths.

That uncertainty matters, but it does not make investigation impossible or irrelevant.

The first task would be to examine the best available casualty data, understand its limitations, and compare the findings of independent investigators.

We could then examine particular military operations.

For a given attack, the central questions would include the identity of the intended target, the anticipated military advantage, the civilian population at risk, the precautions taken, and the information available to those responsible for the decision.

This distinction between what was known at the time and what became known afterward is especially important.

A tragic outcome does not necessarily prove that a commander acted unlawfully. But evidence that foreseeable civilian harm was ignored, that available precautions were not taken, or that the anticipated harm was excessive could support a very different conclusion.

We could also consider the relationship between the military objectives pursued and the consequences experienced by civilians.

Did attacks meaningfully reduce the military capabilities of the intended targets? Were civilian structures destroyed because they qualified as military objectives, because attacks were insufficiently discriminating, or for other reasons? What explains the effects on hospitals, schools, housing, water systems, and other essential civilian infrastructure?

These are matters that require evidence, not assumptions.

Nor would such an inquiry be starting from nothing.

Humanitarian organizations, international investigators, journalists, and legal researchers have already examined numerous aspects of Israel's conduct in Gaza. Their findings include grave allegations and, in some cases, explicit conclusions of serious international crimes.

For example, the United Nations Independent International Commission of Inquiry concluded in September 2025 that Israeli authorities and forces had committed genocide in Gaza, and reaffirmed findings of continuing genocidal acts in its October 2026 report. These are consequential investigative conclusions, although they are distinct from a final judgment by an international court.

The existence of these investigations demonstrates that the questions surrounding civilian harm, military conduct, and legal responsibility are neither frivolous nor beyond meaningful examination.

They are already subjects of extensive inquiry.

And where findings are disputed, the appropriate response is to examine the evidence and reasoning supporting them.

This is how a productive disagreement could have developed.

My interlocutor might have challenged the reliability of particular casualty estimates, identified military constraints I had overlooked, offered evidence of precautions taken, or questioned whether specific alternative tactics were actually feasible.

I could have examined those arguments, reconsidered assumptions, and refined my position.

He might have persuaded me that some particular operation was justified under circumstances I had not fully understood.

Alternatively, the evidence might have supported my concerns.

Or we might have concluded that neither of us possessed enough information to reach a confident judgment.

Any of these outcomes would have represented intellectual progress.

The critical point is that the conversation would have remained focused on the evidence.

### F. The Question Was Not Whether I Knew the Answer

Looking back, what stands out most is that my original inquiry did not require an immediate conclusion.

I was not asking my interlocutor to agree that every Israeli military operation was indiscriminate. Nor was I asserting that the casualty disparity alone proved the existence of a particular war crime.

I was asking whether the scale of death warranted investigation into the conduct and consequences of the military campaign.

The answer to that question should not depend on whether I can personally formulate a comprehensive military alternative.

Nor should it depend on whether the person I am speaking with regards Israel as more legitimate than Hamas, fears Iran, or believes that war inevitably produces suffering.

Those considerations may matter in other parts of the discussion.

But they do not eliminate the obligation to assess military conduct against the relevant evidence and standards.

Indeed, the principles governing armed conflict exist precisely because war is dangerous, because military objectives can conflict with humanitarian concerns, and because the use of force requires limitations even when the underlying security threat is real.

The presence of those difficulties does not make civilian protection irrelevant.

It makes civilian protection essential.

And it demonstrates why the question "Could this have been done differently, with fewer civilian deaths?" deserves consideration rather than ridicule.

There may be circumstances in which the answer is no. There may be operations for which no feasible alternative would have offered a similar military advantage with less expected civilian harm.

But that is a conclusion that must emerge from examining the circumstances.

It cannot be inferred from the mere fact that someone asking the question does not personally know how to conduct the operation.

**The inability of a critic to identify a better strategy does not relieve those defending the existing strategy of the responsibility to justify it.**

The deeper problem, then, is not simply a disagreement over Israel's military conduct. It concerns the relationship between evidence, uncertainty, and the conclusions people are willing to defend.

Why does a legitimate inquiry sometimes provoke a demand that the questioner solve the entire problem?

Why does acknowledging uncertainty become a weakness, while confidently defending a decision without examining the evidence can be treated as strength?

And why do some conversations become more resistant to investigation precisely when the consequences under discussion are most serious?

To understand those reactions, we need to move beyond the formal structure of the arguments and examine the psychological processes that may influence how people respond when evidence challenges deeply held beliefs.

## VI. The Psychology of Defensive Reasoning

Up to this point, I have focused primarily on the logical structure of the conversation: how questions were redirected, how the burden of justification was shifted, and how my inability to provide a complete alternative military strategy was treated as grounds for dismissing my concerns.

But identifying these reasoning errors raises another question.

Why do people reason this way?

Why would someone respond to an invitation to investigate a consequential decision by repeatedly redirecting the conversation away from the evidence? Why does a request for explanation sometimes seem to provoke defensiveness rather than curiosity? And why might a person regard their ability to dismiss a question as equivalent to having answered it?

It would be tempting to conclude that such responses simply reflect intellectual dishonesty, an unwillingness to think critically, or a deliberate attempt to manipulate the discussion.

In some circumstances, those explanations may be accurate.

But I suspect the phenomenon is more interesting, and often more complicated, than conscious dishonesty.

A person can sincerely believe they are reasoning objectively while nevertheless approaching a question in ways that systematically favor conclusions they already hold. They can experience criticism of an idea as a threat to something personally important. They can construct arguments that feel persuasive because those arguments protect an established worldview, even when the arguments fail to address the questions actually being asked.

These possibilities are well established subjects of research in psychology and political cognition.

They also help illuminate why a conversation can appear to involve vigorous argumentation while producing very little genuine examination of the issue at hand.

### A. Motivated Reasoning: When Conclusions Influence the Search for Evidence

One of the most useful concepts for understanding this phenomenon is *motivated reasoning*.

In her influential 1990 paper, *[The Case for Motivated Reasoning](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.108.3.480)*, psychologist Ziva Kunda examined how people's motivations can influence the cognitive processes they use to evaluate information and construct judgments.

The central insight is that reasoning is not always directed exclusively toward discovering what is true.

Sometimes people are also motivated to arrive at a particular conclusion.

These motivations can influence which evidence they notice, which explanations they find persuasive, which objections they consider important, and how rigorously they scrutinize information that challenges their existing beliefs.

This does not necessarily involve consciously deciding to disregard the truth.

A person may genuinely believe they are evaluating evidence fairly while applying different standards to information that supports or threatens their preferred conclusion.

Consider two different approaches to a controversial question.

In the first, someone begins by asking:

"What does the available evidence suggest, and what conclusion should I draw from it?"

In the second, someone begins with an established conclusion and, perhaps without consciously realizing it, approaches the evidence through a different question:

"How can I reconcile this information with what I already believe?"

These approaches can produce very different reasoning processes.

The first invites the possibility that the evidence may require a change in belief.

The second encourages the preservation of an existing belief, even if doing so requires introducing qualifications, exceptions, or alternative explanations that would receive much greater scrutiny in another context.

This distinction is especially relevant to the conversation I experienced.

My questions about Palestinian casualties did not require my interlocutor to immediately conclude that Israel's military conduct was unjustified. They simply invited examination of that possibility.

Yet the responses consistently moved away from the proposed investigation.

Rather than examining whether civilian deaths were avoidable, the conversation shifted toward the inevitability of casualties in war.

Rather than evaluating the conduct of particular military operations, it shifted toward whether Israel or Hamas was preferable.

Rather than addressing the evidence concerning Israel's decisions, it shifted toward Iran's wrongdoing.

And when I continued to ask whether the strategy had been adequately justified, the discussion shifted toward whether I possessed the expertise to devise a replacement.

Each response had the potential to preserve the original favorable assessment of Israel's conduct without requiring that assessment to be subjected to the scrutiny I was proposing.

This is consistent with motivated reasoning, although the exchange alone cannot establish that motivated reasoning was the cause.

The distinction matters. A series of weak arguments does not reveal a person's internal psychological state. But it can reveal a pattern of reasoning whose effects resemble those studied by psychologists.

**The central danger of motivated reasoning is not that people necessarily invent evidence. It is that they may evaluate evidence according to standards influenced by the conclusions they hope to preserve.**

### B. Motivated Skepticism: Unequal Standards for Competing Claims

A particularly revealing form of motivated reasoning involves applying different standards of skepticism to claims depending on whether those claims support or challenge an existing position.

Political scientists Charles Taber and Milton Lodge examined this phenomenon in their 2006 study, *[Motivated Skepticism in the Evaluation of Political Beliefs](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5907.2006.00214.x)*.

Their research found that participants evaluating politically controversial arguments tended to assess information more favorably when it supported their existing attitudes and to scrutinize opposing arguments more critically.

Participants also showed tendencies to seek out confirming information and generate counterarguments against information that challenged their positions.

The broader implication is that people can approach the same subject with substantially different evidentiary expectations depending on which conclusion is being defended.

This suggests an especially interesting way to interpret the final exchange in my conversation.

When I questioned whether Israel's military strategy adequately minimized civilian harm, I was effectively asked to establish a highly demanding counterfactual claim: that I could devise a better military strategy.

But my interlocutor did not appear to apply an equally demanding standard to his own position.

He did not demonstrate that Israel's existing strategy was the least harmful feasible option.

He did not provide evidence establishing that alternative approaches had been considered and reasonably rejected.

He did not explain how the expected civilian harm of particular operations had been evaluated.

Instead, my inability to demonstrate a superior alternative was treated as sufficient reason to dismiss the concerns.

This reveals an asymmetry in evidentiary expectations.

The existing strategy was defended without a detailed demonstration of its necessity, while criticism of that strategy was expected to satisfy a much more demanding test.

Of course, neither participant should be required to prove more than their actual claims entail. The person defending Israel's conduct need not establish that every military decision was perfect, just as I need not demonstrate that every military decision was wrong.

But when someone confidently claims that an existing strategy was justified, while requiring a critic to eliminate every possible justification before questioning it, the two positions are no longer being evaluated under equivalent standards.

The problem is not skepticism itself.

Skepticism is valuable.

The problem is *selective skepticism*: demanding rigorous evidence for conclusions one dislikes while accepting much weaker support for conclusions one prefers.

**A commitment to critical thinking requires that our standards of evidence remain consistent even when the conclusions threaten our existing beliefs.**

Otherwise, skepticism ceases to function as a method of discovering truth and becomes an instrument for defending conclusions.

### C. Identity-Protective Cognition: When an Idea Becomes Part of the Self

Motivated reasoning helps explain how people may selectively evaluate evidence.

But it does not fully explain why some topics provoke such intense resistance to reconsideration.

For that, another concept is useful: *identity-protective cognition*.

Researchers studying cultural cognition, including Dan Kahan and his colleagues, have explored how people's judgments can be influenced by the relationship between information and their social identities or cultural commitments.

The basic proposition is that beliefs are not always held as isolated assessments of factual evidence.

Sometimes they are embedded within a broader understanding of who we are, which communities we belong to, and which values we regard as fundamental.

When this happens, questioning a belief can feel like questioning something much larger than a factual proposition.

A political position may become intertwined with loyalty, moral character, social belonging, historical memory, or a person's understanding of justice.

Under those circumstances, information that threatens the belief may also seem to threaten the identity connected to it.

Consider someone for whom support for Israel is closely associated with concern for Jewish security, the historical persecution of Jewish people, or the importance of a Jewish national homeland.

Those concerns may be deeply sincere, historically grounded, and morally significant.

But it is possible for a person to become so attached to defending a particular political or military institution that criticism of the institution is experienced as a challenge to the underlying values associated with it.

A question about the proportionality of a military operation can then be heard not simply as a question about military conduct, but as an attack on Israel's legitimacy, its population's security, or the moral commitments of the person defending it.

This does not mean that Zionism necessarily produces defensive reasoning. Nor does it mean that criticism of Israel is always free from prejudice, misinformation, or hostility toward Jewish people.

Those possibilities must be assessed on their own merits.

The relevant psychological point is more general: when political judgments become closely connected to identity, the perceived stakes of changing one's mind can become much greater.

This dynamic is not limited to supporters of Israel.

A Palestinian nationalist might defensively reject credible evidence of wrongdoing by a Palestinian armed group. A committed supporter of a political party might minimize misconduct by its leaders. A person deeply invested in a social movement might resist legitimate criticism of its tactics.

The mechanism is not defined by the political position involved, but by the relationship between that position and the person's sense of identity.

In my conversation, the question "Would you rather have Hamas or Israel?" was particularly interesting in this respect.

I had raised concerns about specific military conduct.

The response transformed those concerns into a question about which actor I preferred.

In effect, the conversation moved from evaluating a decision to declaring an allegiance.

This is precisely the kind of shift that can occur when questions about conduct become entangled with questions of identity.

If criticism is interpreted as evidence that someone belongs to an opposing camp, its factual content can become secondary to its perceived social meaning.

Rather than asking whether the criticism is well supported, the listener may begin asking what the criticism implies about the critic.

**The question changes from "Is this claim true?" to "What does accepting this claim mean about whose side I am on?"**

And once that transformation occurs, rational disagreement becomes considerably more difficult.

### D. Cognitive Dissonance: The Discomfort of Contradictory Beliefs

Another relevant concept is *cognitive dissonance*, first developed by psychologist Leon Festinger.

Cognitive dissonance refers to the psychological discomfort that can arise when a person holds conflicting beliefs, attitudes, or commitments, or encounters information that creates a meaningful inconsistency within their existing understanding.

People may respond by revising their beliefs, changing their behavior, introducing additional explanations, or reducing the perceived importance of the conflicting information.

Not every encounter with contrary evidence produces cognitive dissonance. Nor does dissonance necessarily lead to irrationality. It can sometimes motivate genuine reflection and a productive reconsideration of existing beliefs.

But it can also encourage attempts to preserve psychological consistency without fully confronting the underlying contradiction.

Consider a hypothetical person who holds two important beliefs:

"Israel's military campaign is fundamentally necessary and morally defensible."

And:

"The large-scale, avoidable killing of civilians is morally unacceptable."

These beliefs are not inherently contradictory. A person might reasonably distinguish between unavoidable incidental civilian harm during lawful military operations and harm resulting from unlawful or unnecessarily destructive decisions.

But suppose that person encounters substantial evidence suggesting that some civilian deaths may have been foreseeable, excessive, or preventable.

The evidence could create tension between their favorable assessment of the military campaign and their commitment to civilian protection.

One response would be to examine the evidence and determine whether their assessment of the campaign should change.

Another would be to preserve the original assessment by introducing explanations that make reconsideration seem unnecessary.

"War is terrible."

"Hamas is worse."

"Iran is worse."

"You don't understand military strategy."

Again, these statements may contain truths or raise considerations worthy of examination.

But when they substitute for evaluating the disturbing evidence, they can also serve to reduce the pressure to reconsider an established conclusion.

The person need not consciously recognize this process.

They may simply experience the defensive responses as more persuasive or relevant than the original questions.

What makes cognitive dissonance an especially useful concept here is that it provides a possible explanation for why an analytical question can provoke a response that seems disproportionately defensive.

If the question is understood only as an invitation to evaluate evidence, the reasonable response is to examine the evidence.

If the question is experienced as a threat to a valued belief, however, the immediate psychological priority may shift toward resolving that threat.

But I want to emphasize the limits of applying this explanation to my particular interlocutor.

I cannot know whether he experienced cognitive dissonance. I cannot know how closely his views about Israel were connected to his identity, or whether his responses were motivated by discomfort, limited information, genuine strategic disagreement, or some combination of factors.

Those are questions about his internal experience, and the conversation does not provide enough evidence to answer them.

What the conversation does reveal is that the responses repeatedly allowed the original position to remain intact without directly addressing the challenges raised against it.

**The observable pattern is defensive in its argumentative function, even if the psychological motivation behind it remains uncertain.**

That distinction allows the reasoning to be criticized without pretending to know what was happening inside the other person's mind.

### E. How Defensive Reasoning Can Create the Appearance of Intellectual Victory

There is another aspect of this phenomenon that I find particularly revealing.

Defensive reasoning can produce a convincing *feeling* of having won an argument without producing a valid rebuttal.

In the exchange I experienced, my interlocutor's final response was:

"Yeah, see, you don't know."

The tone and structure of that statement suggested that something decisive had been established.

And in one narrow sense, something had been established: I did not possess a comprehensive alternative military strategy.

But that fact was neither surprising nor particularly relevant to the original inquiry.

Nothing in the exchange demonstrated that Israel's strategy was necessary, that feasible alternatives were unavailable, or that the military campaign complied with all applicable obligations to protect civilians.

Nevertheless, the conversation had reached a point where my admission of limited expertise could be interpreted as a defeat.

This is possible because conversational success and logical success are not the same thing.

In ordinary social interactions, people may judge the outcome of a disagreement according to confidence, speed, rhetorical force, emotional composure, or the ability to leave an opponent without an immediate response.

A person who asks a difficult question can make someone else appear uninformed.

A person who introduces a morally charged comparison can force the conversation onto uncomfortable territory.

A person who confidently declares that an opponent does not know what they are talking about may create the impression that the opponent's substantive concerns have been discredited.

But none of these outcomes necessarily establishes that the position being defended is correct.

This distinction becomes especially important when a disagreement concerns complex institutions or specialized decisions.

It is easy to ask a civilian to explain exactly how they would conduct an urban military campaign.

It is much harder to demonstrate, through evidence, that every relevant military decision was justified.

By substituting the first challenge for the second, a defender can obtain a rhetorical advantage without undertaking the difficult work required to support their conclusion.

And if the critic acknowledges uncertainty while the defender expresses confidence, the asymmetry can become even more pronounced.

The critic may appear weak precisely because they are being honest about the limits of their knowledge.

The defender may appear strong precisely because they are not acknowledging comparable limits.

This can reward a style of argumentation in which certainty is valued over justification and confrontation over investigation.

**The ability to end a disagreement is not the same as the ability to resolve it.**

And the ability to expose another person's ignorance does not automatically establish one's own understanding.

### F. A Possible Defensive Sequence

When viewed together, these psychological concepts suggest a possible sequence through which defensive reasoning can develop.

A person begins with a strongly held assessment of a political conflict. That assessment may be supported by genuine evidence, personal experience, moral commitments, or identification with one of the parties involved.

Another person introduces information or questions that complicate the assessment.

The new information does not necessarily disprove the existing position, but it raises uncertainties that could require reconsideration.

If the person is strongly invested in preserving their conclusion, they may respond by emphasizing considerations that support it rather than examining the challenge directly.

They might normalize the troubling evidence, invoke the wrongdoing of opponents, redirect the discussion toward group allegiance, or demand that the critic satisfy an unusually high evidentiary threshold.

If those responses are accepted, the original conclusion survives without undergoing substantial scrutiny.

If they are challenged, the person may become more insistent, eventually treating the critic's limitations or uncertainty as sufficient grounds to dismiss the inquiry.

The important point is not that every conversation follows this sequence or that the sequence proves a particular psychological motive.

It is that the sequence can create a self-protective pattern.

Each response offers another way to preserve the existing conclusion without confronting the question most likely to challenge it.

In that sense, the discussion becomes less an attempt to determine whether the belief is justified and more an exercise in maintaining its defensibility.

This can happen even when the person experiences themselves as sincerely committed to truth.

And it can become self-reinforcing.

Each successful redirection reduces the likelihood that the original belief will be seriously examined. Each perceived argumentative victory may strengthen confidence in the belief. Over time, the person may become increasingly practiced at defending a conclusion without becoming correspondingly better at evaluating the evidence concerning it.

The result is a paradox: someone can become more sophisticated at arguing for a position while becoming less open to discovering whether it is correct.

Research on political cognition provides evidence of selective information processing, although researchers continue to debate the relative roles of identity, prior knowledge, reasoning ability, and other mechanisms.

For example, some studies suggest that politically sophisticated individuals can be particularly effective at constructing defenses of established positions. Other research cautions that apparent identity-driven effects can sometimes be partly explained by differences in prior factual beliefs.

This is an important qualification.

We should not assume that intelligence, education, or technical expertise necessarily eliminate motivated reasoning. But neither should we treat every disagreement or every confident defense of a prior belief as evidence of psychological bias.

The task is to examine how the person is reasoning.

Are competing claims being evaluated under comparable standards? Is contrary evidence actually being considered? Are the arguments responsive to the questions raised? Is the person willing to acknowledge uncertainty or revise a conclusion?

These observable features offer a better basis for evaluating a conversation than speculating about hidden motives.

### G. The Uncomfortable Possibility: I Am Vulnerable to the Same Mechanisms

At this point, there is an important objection that must be directed toward my own analysis.

If motivated reasoning and identity-protective cognition can influence my interlocutor, why should I assume they are not influencing me?

The answer is that I should not.

It would be contradictory to criticize someone for defending a preferred conclusion without sufficient scrutiny while assuming that my own conclusions are automatically immune from the same problem.

I have strong moral concerns about the destruction and loss of life in Gaza. Those concerns influence which questions I find urgent and which evidence attracts my attention.

That does not make the concerns illegitimate.

But it does mean that I have an obligation to distinguish between the evidence that establishes a conclusion and the moral commitments that make the conclusion important to me.

For example, I must acknowledge that an enormous casualty disparity does not independently establish that individual attacks were indiscriminate or disproportionate under international humanitarian law.

I must remain open to evidence that particular military operations were directed at legitimate targets, that feasible precautions were taken, and that certain less destructive alternatives were not realistically available.

I must also be willing to distinguish between substantiated findings, disputed allegations, and conclusions that remain uncertain.

If evidence establishes that a particular operation met the relevant legal requirements, I should be prepared to acknowledge that conclusion even if I remain deeply critical of the broader campaign.

Likewise, a person defending Israel should be prepared to acknowledge credible evidence of unlawful or avoidable civilian harm, even when doing so complicates their favorable assessment of Israel's military objectives.

Applying consistent standards does not require pretending that all claims have equal evidentiary support or that all conduct is morally equivalent.

It requires allowing the evidence to influence our judgments regardless of which position the evidence supports.

This is where genuine intellectual humility becomes important.

Intellectual humility is not the refusal to reach conclusions. Nor is it the belief that every political controversy must be treated as an evenly balanced dispute.

It is the recognition that our beliefs are subject to error, that our knowledge is incomplete, and that our confidence should be proportionate to the evidence available.

It is entirely possible to reach a strong conclusion while remaining open to revising it.

And it is possible to acknowledge uncertainty without surrendering the legitimacy of the questions that produced it.

In fact, this is precisely the distinction I was attempting to maintain during the original conversation.

I was not claiming to possess all the answers.

I was asking whether the questions deserved investigation.

### H. From Defending an Identity to Examining a Claim

This brings us to what I consider the deepest psychological insight of the exchange.

Political disagreement becomes especially unproductive when people experience criticism of a claim as criticism of themselves.

When that happens, evidence is no longer evaluated solely for its factual significance. It may also be evaluated according to what accepting it would mean for one's identity, loyalties, or existing worldview.

Under those conditions, conceding uncertainty can feel like weakness. Acknowledging wrongdoing by a favored actor can feel like betrayal. And admitting that an opponent has raised a legitimate question can feel like surrendering something much larger than the question itself.

The result can be a defensive relationship with information.

Instead of treating evidence as something that may help refine an understanding of reality, the person treats it as something that must be managed in order to preserve an existing position.

But there is another way to approach disagreement.

A person can regard their beliefs as provisional conclusions rather than permanent commitments.

They can recognize that the legitimacy of a question is independent of who asks it.

They can distinguish between the moral worth of a population and the conduct of a government or armed group claiming to represent it.

They can acknowledge that an adversary may make a valid criticism and that an ally may commit serious wrongdoing.

And they can understand that changing one's mind in response to evidence is not a failure of integrity.

It is one of the clearest expressions of it.

Returning to the conversation that prompted this essay, I cannot say with certainty why my interlocutor responded as he did.

Perhaps he was protecting a strongly held identity. Perhaps he was uncomfortable with the implications of the questions. Perhaps he sincerely believed that military alternatives were impossible and lacked the information necessary to explain why.

What I can say is that his responses did not adequately engage the inquiry.

And I can say that the psychological mechanisms examined here provide plausible explanations for why similar patterns arise in political discussions.

The most important distinction is therefore not between people who possess beliefs and people who do not.

Everyone possesses beliefs. Everyone approaches new information with some combination of prior assumptions, values, experiences, and expectations.

The distinction is between how we relate to those beliefs.

Do we treat them as conclusions that remain accountable to evidence?

Or do we treat them as positions that must be defended regardless of what the evidence may reveal?

**The danger begins when protecting a belief becomes more important than discovering whether it is true.**

And the remedy is not simply to become more skilled at identifying fallacies in other people's arguments.

It is to develop a standard of inquiry that applies equally to our own.

A productive conversation does not require that participants arrive without convictions. It requires that those convictions remain open to examination.

It requires the willingness to distinguish uncertainty from ignorance, criticism from hostility, and disagreement from disloyalty.

Most importantly, it requires recognizing that a question can remain legitimate even when neither participant knows the answer.

In the end, the psychological problem and the logical problem converge.

When the goal of a conversation becomes defending a conclusion, an unanswered question can be treated as an obstacle to overcome.

When the goal is understanding reality, an unanswered question becomes a reason to investigate further.

That difference determines whether a disagreement produces greater understanding or merely reinforces the positions with which its participants began.

It also suggests an important qualification to my critique.

If asking for an alternative military strategy can sometimes be a legitimate challenge, how do we distinguish that challenge from the defensive maneuver I have been describing?

To answer that fairly, the next step is to examine the strongest reasonable version of my interlocutor's objection—not merely the dismissive way he expressed it.

## VII. The Steelman: When Asking for an Alternative Is Actually Reasonable

Up to this point, I have examined the ways in which my interlocutor's responses redirected the conversation away from the questions I was raising. I have argued that demanding a comprehensive alternative military strategy as a prerequisite for criticism is logically unjustified, and that such demands can function as a mechanism for protecting existing beliefs from scrutiny.

But there is an important possibility that deserves more serious consideration.

What if my interlocutor was attempting, however imperfectly, to raise a legitimate objection?

What if his demand that I propose an alternative was not entirely disconnected from the question I was asking, but instead reflected a real difficulty inherent in evaluating military decisions?

After all, it is easy to criticize the consequences of a strategy. It is considerably more difficult to establish what should have been done differently, particularly when the alternatives involve competing risks, incomplete information, and an adversary actively attempting to frustrate one's objectives.

If I am going to criticize someone's reasoning for failing to engage with opposing arguments, I should be willing to subject my own position to the strongest version of the objection being raised.

This is the principle of *steelmanning*: reconstructing an opposing argument in its strongest reasonable form, rather than attacking only its weakest or most poorly expressed version.

Doing so does not mean assuming that the opposing position is correct. It means recognizing that an argument deserves to be evaluated according to its strongest relevant considerations.

And in this case, the strongest version of my interlocutor's objection is considerably more sophisticated than the response he actually offered.

### A. The Strongest Version of the Objection

Let us imagine that my interlocutor had responded differently.

Rather than saying, "Well, you tell me what they should do," suppose he had said something like this:

"I understand why the scale of Palestinian casualties concerns you. It concerns me too. But we also have to consider the military problem Israel was attempting to solve.

"Hamas carried out attacks against Israeli civilians, took hostages, and retained the capacity to conduct further violence. Its forces have operated through tunnel networks and within densely populated urban environments, creating serious difficulties for military operations.

"If Israel's objective was to dismantle Hamas's military capabilities while protecting its population against future attacks, how could it realistically accomplish that objective without causing substantial civilian harm?

"Airstrikes can cause civilian casualties, but ground operations also expose soldiers and civilians to serious risks. Targeted raids may be appropriate in some circumstances but insufficient for broader military objectives. Ceasefires may reduce immediate casualties but raise questions about the future security threat. Negotiations might secure particular objectives while leaving others unresolved.

"So before concluding that Israel's strategy was unnecessarily destructive, shouldn't we examine what alternatives were realistically available and what consequences those alternatives might have produced?"

This is a substantially stronger argument.

It acknowledges the humanitarian concern rather than dismissing it.

It identifies relevant strategic constraints.

It recognizes that alternatives must be evaluated not merely by their theoretical appeal, but by their practical feasibility and likely consequences.

Most importantly, it asks a question that is directly relevant to the original inquiry.

And my answer would be straightforward:

**Yes. We absolutely should examine those alternatives. That was precisely what I was proposing.**

The strength of this reconstructed objection is that it invites an investigation rather than attempting to terminate one.

It identifies areas of uncertainty that both participants should take seriously.

It also exposes a genuine weakness that can arise in criticism of military operations: evaluating a strategy entirely by its undesirable consequences, without adequately considering the consequences of feasible alternatives.

That weakness deserves attention.

### B. The Difference Between a Bad Outcome and a Bad Decision

One of the most important principles in evaluating decisions is that an undesirable outcome does not necessarily establish that the decision producing it was unreasonable.

This is sometimes described as the problem of *outcome bias*.

We may judge a decision harshly because we know that its consequences were tragic, even when the decision was made under conditions where those consequences could not reasonably have been anticipated or avoided.

Conversely, we may regard a decision favorably because it produced a good result, even when the reasoning behind it was reckless or poorly informed.

But the quality of a decision and the quality of its eventual outcome are not identical.

Consider an emergency physician treating a critically injured patient.

The physician must choose among several interventions, each carrying risks. Some may be more aggressive, others more conservative. The patient's condition may be deteriorating, and the available information may be incomplete.

Suppose the physician chooses an intervention that carries substantial risks, and the patient dies.

That tragic outcome does not, by itself, establish that the physician made the wrong decision.

A responsible evaluation would examine what the physician knew, what alternatives were available, what risks were reasonably foreseeable, and whether the decision complied with accepted professional standards.

Likewise, if a patient survives despite a reckless decision, the favorable outcome does not automatically justify the physician's conduct.

The relevant question is whether the decision was reasonable in light of the information and alternatives available at the time.

The same principle applies to military operations.

The fact that a military campaign causes enormous destruction does not independently establish that every decision contributing to that destruction was strategically irrational or legally prohibited.

War involves circumstances in which even carefully planned operations can cause grave harm.

However, this principle also works in the opposite direction.

The fact that commanders faced difficult decisions does not establish that the decisions they made were reasonable.

The existence of operational constraints does not prove that every method employed was necessary.

And the possibility that some civilian casualties were unavoidable does not establish that all civilian casualties were unavoidable.

**A difficult decision is not automatically a justified decision, just as a tragic outcome is not automatically proof of an unjustified one.**

The purpose of investigation is to determine which conclusion the available evidence supports.

### C. The Real Importance of Feasible Alternatives

Here the request for alternatives becomes genuinely relevant.

When evaluating a proposed course of action, it often makes sense to compare it against the other options reasonably available.

A decision cannot be fully understood in isolation from the constraints under which it was made.

Suppose military commanders face a choice between several methods of pursuing a legitimate objective.

One option involves an airstrike. Another involves a ground operation. A third involves delaying the attack while gathering additional intelligence.

Each option may involve different risks.

An airstrike might reduce exposure of soldiers but increase risks to surrounding civilians. A ground operation might permit closer target verification in some circumstances while also exposing civilians to prolonged fighting. Delaying an operation might create an opportunity to obtain better information, but could also allow an adversary to escape or prepare further attacks.

There is no reason to assume in advance that one method is always superior.

The correct choice depends on the circumstances.

And those circumstances are exactly what a serious investigation should examine.

But this reveals the crucial difference between two claims.

The first is:

"We cannot establish that the existing strategy was inferior without considering feasible alternatives."

The second is:

"Because you cannot personally identify a superior alternative, the existing strategy should be presumed justified."

The first is a legitimate analytical consideration when making a comparative claim.

The second is not.

It substitutes a limitation in the critic's knowledge for evidence about the available alternatives.

If someone asserts that there was no feasible way to reduce civilian casualties while achieving a particular military objective, that is itself a substantive claim about the circumstances.

It requires support.

One must examine what alternatives were available, what tradeoffs they involved, and why they were considered inadequate.

The fact that an ordinary citizen cannot immediately identify a better strategy does not answer those questions.

Nor does the inability to establish a superior alternative eliminate other grounds for criticism. An attack can be unlawful because it deliberately targets civilians, for example, regardless of whether a critic has devised an alternative way to accomplish the broader campaign's objectives.

So the legitimate demand to consider alternatives must be kept within its proper scope.

**Alternatives matter when they are relevant to the claim being evaluated. They do not become a universal prerequisite for questioning any existing decision.**

### D. Minimizing Civilian Harm Does Not Mean Ignoring Military Objectives

There is another qualification that strengthens the opposing argument.

When I asked whether Israel could have pursued its military objectives while minimizing civilian casualties, I was implicitly describing a problem involving more than one objective.

Military operations are not ordinarily designed to minimize civilian casualties in isolation from every other consideration.

They also involve legitimate military objectives, operational constraints, force protection, intelligence uncertainty, and the need to respond to threats.

A strategy that produces fewer civilian casualties but completely fails to accomplish a necessary military objective may not be a straightforwardly superior strategy.

Conversely, a strategy that achieves substantial military objectives while causing avoidable or excessive civilian harm may be strategically successful in one respect while remaining morally or legally indefensible in another.

This distinction is important because different standards are being applied.

**Strategic effectiveness** asks whether an operation achieved its intended military or security objectives.

**Moral justification** asks whether the action and its consequences can be defended according to relevant ethical principles.

**Legal compliance** asks whether the operation satisfied the applicable rules of international humanitarian law.

These questions overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

A strategically effective operation can violate international law. A lawful operation may fail to achieve its broader strategic objectives. A decision might satisfy a particular legal requirement while still raising serious moral or political objections.

This is why the phrase "Was Israel minimizing civilian deaths?" requires some precision.

If minimizing deaths means achieving the lowest possible casualty figure regardless of military circumstances, that is not an accurate statement of the applicable legal standard.

International humanitarian law does not require military commanders to abandon every legitimate objective whenever civilian casualties are possible.

But it does impose binding restrictions on attacks, including the principles of distinction and proportionality and the obligation to take all feasible precautions to avoid or minimize incidental civilian harm.

It prohibits certain actions outright, regardless of their strategic utility.

And it requires that decisions be assessed in light of the relevant information and circumstances.

These obligations are not suspended merely because an adversary operates in civilian areas or commits its own violations of international law.

Accordingly, the question becomes more precise:

**Did Israel's military operations comply with the applicable restrictions on targeting and civilian harm, including the requirement to take all feasible precautions, while pursuing their stated military objectives?**

That question is neither simplistic nor unrealistic.

It recognizes the existence of genuine military constraints without treating those constraints as a blanket justification for whatever actions were taken.

### E. The Danger of Imaginary Alternatives

A serious critique must also avoid treating hypothetical alternatives as though their feasibility were self-evident.

It is easy to suggest that Israel should have relied more heavily on targeted raids, intelligence operations, negotiations, different targeting methods, or other approaches.

But identifying a conceivable alternative is not the same as demonstrating that it was feasible or superior under the circumstances.

A targeted raid may reduce collateral damage in one situation while creating greater danger in another.

An intelligence operation may require information that was unavailable.

A negotiated arrangement may depend on the willingness of other parties to cooperate.

Delaying an attack may reduce some risks while creating others.

There are also questions of scale.

An approach capable of neutralizing a small number of specific threats may not necessarily achieve the broader military objectives associated with a sustained campaign.

These difficulties cannot simply be ignored.

If I claimed that a particular alternative would definitely have achieved Israel's stated objectives with substantially fewer civilian deaths, I would have an obligation to demonstrate why that claim was credible.

And if I could not provide supporting evidence, my confidence should be reduced accordingly.

This is a legitimate demand for intellectual discipline.

But again, it does not establish that the existing military strategy was justified.

It merely establishes that evaluating alternatives requires serious analysis.

There is a symmetrical error worth identifying here.

A critic may imagine an appealing alternative and assume that it was feasible without investigating the constraints.

A defender may imagine constraints that would make every alternative infeasible and assume that those constraints existed without providing evidence.

Both errors substitute speculation for investigation.

**The relevant question is not whether we can imagine a better alternative or imagine reasons that no better alternative could exist. It is what the available evidence establishes about the alternatives that were actually feasible.**

That standard applies equally to criticism and defense.

### F. The Point Where a Legitimate Objection Becomes a Deflection

We can now identify more precisely where my interlocutor's argument went wrong.

The problem was not that he raised the issue of alternatives.

The problem was the way he used my inability to provide one.

There is a meaningful difference between asking:

"What alternatives were available, and why should we believe they would have produced better outcomes?"

And declaring:

"You cannot produce an alternative, so your concerns are invalid."

The first question requests information relevant to evaluating a claim.

The second attempts to impose a condition on whether the concern may be considered at all.

One invites analysis.

The other can terminate analysis.

To see the distinction, imagine that my interlocutor had genuinely believed that Israel faced constraints making substantially less destructive operations impossible.

He could have explained those constraints.

He could have identified military assessments supporting his conclusion.

He could have discussed why specific alternative tactics might have failed.

He could have acknowledged that the available evidence was incomplete while maintaining that Israel's security concerns made certain decisions understandable.

Any of those responses could have advanced the conversation.

But simply saying "You don't know" did none of these things.

It established only that I lacked particular knowledge.

That might have been relevant if I had claimed to possess military expertise or asserted with certainty that a specific alternative was superior.

But I had made neither claim.

I was asking whether the existing decisions had been adequately justified, and whether there were reasons to believe civilian harm could have been reduced.

The distinction is especially important because a request for alternatives can be framed in a way that makes the original question practically impossible to pursue.

Suppose someone must first demonstrate a complete, feasible, operationally effective military strategy before they are permitted to question a campaign's humanitarian consequences.

That standard would exclude nearly everyone outside the military establishment from meaningful criticism.

And yet the person defending the existing strategy might possess no comparable expertise.

The result would be an unjustified asymmetry: one participant could confidently defend military decisions while requiring the other to demonstrate professional military competence before questioning them.

This is not a serious standard of evidence.

It is a shifting standard of participation.

**A demand for alternatives becomes a deflection when it is used to replace the question under examination rather than help answer it.**

### G. The Strongest Criticism Requires Intellectual Reciprocity

I think this is where the most productive lesson emerges.

The best response to my interlocutor would not have been to insist that alternatives were irrelevant.

They were relevant.

Nor would it have been to claim that the scale of Palestinian deaths conclusively established that Israel's strategy was unlawful or unnecessarily destructive.

The aggregate casualty disparity alone cannot establish those conclusions.

Instead, I could have acknowledged the legitimate concern embedded within his objection while refusing the unreasonable burden he attempted to impose.

I might have said:

"You're right that military alternatives have to be evaluated realistically. I'm not assuming that there was some obvious strategy that would have eliminated Hamas's threat without substantial risk or civilian harm.

"But that's exactly why I think this deserves investigation.

"If you believe Israel's strategy was necessary, what evidence supports that conclusion? What alternatives were available? What constraints made less destructive approaches infeasible? How were civilian casualties considered in those decisions?

"I'm open to the possibility that some operations were justified under circumstances I don't fully understand. But are you open to the possibility that some were unnecessarily destructive or failed to meet the relevant legal requirements?

"Because if neither of us knows enough to answer those questions confidently, shouldn't that make us more interested in investigating them, rather than less?"

This response concedes what should be conceded without surrendering the original argument.

It recognizes that military expertise matters.

It acknowledges uncertainty.

It accepts that feasible alternatives must be evaluated rather than merely imagined.

And it keeps the burden of evidence connected to the claims each participant is actually making.

Most importantly, it asks both participants to accept the same intellectual obligation: to consider evidence that might undermine their preferred conclusions.

That reciprocity is essential.

If I expect someone defending Israel to consider evidence of unlawful or unnecessarily destructive operations, I must also be willing to consider evidence that particular operations were lawful, necessary, or conducted with feasible precautions.

If they expect me to acknowledge the military difficulties of confronting Hamas, they must also be willing to consider whether Israel's responses to those difficulties were justified.

Neither side should be permitted to treat uncertainty as grounds for automatically accepting its preferred position.

And neither should be allowed to transform the other person's willingness to acknowledge uncertainty into a rhetorical defeat.

### H. What This Reveals About the Original Exchange

Steelmanning my interlocutor's objection does not weaken the central criticism of his reasoning.

It makes that criticism more precise.

The strongest version of his argument raises a legitimate question about feasible alternatives, competing risks, and the difficulty of evaluating military decisions.

But the argument he actually offered did not develop those considerations.

It did not provide evidence that less destructive alternatives were unavailable.

It did not identify the relevant operational constraints.

It did not distinguish between the legality of individual attacks and the broader strategic effectiveness of the campaign.

It did not address the specific concerns about civilian casualties that prompted my questions.

Instead, it converted my inability to prescribe a comprehensive replacement strategy into a reason to dismiss the inquiry.

That is the critical failure.

The legitimate concern contained within his objection was that military criticism should account for practical constraints and available alternatives.

The illegitimate conclusion was that someone who cannot independently resolve those constraints has no grounds to question the decisions made.

The first is a useful standard for serious analysis.

The second would make serious analysis inaccessible to almost everyone.

And this distinction extends far beyond the conflict that prompted the conversation.

We should be cautious about critics who confidently promise simple solutions to extraordinarily complicated problems.

But we should be equally cautious about defenders of powerful institutions who invoke complexity to suggest that their decisions should not be questioned.

Complexity is not proof that a criticism is correct.

It is not proof that the existing decision is correct, either.

It is a reason to examine the available evidence more carefully.

And perhaps most importantly, it is a reason to distinguish what we know from what we merely assume.

That is what a serious discussion could have accomplished.

My interlocutor could have challenged my understanding of military strategy without dismissing my questions. I could have acknowledged the limits of my knowledge without abandoning the concerns that prompted the inquiry.

We could have examined competing claims, clarified the relevant standards, and identified what additional information would be necessary to reach a responsible conclusion.

We might still have disagreed.

But we would at least have been disagreeing about the substance of the issue rather than about whether I had earned the right to ask questions.

That, ultimately, is the difference between using a challenge to deepen an investigation and using it to avoid one.

**The inability to produce a superior alternative is a reason to be cautious about claiming that one exists. It is not a reason to assume the existing approach is justified.**

A productive conversation must leave room for both propositions.

And having established that distinction, we can turn to a more practical question: what would the original conversation have looked like if both participants had approached it as an opportunity for genuine inquiry rather than an argument to be won?

## VIII. How the Conversation Could Have Been Productive

Having examined the logical failures, psychological mechanisms, and legitimate counterarguments involved in my original exchange, it is worth considering a different possibility.

What would the conversation have looked like if both participants had approached it as an opportunity to investigate a difficult question rather than defend their respective positions?

This question matters because identifying defective reasoning is only part of the challenge. It is relatively easy to recognize when someone has changed the subject, shifted the burden of proof, or substituted an attack on a critic's qualifications for an answer to their argument.

It is considerably more difficult to establish a conversational framework that encourages better reasoning in the first place.

And that distinction is important.

The goal of a productive discussion should not simply be to become more skilled at identifying an opponent's fallacies. Doing so may help us recognize when an argument fails, but it does not necessarily bring us closer to understanding the underlying issue.

A person can correctly identify every logical error made by someone else and still contribute very little to determining what is actually true.

The deeper objective is to create conditions in which competing claims can be meaningfully examined, uncertainty can be acknowledged, and conclusions can be revised when the evidence warrants it.

In my conversation about Israel's military conduct, several relatively simple changes could have transformed the exchange from a defensive confrontation into a productive investigation.

### A. Begin by Establishing What Is Actually Being Questioned

The first requirement of productive disagreement is conceptual clarity.

Before evaluating a claim, both participants should understand what the claim actually is.

This may sound obvious, but much of the dysfunction in my original conversation arose because the subject repeatedly changed without either participant explicitly recognizing the shift.

I began by asking whether the enormous scale of Palestinian casualties warranted scrutiny of Israel's military decisions.

My interlocutor responded as though I were questioning Israel's right to protect its population, expressing sympathy for Hamas, or asserting that I possessed a superior military strategy.

These were not the positions I had advanced.

A more productive exchange would have begun by clarifying the precise question under consideration.

I might have said:

"I'm not arguing that Israel has no legitimate security concerns, and I'm not defending Hamas's attacks against civilians. I'm asking whether Israel's military response has been conducted in a way that adequately protects civilian life, and whether less destructive methods were feasible."

This clarification would serve two purposes.

First, it would distinguish criticism of military conduct from broader judgments about political legitimacy or allegiance.

Second, it would establish that my position was investigative rather than conclusory.

I was not claiming to have already established that every military operation was unlawful or that a particular alternative would necessarily have produced better results.

I was asking what evidence would be necessary to determine whether the existing strategy was justified.

Once this distinction is established, a productive interlocutor can respond to the actual question.

They might say:

"I think the military constraints were more severe than you're accounting for."

Or:

"I agree the scale of civilian harm deserves investigation, but I don't think the casualty ratio tells us enough to judge the campaign."

Either response would create an opportunity for substantive discussion.

The first introduces a factual claim about operational constraints.

The second introduces an evidentiary challenge concerning how casualty statistics should be interpreted.

Both can be examined.

Neither requires abandoning the original inquiry.

**Productive disagreement begins by ensuring that both participants are evaluating the same proposition.**

Without that foundation, a conversation can generate considerable argument without ever establishing what the disagreement is actually about.

### B. Separate Empirical Questions from Moral and Legal Judgments

The second improvement would be to distinguish the different kinds of questions involved.

Discussions about warfare frequently combine factual claims, strategic assessments, moral judgments, and legal conclusions.

These dimensions are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Consider the statement:

"Israel's military campaign has caused an unacceptable level of civilian harm."

This contains several questions that should be distinguished.

There is an empirical question: How many civilians have been killed or injured, and under what circumstances?

There is a causal question: Which military decisions contributed to those outcomes?

There is a strategic question: What objectives were being pursued, and were the methods effective?

There is a legal question: Did particular attacks comply with the applicable principles of international humanitarian law?

And there is a moral question: Even where conduct might satisfy minimum legal requirements, should it nevertheless be condemned as unjustified or excessively destructive?

Each question may require different evidence and different standards of evaluation.

For example, determining the number of people killed requires reliable casualty records and an understanding of their limitations.

Determining whether particular attacks were disproportionate under international humanitarian law requires examining anticipated civilian harm and concrete military advantage in the circumstances of those attacks.

Determining whether a broader military strategy was effective requires examining its stated objectives and actual consequences.

And determining whether a campaign was morally justified requires ethical judgments that cannot be reduced to casualty statistics alone.

A discussion becomes confused when conclusions from one category are treated as though they automatically settle another.

A high death ratio does not independently establish that every military operation was unlawful.

But neither does the existence of a legitimate military objective establish that all methods employed to pursue it were morally or legally acceptable.

Likewise, the fact that Hamas committed atrocities against Israeli civilians is relevant to understanding Israel's security concerns, but does not determine whether Israel's subsequent conduct complied with its own obligations.

By separating these questions, participants can identify more precisely where they agree and disagree.

Perhaps both agree that the civilian death toll is catastrophic but disagree about how much of that harm was avoidable.

Perhaps both agree that Hamas posed a serious threat but disagree about whether Israel's chosen response was strategically effective.

Perhaps both agree on the factual circumstances of a particular attack but disagree about its moral implications.

These are meaningful disagreements.

They identify specific propositions that can be investigated or debated.

The alternative is to collapse every question into a general contest over which side is justified.

And once that happens, evidence about particular actions becomes subordinate to defending an overall political position.

**The more precisely we identify the question, the more precisely we can identify the evidence needed to answer it.**

### C. Match the Burden of Proof to the Claim Being Made

Another essential requirement is to establish fair standards of evidence.

In the original exchange, my interlocutor demanded that I produce a superior military strategy before my concerns could be taken seriously.

But that demand was disproportionate to the claim I had actually made.

I was asking whether the existing strategy was justified and whether available alternatives had been adequately considered.

I was not claiming to possess a complete replacement.

A productive conversation would recognize that different claims carry different evidentiary responsibilities.

If I claim that a particular military operation was indiscriminate, I should identify evidence supporting that allegation.

If I claim that a specific alternative strategy would have produced fewer civilian casualties while achieving the same objectives, I should explain why that alternative was feasible and likely to be superior.

But if my interlocutor claims that Israel had no feasible less destructive options, that assertion also requires evidence.

And if he claims that the military campaign adequately minimized civilian harm, he should be prepared to explain the basis for that conclusion.

Neither participant should be allowed to treat their preferred position as self-evident while demanding extraordinary proof from the other.

This does not mean that the evidentiary burden must always be identical.

Different propositions require different kinds and degrees of support.

It means that the burden should correspond to what a person is actually asserting.

An especially important distinction is between asking whether a claim has been justified and asserting that the opposite claim has been proven.

"I am not convinced that this strategy was necessary" is not equivalent to "I have demonstrated that this strategy was unnecessary."

The first expresses a judgment about the sufficiency of available evidence.

The second makes a positive claim requiring its own support.

A serious discussion should preserve that distinction.

Similarly, uncertainty about an alternative strategy does not justify confidently concluding that no better alternative existed.

The appropriate response to incomplete evidence is to identify what remains unknown and how those uncertainties affect the conclusions being drawn.

**Intellectual consistency requires that we apply comparable standards of scrutiny to claims we favor and claims we oppose.**

Without that consistency, the conversation becomes less about evaluating evidence and more about allocating evidentiary burdens in ways that protect our preferred conclusions.

### D. Replace Rhetorical Challenges with Investigable Questions

The most practical improvement would be to transform rhetorical challenges into questions that can actually be investigated.

Consider the difference between the following responses:

"Well, what would you do instead?"

And:

"What alternatives were realistically available, and what evidence suggests they would have reduced civilian harm without undermining legitimate military objectives?"

The first question may be legitimate in some contexts, but when delivered as a challenge to the critic's qualifications, it can become a conversational dead end.

The second identifies a subject for investigation.

It asks about feasible options, likely consequences, and the evidence needed to compare competing decisions.

It also permits a perfectly reasonable answer:

"I don't know yet. Let's examine what the available evidence shows."

That answer should not be treated as a defeat.

It is often the appropriate starting point for serious inquiry.

In the context of Israel's military campaign, we could have identified several concrete areas for investigation.

We could examine the distinction between civilian and combatant casualties, the reliability of available estimates, and the circumstances in which deaths occurred.

We could investigate particular military operations, including their intended objectives, anticipated civilian harm, and the precautions undertaken.

We could consider what alternative tactics were realistically available, what limitations applied to those alternatives, and whether the decisions made were consistent with international humanitarian law.

We could also examine the broader effectiveness of the campaign, including whether its military achievements translated into sustainable security or whether its humanitarian and political consequences undermined those objectives.

None of these inquiries requires either participant to arrive with a complete answer.

They require a willingness to examine evidence and recognize the limits of what can presently be established.

That is the essential difference between a rhetorical challenge and a productive question.

A rhetorical challenge often aims to put the other person on the defensive.

A productive question aims to identify information that would improve our understanding.

The same sentence can sometimes serve either purpose depending on its context and how the speaker responds to the answer.

"What would you do instead?" can be a sincere invitation to explore alternatives.

Or it can become a test designed to disqualify criticism.

The distinction becomes apparent when the questioner encounters uncertainty.

Do they respond by exploring the issue further?

Or do they treat the absence of an immediate answer as permission to dismiss the original concern?

**A question is intellectually productive when its answer, including an acknowledgment of uncertainty, can move the investigation forward.**

### E. Ask What Evidence Would Change Each Person's Mind

Perhaps the most revealing improvement would be to ask a question that is rarely heard in contentious political discussions:

"What evidence would make you reconsider your position?"

This question shifts attention from the conclusions people currently defend to the process through which those conclusions might change.

It asks whether their beliefs are genuinely responsive to evidence.

Suppose my interlocutor maintains that Israel's military campaign was conducted with appropriate precautions and that its civilian casualties were largely unavoidable given the operational circumstances.

I might ask:

"What kind of evidence would lead you to conclude that some military operations were unnecessarily destructive or failed to adequately protect civilians?"

A serious answer could identify several possibilities.

Credible evidence that commanders knowingly disregarded foreseeable civilian harm might change his assessment.

Evidence that attacks were conducted without adequate target verification could raise concerns.

Evidence that less harmful military methods were feasible but were not considered might undermine confidence in the precautions taken.

He might also identify evidence that would strengthen his current position, such as credible documentation of legitimate military targets, targeting procedures, operational constraints, and precautions implemented before particular attacks.

These answers would provide something valuable: a clearer understanding of the standards by which he is evaluating the conduct.

But the question must also be applied to me.

What evidence would lead me to reconsider my own assessment?

I should be prepared to acknowledge that some apparently troubling outcomes may have explanations I do not presently understand.

Evidence that a particular attack was directed at a legitimate military objective, that the anticipated civilian harm was not excessive relative to the concrete and direct military advantage, and that feasible precautions were taken would be relevant to my assessment of that operation.

Evidence demonstrating that a proposed alternative was operationally infeasible could weaken a claim that the alternative should have been used.

And evidence revealing serious limitations in casualty estimates or other factual assumptions should prompt me to revise the conclusions that depend on them.

This does not mean I must abandon a broader criticism of the campaign whenever evidence supports the lawfulness of one particular operation.

Nor does it mean that every piece of contrary evidence requires treating the overall issue as evenly balanced.

It means that my conclusions should remain sensitive to the quality and implications of the evidence.

There is also an important limitation to this approach.

Not every disagreement can be resolved by identifying a single decisive fact. Some disagreements involve competing ethical principles, different assessments of acceptable risk, or uncertainty that cannot realistically be eliminated.

Nevertheless, asking what evidence would change a person's mind helps clarify whether the disagreement is primarily factual, moral, or rooted in commitments that the participants may be unwilling to reconsider.

If neither person can identify any conceivable evidence that would count against their position, the discussion may no longer be functioning as an investigation.

It may instead be an exercise in expressing and defending fixed commitments.

**A belief that is genuinely accountable to evidence must remain open, at least in principle, to evidence that could undermine it.**

### F. What a Better Version of the Conversation Might Have Looked Like

To illustrate these principles, consider how the original exchange might have unfolded under a different conversational approach.

I begin with the concern that prompted the discussion:

"The disparity between Palestinian and Israeli deaths is extraordinary. Doesn't that warrant serious scrutiny? I'm particularly interested in who was killed, whether the civilian harm was avoidable, and whether different military decisions could have reduced it."

My interlocutor responds:

"I agree that civilian deaths are concerning, but the ratio doesn't necessarily tell us whether Israel's operations were justified. Hamas operated in densely populated areas, and Israel faced serious military constraints."

I respond:

"That's fair. The ratio alone doesn't establish whether particular attacks were unlawful. But then shouldn't we investigate the circumstances? What evidence shows that the military took adequate precautions and considered less harmful alternatives?"

He replies:

"That's difficult to determine from outside the military. There are intelligence and operational considerations we don't know."

I say:

"I agree. But doesn't that uncertainty apply in both directions? If we don't know whether better alternatives were available, we also can't confidently conclude that the existing strategy was the least harmful feasible approach."

He considers the point:

"Yes, although I think there are good reasons to believe Israel faced unusually difficult operational conditions."

I respond:

"Then let's look at those conditions. What evidence do we have? And what would convince either of us that particular operations were unnecessarily destructive?"

At this point, the conversation has become meaningfully different.

Neither participant has abandoned their original concerns.

Neither has been required to accept the other's conclusions.

But the disagreement now concerns identifiable claims and evidence.

We might investigate those claims and discover that we disagree about the reliability of particular sources.

We might find evidence supporting some military decisions and undermining others.

We might conclude that the information available is insufficient for confident judgment about certain operations.

Or we might eventually reach different conclusions about the broader campaign despite agreeing on substantial portions of the evidence.

Any of these outcomes would represent progress.

The purpose of a productive conversation is not necessarily to eliminate disagreement.

It is to improve the quality of the understanding from which disagreement proceeds.

### G. Recognizing When a Conversation Is No Longer Productive

There is, however, a practical limitation to this approach.

A productive discussion requires some degree of reciprocal willingness to engage.

One participant cannot single-handedly transform every argument into a sincere investigation.

If someone repeatedly changes the subject, dismisses questions without addressing them, or refuses to apply consistent standards of evidence, there may be little value in continuing indefinitely.

This raises an important question: how can we distinguish a difficult but productive disagreement from one that has ceased to advance understanding?

I think several indicators are useful.

A productive disagreement generally becomes more precise over time.

Participants begin with broad claims, identify the specific points of contention, clarify relevant evidence, and discover which uncertainties actually matter.

Even when they remain opposed, they should become better able to explain what the other person believes and why.

An unproductive disagreement often moves in the opposite direction.

The subject becomes increasingly diffuse. New accusations replace unresolved questions. Participants become preoccupied with exposing each other's ignorance, motives, or political affiliations.

Rather than clarifying the disagreement, the conversation generates additional opportunities for misunderstanding.

One useful test is to ask whether the original question has actually been addressed.

If I raise concerns about civilian casualties and the response becomes a discussion about Iran, political allegiance, or my personal military expertise, I can reasonably attempt to return to the subject.

For example:

"I understand those considerations may be relevant to other questions, but they don't answer what I'm asking. What evidence supports your conclusion that the civilian harm resulting from these operations was adequately minimized?"

If the person engages that question, the discussion may move forward.

If the response is simply another demand that I produce a military strategy, I can identify the distinction once more.

"I'm not claiming to know the best alternative. I'm asking how you know the existing approach was justified."

But if the conversation repeatedly returns to the same evasive structure, continuing may accomplish little.

At that point, disengagement need not represent intellectual surrender.

It may simply reflect recognition that the participants are not currently engaged in the same activity.

One person may be attempting to investigate a claim while the other is primarily concerned with defending a position.

And a meaningful investigation cannot proceed unless both participants are willing to treat the evidence as relevant to their conclusions.

### H. The Goal Is Not Agreement, but Better Reasoning

This brings me to what I consider the most important lesson of the entire exchange.

The objective of rational dialogue should not be to manufacture agreement.

People can examine evidence sincerely and still arrive at different conclusions.

They may assign different weight to uncertain information. They may disagree about the feasibility of particular alternatives. They may hold different ethical assumptions or interpret the same legal standards differently.

Disagreement, in itself, is not evidence of defective reasoning.

The question is whether the disagreement is being conducted in a way that permits meaningful scrutiny of the competing claims.

A person reasoning responsibly should be able to articulate their position, explain the evidence supporting it, acknowledge its limitations, and identify circumstances under which it might need revision.

They should also be capable of recognizing when an opponent raises a legitimate question, even if the question creates discomfort or challenges their preferred conclusion.

These expectations apply equally to me.

If I demand that someone defending Israel examine evidence of civilian harm, I must also be prepared to examine evidence of genuine operational constraints.

If I criticize the use of rhetorical deflections, I must avoid relying on emotional force or casualty figures as substitutes for evidence about the legal and strategic questions I am raising.

And if I expect intellectual humility from my interlocutor, I must be willing to acknowledge the limits of my own knowledge without treating that acknowledgment as a weakness.

What I should not have to do, however, is personally design a comprehensive alternative military campaign before being permitted to ask whether the existing one was justified.

That demand confuses the responsibilities of inquiry with the responsibilities of implementation.

It also mistakes the absence of certainty for the absence of a legitimate question.

The better approach is to recognize that uncertainty often provides the strongest reason to investigate.

When the consequences of a decision are severe, when the available evidence is incomplete, and when competing explanations are plausible, the appropriate response is not to become less curious.

It is to become more careful.

More careful about the claims we make.

More careful about the standards of evidence we apply.

More careful about distinguishing what has been established from what remains uncertain.

And more careful about allowing our moral and political commitments to determine which questions we are willing to entertain.

Returning to the original conversation, I do not know whether a more productive exchange would have changed my interlocutor's assessment of Israel's military campaign.

It might not have.

Nor do I know whether examining the evidence together would have changed my own assessment.

It might have clarified some conclusions, strengthened others, or exposed areas where my confidence exceeded my knowledge.

But that uncertainty is not a reason to avoid the investigation.

It is the very reason to undertake it.

**A successful conversation need not end with one person admitting defeat. It can end with both people understanding more clearly what they know, what they do not know, and what evidence would be needed to resolve the questions that remain.**

That is a far more meaningful form of intellectual progress than forcing someone to acknowledge that they cannot personally solve a problem.

And it reveals the essential distinction that runs throughout this case study.

When a discussion is organized around defending conclusions, uncertainty becomes a vulnerability to exploit.

When it is organized around understanding reality, uncertainty becomes a shared problem to investigate.

The first orientation encourages rhetorical victory.

The second makes learning possible.

And this distinction extends far beyond any particular political conflict. It applies wherever people question decisions made by experts, governments, institutions, or authorities whose judgments carry significant consequences.

The broader danger is that demanding expert-level solutions from critics can become a way of discouraging public scrutiny precisely where that scrutiny is most necessary.

To understand why, we must move beyond the individual exchange and examine the larger relationship between expertise, institutional power, and the public's right to demand justification.

## IX. The Larger Pattern: From Political Debate to Institutional Deference

Although this case study began with a conversation about Israel's military conduct, the reasoning pattern it illustrates is neither unique to that conflict nor confined to political disagreements.

In fact, one of the most revealing aspects of the exchange is how easily its underlying logic can be transferred to other areas of life.

A person questions a consequential decision. Someone defending the decision invokes the complexity of the problem or the expertise of those responsible. The critic is then challenged to produce a superior solution. When they cannot, their original concern is dismissed.

This pattern can appear in discussions about healthcare, economic policy, business management, technological safety, and government decision-making.

And in each setting, it raises the same fundamental question:

**Does the inability to personally solve a complex problem invalidate one's ability to recognize, question, or demand an explanation for the decisions being made about it?**

The answer has implications extending far beyond the quality of individual arguments.

It concerns the relationship between expertise and authority, the distribution of responsibility within institutions, and the conditions necessary for meaningful accountability.

At its most consequential, the reasoning error I encountered can become more than a conversational deflection.

It can become a justification for institutional deference: the idea that those entrusted with making specialized decisions should be presumed correct unless outsiders can demonstrate that they would have made better ones.

That is a dangerous standard, not because expertise is unimportant, but because it confuses expertise with an exemption from scrutiny.

### A. Healthcare: The Patient Who Cannot Perform Surgery

Consider a patient who has been advised to undergo an invasive medical procedure.

The physician recommends surgery, explaining that the patient's condition requires intervention.

The patient, concerned about the risks, asks whether less invasive treatments might be appropriate.

"Is surgery absolutely necessary? Are there alternative treatments? What are the relative risks? Should I seek another opinion?"

Suppose the physician responds:

"Are you a surgeon?"

"No."

"Then tell me how you would treat the condition."

"I don't know exactly how to treat it. That's why I'm consulting a medical professional."

"Well, then you aren't qualified to question my recommendation."

The inadequacy of this response should be obvious.

The patient's inability to practice medicine does not establish that the recommended surgery is necessary.

Nor does the physician's expertise, however genuine, eliminate the responsibility to explain the diagnosis, available treatment options, associated risks, and reasons for recommending one course of action over another.

Indeed, informed consent depends in part on patients receiving sufficient information to make meaningful decisions about their care.

A patient need not possess the knowledge required to perform a procedure in order to evaluate whether they are willing to undergo it.

This does not mean the patient is equally qualified to determine which treatment is medically appropriate.

Expertise matters enormously. A physician's professional assessment should generally carry much more weight than a patient's uninformed speculation about medical procedures.

But placing appropriate weight on expertise is not the same as treating expert judgment as beyond examination.

A patient may reasonably ask for supporting evidence, explanations of uncertainty, alternative treatment options, or a second professional opinion.

These forms of scrutiny do not undermine medical expertise. They make it possible for expertise to operate within a framework of informed decision-making.

The distinction mirrors my original conversation.

Questioning whether a particular intervention is justified does not require possessing the ability to perform the intervention oneself.

And the appropriate response from someone defending the intervention is to explain why it is warranted, not to demand that the questioner demonstrate comparable technical competence.

### B. Economics: The Citizen Who Cannot Design an Entire Economy

The same pattern appears in economic and political debates.

Suppose a government introduces a major economic policy that imposes substantial costs on ordinary citizens.

Perhaps the policy involves new taxes, significant reductions in public services, extensive subsidies, or an expensive industrial initiative.

A citizen questions whether the policy is effective.

"Are the projected benefits supported by evidence? Were less costly alternatives considered? Who bears the financial burden? What happens if the policy fails?"

A defender responds:

"Well, what's your economic plan? How would you manage the entire economy?"

The implication is familiar.

Unless the citizen can formulate a comprehensive alternative economic program, their criticism of the existing policy is supposedly invalid.

But this demand once again substitutes a different question.

The citizen may be questioning the assumptions underlying a particular policy, the reliability of its projected benefits, or the fairness of its consequences.

Those questions do not require designing an entire economic system.

A person can reasonably question whether a government expenditure is wasteful without knowing how to allocate every dollar in the national budget.

They can ask whether a tax policy is regressive without possessing a complete alternative tax code.

They can identify contradictions in an economic forecast without knowing how to construct a superior macroeconomic model.

Of course, if the person claims that a specific alternative policy would produce better outcomes, they should provide evidence supporting that claim.

And policymakers can reasonably explain why an apparently attractive alternative would involve unacceptable tradeoffs.

But neither of those considerations establishes that criticism is illegitimate unless accompanied by a comprehensive replacement plan.

In democratic societies, this distinction is especially important.

Citizens delegate substantial decision-making authority to elected representatives and specialized institutions precisely because modern governance requires expertise and administrative capacity beyond what any individual citizen possesses.

If citizens were permitted to question government policies only when they could personally administer superior alternatives, meaningful public accountability would become practically impossible.

**The right to evaluate how authority is exercised cannot depend on possessing the expertise required to exercise that authority oneself.**

### C. Business: When Questioning Management Becomes a Test of Loyalty

Similar dynamics appear within organizations.

Imagine an employee who notices that a company is committing substantial resources to a project that appears increasingly unlikely to succeed.

Costs are rising, deadlines are repeatedly missed, and the projected benefits remain uncertain.

The employee raises concerns.

"Are we confident this project is still viable? Have we reconsidered the assumptions behind it? Would it make sense to evaluate whether continuing is the best use of our resources?"

A manager responds:

"Well, if you think you know better, why don't you come up with a complete business strategy?"

The employee does not possess the authority, information, or resources needed to formulate and implement a company-wide alternative.

But that limitation has no direct bearing on whether the concerns about the existing project deserve examination.

The project might be entirely justified. The rising costs might be anticipated, the delayed benefits might be substantial, and management might possess information unavailable to the employee.

But those possibilities would need to be explained or examined.

They are not established by the employee's inability to design a superior corporate strategy.

This example introduces another dimension of the problem: the relationship between criticism and organizational loyalty.

Within hierarchical institutions, questioning a decision may be interpreted as questioning the competence or authority of those responsible.

A manager who closely identifies with a project may experience criticism of the project as criticism of their own judgment.

Under those circumstances, the conversation can shift from evaluating the project's merits to evaluating the employee's standing, experience, or qualifications.

"Do you understand how complicated this is?"

"Have you ever managed a project this large?"

"Do you think you could do my job better?"

These questions may sometimes reveal genuine gaps in the employee's understanding.

But they can also redirect attention away from the evidence that initially prompted concern.

The danger is that an organization may begin rewarding confidence and deference over the identification of problems.

Employees learn that raising concerns exposes them to personal scrutiny, while accepting existing decisions requires little explanation.

Over time, this can discourage precisely the kinds of questions that might reveal avoidable failures.

A healthy organization should be capable of distinguishing between criticism intended to undermine authority and criticism intended to improve decisions.

That distinction does not require every objection to be correct.

It requires that objections be evaluated according to their substance rather than the status of the person expressing them.

### D. Technology: You Cannot Question a System Unless You Can Build a Better One

The same reasoning becomes particularly relevant in technological development, where specialized knowledge is often highly concentrated.

Consider a company deploying an automated system that will influence consequential decisions.

Perhaps the system helps determine access to financial services, evaluates job applicants, or supports decisions in healthcare.

Researchers, affected individuals, or members of the public raise concerns about its reliability, fairness, or safety.

A defender responds:

"Do you know how to build a better algorithm?"

The critic may not.

But that is not the question.

The question is whether the system performs as claimed, whether its limitations are adequately understood, whether its outcomes have been appropriately evaluated, and whether its use creates unacceptable risks.

Those questions can be investigated through testing, independent audits, error analysis, documentation, and examination of real-world consequences.

A person harmed by an automated decision should not need to understand the system's architecture before being entitled to question the decision.

Likewise, a researcher identifying a serious safety vulnerability need not be capable of constructing an entirely new technological platform before the vulnerability deserves attention.

There is a broader principle here.

The complexity of a system does not eliminate the need for accountability.

It often increases it.

As systems become more complicated, outsiders may become less able to independently determine whether the claims made about them are accurate.

This creates a greater need for meaningful testing, transparency where appropriate, independent evaluation, and mechanisms for correcting errors.

The alternative is to permit complexity itself to become a form of insulation.

The more difficult a technology is to understand, the easier it becomes to dismiss questions by appealing to the critic's lack of expertise.

But that reverses the proper relationship between complexity and scrutiny.

**A system does not become less accountable because it becomes more difficult for ordinary people to understand.**

Its complexity simply changes the methods through which accountability must be exercised.

### E. The Common Structure: Authority, Complexity, and Burden Shifting

These examples share a recognizable structure.

First, a consequential decision is made by someone possessing specialized knowledge, institutional authority, or access to information unavailable to the critic.

Second, an observer identifies a possible problem and raises questions about the decision's justification, effectiveness, or consequences.

Third, the defender invokes the complexity of the problem or the expertise of the decision-maker.

Fourth, the observer is challenged to demonstrate the ability to produce a superior solution.

Finally, when the observer cannot satisfy that demand, the original concern is dismissed.

The result is an inversion of accountability.

Instead of requiring those exercising authority to provide adequate justification for their decisions, the critic is required to demonstrate competence to replace them.

But the ability to question a decision and the ability to implement an alternative involve different kinds of responsibility.

The person making a decision is responsible for exercising their authority appropriately.

The person evaluating that decision is responsible for making accurate claims, presenting relevant evidence, and acknowledging the limits of their knowledge.

Neither role automatically requires possessing all the capabilities of the other.

A citizen questioning military conduct need not be a commander.

A patient questioning a recommendation need not be a surgeon.

A shareholder questioning financial management need not be a chief executive.

And a person identifying a technological risk need not be capable of developing a complete replacement system.

In each case, the critic's obligation is to provide adequate support for the particular claims they are making.

Their obligation is not to establish that they could personally assume responsibility for the entire institution.

**The logical error is to confuse the qualifications necessary to make a decision with the qualifications necessary to demand that the decision be justified.**

### F. The Hidden Presumption: Whatever Exists Must Be Justified Until Replaced

There is another important feature of this reasoning pattern.

It often grants the existing course of action a special presumption of correctness.

The logic is rarely stated explicitly, but it can be reconstructed as follows:

The current decision exists.

No superior alternative has been demonstrated by the critic.

Therefore, the current decision should continue to be accepted.

This reasoning gives the status quo an advantage that has not been earned through evidence.

The fact that a particular decision was made establishes that someone chose it.

It does not establish that they chose correctly.

Likewise, the fact that an institution currently employs a particular strategy does not establish that it is the best feasible strategy, that its consequences are acceptable, or that its implementation satisfies all relevant obligations.

There may be good reasons for preserving an existing approach.

Changing course can introduce risks. Alternatives can involve substantial costs. Institutions may possess information that makes their decisions more reasonable than they appear to outsiders.

These considerations are legitimate.

But they must be examined rather than presumed.

It is particularly important to distinguish between two statements:

"We have not established that an alternative would be better."

And:

"We have established that the current approach is the best available option."

The first acknowledges a limitation in our knowledge.

The second asserts a conclusion about the available possibilities.

Moving from the first to the second without additional evidence is precisely the kind of reasoning error that appeared in my original conversation.

My inability to propose a complete alternative military strategy did not establish that Israel's existing strategy was the best feasible approach.

It established only that I could not personally demonstrate what the best approach would have been.

And those are very different conclusions.

### G. When Expertise Becomes a Shield Against Accountability

The danger becomes greater when appeals to expertise are used selectively.

In a healthy process of inquiry, expertise helps us evaluate complex claims.

Experts can identify hidden constraints, explain technical considerations, challenge unrealistic assumptions, and provide information that improves our understanding.

The appropriate response to an expert's explanation is to consider the quality of the evidence and reasoning supporting it.

But expertise can also be invoked rhetorically, without providing any of those benefits.

This occurs when someone appeals to the authority or presumed knowledge of a specialist without explaining how that knowledge supports the conclusion being defended.

The distinction is important.

A military analyst who explains why certain proposed tactics were infeasible is contributing substantive expertise.

Someone who merely says, "You don't understand military strategy," is not necessarily doing the same.

A physician who explains why a particular procedure is necessary provides reasons that can be evaluated.

Someone who says, "I'm the doctor, so don't question me," substitutes professional status for explanation.

The existence of expertise is not disputed.

What is disputed is whether expertise has actually been used to justify the particular conclusion.

This distinction becomes especially significant when the person invoking expertise is not the expert themselves.

In my original conversation, neither participant was presenting a detailed operational assessment demonstrating that Israel's military decisions were necessary or adequately protective of civilians.

My interlocutor was not explaining the relevant military constraints through specific evidence.

He was invoking the complexity of military strategy to argue that my questions lacked legitimacy.

Yet he appeared willing to maintain confidence in the existing strategy without satisfying the same technical demands he placed on me.

This is where an appeal to expertise can become an appeal to authority without adequate justification.

The critic is told they lack the knowledge necessary to question a decision.

But the defender's own confidence is not subjected to equivalent scrutiny.

**Expertise should help answer difficult questions. It should not become a reason why difficult questions are forbidden.**

### H. Accountability Does Not Require Universal Access to Every Decision

An important qualification is necessary here.

Demanding accountability does not mean that every individual is entitled to immediate access to every piece of information underlying a consequential decision.

Some forms of expertise depend on confidential information. Medical decisions involve patient privacy. Businesses may possess proprietary information. Military operations may involve intelligence that cannot safely be disclosed.

Nor does accountability require that every technical decision be submitted to public approval.

Institutions often need specialized personnel who can act efficiently within established responsibilities.

The relevant principle is not that expertise should be replaced by direct public control over every decision.

It is that expertise and authority should operate within systems capable of evaluating whether they are being exercised responsibly.

Those systems may include professional standards, independent review, judicial oversight, external audits, legislative scrutiny, investigative journalism, internal reporting mechanisms, or other forms of institutional accountability.

Different settings require different mechanisms.

In medicine, professional guidelines, second opinions, and informed consent help patients evaluate recommendations.

In business, financial reporting, independent audits, and governance processes provide opportunities to examine management decisions.

In technology, testing, documentation, safety evaluations, and independent assessments can help identify weaknesses that developers may have overlooked.

In military affairs, compliance review, independent investigation, legislative oversight, and applicable international legal processes can help assess decisions that ordinary citizens are not equipped to evaluate directly.

These mechanisms are imperfect. They can fail, be underfunded, encounter conflicts of interest, or lack access to necessary information.

But their importance arises from a basic recognition: those exercising specialized authority should not be the sole judges of whether they have exercised it appropriately.

The absence of individual expertise does not require blind trust.

It requires appropriate methods of verification.

And where adequate verification is unavailable, the responsible response may be to acknowledge uncertainty rather than adopt an unjustified presumption of correctness.

### I. The Democratic Consequences of Disqualifying Ordinary Critics

The broader implications become especially serious in democratic political life.

Modern governments exercise powers that no ordinary citizen could independently administer.

They make decisions about national defense, public health, economic policy, infrastructure, intelligence, and countless other complex matters.

Citizens generally lack the technical knowledge necessary to personally design complete alternatives in each domain.

But democracy does not rest on the assumption that every citizen is an expert in government administration.

It rests, in part, on the principle that political authority must remain subject to public evaluation and institutional accountability.

Citizens may reasonably scrutinize government decisions, demand explanations, question official claims, support independent investigations, and advocate changes in policy.

They can do so without being qualified to personally perform the duties of every official whose decisions they question.

Imagine a political system in which criticism of a military campaign were permitted only from those capable of designing superior military operations.

Or one in which economic policies could be questioned only by individuals able to produce complete national budgets.

Or one in which public concerns about a healthcare system could be dismissed unless the critics could independently design and administer its replacement.

Such a system would dramatically narrow the scope of legitimate public participation.

Expertise would cease to function primarily as a resource informing democratic judgment.

It would become a condition controlling who was permitted to exercise judgment at all.

That would be a profound inversion of accountability.

The public delegates authority because specialized decisions must be made.

It does not follow that the public has surrendered its legitimate interest in how that authority is exercised.

Nor does the fact that public criticism can be uninformed eliminate its value.

Some criticism will be mistaken. Some will ignore relevant constraints. Some will involve unrealistic expectations or unsupported accusations.

Those weaknesses should be exposed through evidence and argument.

But the existence of poor criticism is not a justification for disqualifying criticism generally.

**A democratic society does not require citizens to possess the expertise of their government. It requires institutions capable of explaining, reviewing, and being held accountable for how governmental expertise is exercised.**

### J. The Difference Between Respecting Expertise and Surrendering Judgment

There is an important balance to maintain.

Nothing in this argument should be interpreted as an endorsement of dismissing professional knowledge or treating uninformed speculation as equivalent to expert analysis.

That would simply reproduce a different form of irrationality.

Experts often understand constraints that outsiders do not. Professional experience can reveal why apparently obvious solutions are impractical. Technical evidence may demonstrate that a decision initially appearing excessive was appropriate under the circumstances.

When such evidence is presented, it deserves serious consideration.

Indeed, intellectual humility requires recognizing when other people possess knowledge that we lack.

But humility is not the same as unquestioning deference.

I can acknowledge that a military commander possesses operational knowledge unavailable to me without concluding that every decision made by that commander was justified.

I can recognize a physician's expertise without believing that every recommendation should be accepted without explanation.

I can respect the knowledge of economists, engineers, or public officials while continuing to ask whether their claims are supported by evidence.

These positions are not contradictory.

They reflect the difference between respecting the methods through which knowledge is acquired and surrendering the responsibility to evaluate claims.

The goal is not to eliminate trust in expertise.

It is to make that trust appropriately responsive to evidence, institutional safeguards, demonstrated competence, and the possibility of error.

A credible expert should be able to acknowledge uncertainty.

A credible institution should be capable of investigating its mistakes.

And a credible defense of a consequential decision should do more than insist that its critics lack the qualifications to make it themselves.

### K. Returning to the Original Conversation

Viewed through this broader framework, my conversation about Israel's military campaign illustrates more than a single flawed response.

It reveals a general problem in how people sometimes defend powerful institutions.

The institution makes a consequential decision.

An observer raises questions about the decision's justification and effects.

The defender invokes the institution's specialized knowledge or the complexity of its responsibilities.

The observer is then challenged to demonstrate that they could have made a better decision.

When they cannot, the concern is dismissed.

Yet the relevant evidence about the institution's conduct remains unexamined.

The psychological and rhetorical attraction of this pattern is understandable.

It is much easier to demonstrate that an ordinary citizen cannot formulate a comprehensive military strategy than to demonstrate that a particular military strategy complied with every relevant legal obligation.

It is much easier to point out that someone cannot perform surgery than to explain why a proposed surgery is necessary.

It is much easier to ask a critic to solve an entire economic problem than to justify the assumptions underlying a particular policy.

But ease of argument is not evidence of correctness.

And the ability to exploit a critic's limited expertise does not relieve an institution of its responsibility to operate within the standards governing its conduct.

My original questions about Israel's military campaign concerned civilian casualties, military objectives, feasible precautions, and the possibility of less destructive alternatives.

Those questions remain legitimate regardless of whether I know how to design a complete military campaign.

They may be difficult to answer. The evidence may be incomplete. Some decisions may be defensible, while others may not be.

Determining which conclusions are warranted requires investigation.

What cannot be justified is the assumption that the investigation becomes unnecessary because an ordinary citizen cannot personally solve the underlying military problem.

And this is the larger lesson.

Whenever a consequential decision is defended by demanding that critics produce an entire replacement plan, we should ask whether that demand is actually helping to evaluate the decision—or merely moving the burden of justification away from those responsible for it.

Sometimes asking about alternatives is necessary and productive.

But when the absence of a ready-made alternative becomes an excuse to avoid examining evidence, the reasoning has ceased to serve inquiry.

It has begun to serve institutional deference.

The difference is fundamental.

**Respecting expertise means taking specialized knowledge seriously. Unquestioning deference means treating the existence of specialized authority as though it were itself sufficient justification for the decisions made.**

One encourages informed judgment.

The other can insulate judgment from examination.

And that distinction returns us to the central insight of this case study.

I do not need to know how to repair a roof to question whether it must be replaced.

I do not need to be a surgeon to ask whether an operation is necessary.

I do not need to design an entire economic system to question the consequences of a particular policy.

And I do not need to be a military strategist to ask whether a military campaign has been conducted lawfully, effectively, and with adequate regard for civilian life.

In every case, my responsibility is to ask questions carefully, make claims proportionate to the evidence, and remain willing to reconsider my conclusions.

The responsibility of those defending a decision is to engage those questions with relevant evidence and reasoning.

Neither responsibility can be discharged by demonstrating that the other person does not possess all the answers.

**The absence of an alternative is not the presence of a justification.**

And when we forget that distinction, we risk transforming expertise from a tool for understanding reality into a shield protecting authority from scrutiny.

## X. Conclusion: The Difference Between Defending Beliefs and Seeking Truth

When I reflect on the conversation that prompted this essay, what strikes me most is not that my interlocutor and I disagreed about Israel's military conduct.

Disagreement was inevitable, and potentially productive.

The conflict involves difficult questions about security, military necessity, civilian protection, international law, and moral responsibility. Reasonable inquiry into these questions requires confronting uncertainty, evaluating competing claims, and recognizing that some conclusions demand evidence beyond what ordinary participants in a political conversation may possess.

What troubled me was something more fundamental.

I had raised questions about the extraordinary scale of Palestinian deaths and whether Israel's military campaign had been conducted in a manner that adequately minimized civilian harm. I wanted to examine who was being killed, what military objectives were being pursued, what precautions were taken, and whether less destructive alternatives might have been feasible.

These questions were not offered as definitive conclusions. They were invitations to investigate.

Yet instead of examining them, my interlocutor repeatedly redirected the discussion.

War inevitably produces casualties. Hamas is worse. Iran is worse. What would I have done differently?

And finally, when I acknowledged that I could not personally formulate a comprehensive military strategy:

"Yeah, see, you don't know."

The exchange ended with the appearance of a rebuttal.

But the central questions remained unanswered.

Nothing had established that the military campaign was necessary in all its particulars. Nothing had demonstrated that feasible precautions had been consistently taken, or that less harmful alternatives had been unavailable. Nor, importantly, had my questions established the opposite conclusions.

The evidence necessary to make those judgments had largely gone unexamined.

That is what makes the exchange such a useful case study.

It illustrates how an argument can appear to reach a conclusion without actually resolving the question that prompted it.

### The Difference Between Questioning a Decision and Replacing It

The central logical failure was the assumption that I could not legitimately criticize a strategy unless I could personally design a superior one.

But questioning a decision and possessing the expertise to replace it are fundamentally different activities.

A person may recognize evidence that something warrants scrutiny without knowing precisely how the underlying problem should be solved.

They may identify risks, inconsistencies, unexplained consequences, or apparent failures in a decision-making process without possessing the knowledge required to construct a complete alternative.

Their observations might ultimately prove mistaken. Further investigation might reveal constraints that justify the decision or demonstrate that an apparently preferable alternative was infeasible.

But those possibilities are reasons to examine the evidence, not reasons to dismiss the questions.

This distinction is particularly important when the decisions under consideration involve specialized expertise.

We depend on experts and institutions because the problems facing modern societies are too complex for individuals to solve independently.

But our dependence on expertise does not require surrendering the right to evaluate how that expertise is exercised.

The inability to perform surgery does not invalidate a patient's questions about a recommended procedure.

The inability to administer a national economy does not invalidate a citizen's concerns about government policy.

And the inability to devise a comprehensive military strategy does not invalidate questions about the necessity, effectiveness, or legality of military operations.

In each case, the critic's responsibility is to support the claims they actually make, acknowledge relevant limitations, and remain responsive to contrary evidence.

It is not to demonstrate that they could personally assume responsibility for the entire decision-making process.

**The expertise required to solve a problem is not identical to the knowledge required to recognize that a problem deserves investigation.**

### When Reasoning Becomes the Defense of Belief

But this case study reveals something beyond a failure of logic.

The progression of the conversation suggests how defensive reasoning can gradually replace genuine inquiry.

A troubling observation is introduced.

Rather than examining it directly, the discussion shifts toward broader justifications, comparisons with other actors, questions of political allegiance, and eventually the critic's personal qualifications.

Each shift creates another opportunity to avoid reconsidering the position being defended.

The process need not be deliberate.

People can sincerely believe they are making persuasive arguments while evaluating information in ways that favor conclusions to which they are already committed.

Motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition, and cognitive dissonance offer possible explanations for why this occurs, particularly when political beliefs are intertwined with moral convictions or group loyalties.

But the actual motives of my interlocutor remain uncertain.

He may have been defending an identity, avoiding discomfort, expressing genuine confidence in Israel's military strategy, or simply struggling to articulate the considerations he believed were relevant.

I cannot know.

What I can examine is the reasoning itself.

And the reasoning repeatedly allowed the existing conclusion to remain intact without addressing the questions that challenged it.

This distinction between motivation and argumentative function is essential.

We do not need to diagnose another person's psychology to recognize that their response fails to answer a question.

Nor should we assume that recognizing a flaw in someone else's reasoning establishes the correctness of our own position.

That would merely reproduce the intellectual problem in another form.

### The Obligation to Apply the Same Standard to Ourselves

Perhaps the most important safeguard against defensive reasoning is the willingness to direct our critical standards inward.

If I expect someone defending Israel's military conduct to examine evidence of avoidable civilian harm, I must also be prepared to examine evidence of genuine military constraints, legitimate targets, and precautions taken.

If I criticize someone for treating an unverified assumption as fact, I must distinguish carefully between allegations, independently established findings, and conclusions that remain uncertain.

If I argue that the scale of Palestinian casualties deserves investigation, I must acknowledge that an aggregate death ratio does not independently establish whether particular military attacks violated international humanitarian law.

My moral concern about the consequences of the campaign does not exempt my claims from scrutiny.

But neither does the uncertainty surrounding particular military decisions require me to suspend all criticism until every operational detail becomes known.

We can make judgments proportionate to the available evidence while remaining open to reconsideration.

We can recognize that some conclusions are strongly supported, others disputed, and still others unresolved.

We can condemn established wrongdoing without pretending to possess complete knowledge of every relevant circumstance.

And we can acknowledge uncertainty without allowing it to become an excuse for indifference.

Intellectual humility does not require abandoning convictions.

It requires understanding the relationship between our convictions and the evidence supporting them.

**The purpose of critical thinking is not to ensure that our preferred conclusions survive examination. It is to ensure that the conclusions we accept have earned our confidence.**

### The Difference Between Winning an Argument and Understanding a Problem

This brings us to a distinction that I believe explains much of what makes political discussions so unproductive.

There is a difference between trying to win an argument and trying to understand a problem.

When the objective is victory, the other person's uncertainty becomes an opportunity.

Their inability to answer a difficult question can be exploited. Their limited expertise can be emphasized. Their arguments can be reframed into positions they never actually advanced.

The exchange becomes a contest in which the appearance of superiority matters more than the quality of the explanation.

But when the objective is understanding, uncertainty takes on a different role.

It becomes information about the limits of our knowledge.

An unanswered question becomes something worth examining rather than a weakness to exploit.

A legitimate objection becomes an opportunity to refine a position rather than a threat that must be defeated.

And discovering that one's original judgment was mistaken becomes a form of intellectual progress rather than a humiliating defeat.

This does not mean that every position deserves equal consideration or that disagreements should remain unresolved indefinitely.

Some claims are false. Some arguments are demonstrably invalid. Some conclusions are supported by overwhelming evidence.

A commitment to inquiry requires the willingness to recognize these realities.

But it also requires distinguishing between demonstrating that a claim is false and merely finding a way to dismiss the person making it.

That distinction was lost in my original exchange.

My interlocutor established that I did not possess a complete military solution.

He did not establish that the concerns I raised were invalid.

And because the latter question was never meaningfully addressed, the apparent victory was intellectually hollow.

### Why This Matters Beyond One Conversation

The consequences of this reasoning pattern extend well beyond interpersonal debate.

When critics are required to produce complete alternative solutions before their concerns can be considered, existing institutions acquire an unjustified advantage.

Their decisions can be defended without adequate explanation, while those questioning them face increasingly demanding requirements of expertise.

The result can be a system in which authority is protected not because its decisions have been demonstrated to be sound, but because outsiders are unable to independently perform the tasks entrusted to it.

This is particularly concerning when the decisions involve enormous power and potentially irreversible consequences.

Military operations, medical interventions, economic policies, and technological systems can affect people who possess little control over the decisions made on their behalf or imposed upon them.

Those affected may lack the expertise to devise alternatives.

They may lack access to relevant information.

They may be unable to demonstrate precisely what should have been done differently.

But these limitations do not eliminate the importance of their concerns.

Indeed, the concentration of expertise and authority within powerful institutions creates a greater need for meaningful oversight and independent evaluation.

That oversight may require specialists, investigative bodies, legal processes, professional standards, and other mechanisms capable of addressing questions that ordinary citizens cannot resolve independently.

The point is not that every critic is correct.

It is that criticism should be evaluated by its substance, and consequential decisions should remain accountable to appropriate standards of evidence and justification.

The alternative is to confuse institutional power with intellectual authority, and intellectual authority with infallibility.

### Returning to the Question That Was Never Answered

Ultimately, my original question remains straightforward.

Given the extraordinary scale of death and destruction in Gaza, what evidence supports the conclusion that Israel's military operations were conducted in accordance with the relevant obligations to protect civilians?

What precautions were taken?

What civilian harm was anticipated?

What alternatives were considered?

And what evidence would allow us to distinguish harm that was unavoidable under the circumstances from harm that resulted from decisions that were unlawful, inadequately justified, or unnecessarily destructive?

These questions do not require assuming that Israel had an easy or obvious solution to the security threats it faced.

They do not require denying the atrocities committed by Hamas or dismissing the legitimate security interests of Israeli civilians.

They do not require pretending that warfare can be conducted without risk, uncertainty, or suffering.

And they do not require me to claim expertise I do not possess.

They require only the recognition that military conduct, like other consequential exercises of power, should be examined through evidence and held accountable to the standards governing it.

Perhaps a serious investigation would establish that particular operations were justified under circumstances I had not fully understood.

Perhaps it would establish that some were unlawful or that feasible precautions were neglected.

Perhaps some questions would remain unresolved because the necessary evidence is unavailable.

But each of these possibilities is a reason to pursue the investigation rather than dismiss it.

And that brings us back to the fundamental distinction at the center of this essay.

There is nothing wrong with asking a critic to consider alternatives.

There is nothing wrong with challenging unsupported assumptions, identifying practical constraints, or pointing out that someone lacks information necessary to reach a confident conclusion.

Those are legitimate parts of critical discussion.

The error occurs when these challenges are used to replace the inquiry rather than improve it.

When the inability to provide a solution becomes grounds for ignoring evidence of a problem, reasoning has ceased to function as a method of investigation.

It has become a method of protecting an existing position.

And when that pattern takes hold, we may become increasingly skilled at defending our beliefs while becoming less capable of discovering whether those beliefs are justified.

The lesson I take from this conversation is therefore not that criticism should prevail over expertise, or that every troubling outcome proves wrongdoing.

It is that neither expertise nor uncertainty should be used to place consequential decisions beyond reasonable examination.

We should expect evidence from those who make claims.

We should expect accountability from those who exercise power.

We should remain willing to recognize the limits of our own understanding.

And we should resist the temptation to treat another person's inability to answer a difficult question as evidence that our own preferred answer must be correct.

Because the most important question in a serious disagreement is not always, "Can you tell me what should have been done instead?"

Sometimes it is simply, "How do we know that what was done was justified?"

And if we cannot answer that question, the responsible conclusion is not that the question should never have been asked.

It is that the investigation is not yet complete.

**The inability to provide a better solution is not evidence that the existing solution is correct. When uncertainty becomes an excuse to stop asking questions rather than a reason to investigate further, reasoning has given way to the defense of belief.**
