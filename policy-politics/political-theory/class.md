I think you're identifying a real tension that has occupied socialist thinkers for over a century. The assumption that people will naturally organize around their objective economic interests has repeatedly collided with the fact that humans don't experience politics primarily as an exercise in material self-interest.

There's an old problem here that even many Marxists recognized. Marx expected that capitalism would increasingly simplify society into antagonistic classes, making class identity more obvious. But history often seems to show the opposite: economic insecurity frequently intensifies *other* identities rather than replacing them.

A few thoughts.

First, class is abstract. Ethnicity, religion, nationality, neighborhood, and family are concrete.

You know your family. You know your church. You know your race because other people treat you as belonging to one. But "working class" is a statistical category. The warehouse worker in Fresno, the Uber driver in Chicago, and the cashier in Alabama all occupy similar structural positions, but they rarely interact. There's no daily ritual that reinforces "we are one class."

Second, humans appear evolutionarily predisposed toward coalitional thinking, but the coalition itself is flexible. We seem remarkably willing to form "us versus them" distinctions. The question is *which* distinction becomes politically salient.

Sometimes labor movements succeeded in making class the dominant identity. Sometimes nationalism did. Sometimes religion. Sometimes race. There isn't evidence that class naturally wins.

Third, economic hardship doesn't necessarily produce solidarity—it often produces competition.

If everyone is struggling for scarce jobs, housing, or status, it's psychologically easier to conclude that some visible out-group is "cutting in line" than to reason about impersonal economic systems. Conspiracy theories are attractive because they personalize structural problems. "The economy is shaped by diffuse institutional incentives" is cognitively unsatisfying compared to "someone is doing this to us."

That doesn't mean the conspiracy is true; it means it is emotionally legible.

I think your observation about people "reinterpreting class through fascist lenses" is especially important. Fascist movements have historically been quite adept at this.

Instead of saying:

> Workers are exploited by capital.

they say:

> The *real* workers are being betrayed by corrupt elites working with foreigners/minorities/globalists/etc.

They preserve the emotional appeal of populism while redirecting its target. Economic grievances remain, but the explanation becomes ethnic or conspiratorial rather than structural.

This is one reason fascism has often recruited from populations experiencing genuine economic distress. The distress is real; the interpretation differs.

Where I might push back slightly is on your conclusion that class consciousness is impossible.

History suggests it's difficult, fragile, and contingent rather than impossible.

Periods of strong labor organizing—late 19th century Europe, parts of the New Deal era in the U.S., postwar labor movements in several countries—show that people *can* develop durable class identities. But those identities required institutions:

* unions
* worker newspapers
* mutual aid societies
* political parties
* social clubs
* education
* repeated collective victories

In other words, class consciousness wasn't spontaneous. It was built.

When those institutions weaken, class identity often dissolves into other identities that are already socially reinforced.

One thing I find interesting about your perspective is that it resonates less with classical Marxism and more with thinkers like Antonio Gramsci or Ernesto Laclau, who argued that political identities have to be actively constructed. Or even with critics of Marxism like George Orwell, who repeatedly noted that patriotism, religion, and national identity often outweighed class.

I also wonder if there's a distinction between **class interest** and **class consciousness**.

People may objectively share interests—higher wages, cheaper housing, better healthcare—without interpreting those interests through the language of class. They might support similar policies while understanding them as benefiting "families," "taxpayers," "ordinary Americans," or "people who work hard."

That suggests populism may not need explicit class consciousness to achieve redistributive goals. The challenge is that without a durable class identity, those coalitions are often unstable and vulnerable to being redirected toward cultural conflict.

So I don't think your skepticism is fatalistic. It points to a harder question: if humans default to tribe, can a political movement make "working people" feel like a tribe? Historically, that has happened—but only under fairly unusual institutional and historical conditions. The evidence suggests it's possible, but neither natural nor permanent.

I think you're putting your finger on one of the central paradoxes of emancipatory politics.

If class consciousness does **not** arise spontaneously, then someone has to cultivate it. But as soon as someone is cultivating it, questions emerge:

* Who decides what the "correct" consciousness is?
* Who educates whom?
* Who distinguishes false consciousness from genuine disagreement?
* Who has the authority to speak for the class?

Those questions are difficult to answer without creating institutions that accumulate power.

This is precisely why the history of socialist thought after Marx becomes so fractured. Marx himself often wrote as though capitalism would generate its own gravediggers. When that didn't happen predictably, later thinkers tried to explain why.

Lenin's answer was essentially: workers, left to themselves, develop trade-union consciousness rather than revolutionary consciousness. Therefore a disciplined party must bring revolutionary theory to them.

That move solves one problem but creates another. If the party possesses the "correct" understanding of history, criticism of the party becomes criticism of history itself. It becomes easy for disagreement to be interpreted not as honest dissent but as ideological deviation.

You can almost see the slippery slope:

> The workers don't yet understand their interests.

becomes

> The party understands the workers' interests better than the workers do.

becomes

> Therefore opposition to the party is objectively anti-worker.

From there, dogmatism becomes structurally tempting, even if it wasn't the original goal.

Your comparison to modern right-wing movements is insightful because it points to a broader mechanism rather than something unique to Marxism.

Many ideological movements eventually fuse multiple identities into one comprehensive worldview:

* political allegiance
* moral values
* religion or quasi-religious narratives
* historical interpretation
* epistemology ("who can be trusted")
* community and belonging

Once those become fused, changing your political opinion isn't just changing your vote—it's risking your entire social identity.

That is a remarkably powerful form of social cohesion, but also one that can become resistant to self-correction.

One thing I'd add, though, is that not every attempt to construct political identity has to take the form of top-down ideological control.

Gramsci is interesting precisely because he didn't reduce hegemony to state propaganda. For him, civil society mattered enormously: schools, churches, newspapers, unions, clubs, literature, families. Culture itself generates common sense. In that view, every society is already "constructing" political identities; the question isn't whether construction happens, but who shapes it and through what institutions.

That makes the problem more subtle.

Even liberal societies often imagine themselves as neutral, but they also socialize citizens into particular norms—individual rights, markets, constitutional legitimacy, patriotism, civic participation. Those aren't natural facts; they're reproduced through education, media, law, and public rituals.

So perhaps there isn't a choice between "constructed identity" and "natural identity." The choice is between different kinds of construction.

Where your skepticism strikes me as valuable is in resisting the idea that political enlightenment can simply be administered. Once a movement believes it possesses privileged access to historical truth, it becomes increasingly difficult for it to distinguish education from indoctrination.

That tension has never really disappeared. It's visible in debates across the political spectrum whenever people ask questions like:

* Should schools cultivate particular civic values?
* Should media platforms shape public discourse?
* Should universities promote specific conceptions of justice?
* Should churches explicitly align with political projects?

These are all, in different ways, arguments about how collective identities are formed.

I also wonder if this is why you're skeptical of *class consciousness* as a political project but not necessarily of *solidarity*.

Solidarity can remain relatively modest. It says: "We share certain interests, let's cooperate where we can."

Class consciousness, especially in its stronger historical forms, often carries a much larger claim: "We constitute the true historical subject whose interests reveal the direction of history."

The latter almost invites orthodoxy. The former leaves more room for pluralism, disagreement, and revision.

In that sense, perhaps the challenge is not how to create a perfectly unified political identity, but how to sustain enough solidarity for collective action without demanding that people subsume every other aspect of themselves into a single ideological framework. That is a much less totalizing ambition, but it may also be more compatible with democratic politics.

I actually think you've drifted toward a critique that isn't simply anti-Marxist—it's a critique of **political anthropology**. You're making a claim about what kinds of identities human beings naturally privilege.

The strongest version of your argument, as I understand it, would be something like:

> Human beings do not ordinarily organize their perception of the social world through abstract structural relations. They organize it through concrete, emotionally salient group identities. Therefore any political project that assumes abstract class identity will naturally supersede those identities is working against deep features of human psychology.

That's a much deeper objection than saying "Marx was empirically wrong about a prediction."

It says the mechanism itself is suspect.

What's interesting is that there are several traditions that converge on something like this, despite arriving there from very different directions.

* Liberal pluralists became skeptical of monolithic identities because people belong simultaneously to families, religions, professions, ethnicities, local communities, and nations.
* Political realists often emphasize power and coalition over universal interests.
* Evolutionary psychology emphasizes coalition formation and kinship heuristics.
* Even some post-Marxists eventually abandoned the notion that class is the privileged political subject.

Where I think your argument becomes especially interesting is this sentence:

> people don't see the world through economic transactions and processes.

I think Marxists often oscillate between two claims.

One is descriptive:

> Capitalism structures society.

That's hard to dispute. Markets, wages, ownership, labor, and capital profoundly shape people's lives.

The second is psychological:

> Therefore people will come to understand themselves primarily through those relations.

That second claim is much weaker.

Consider something simple. Two factory workers might occupy almost identical economic positions.

One thinks:

> "I'm a worker."

The other thinks:

> "I'm Catholic."

A third thinks:

> "I'm white."

A fourth thinks:

> "I'm an American."

A fifth thinks:

> "I'm a father."

Objectively, they share class position. Subjectively, they inhabit different identities.

Marxism sometimes assumes that objective position will eventually generate subjective identity.

History suggests that translation is anything but automatic.

---

This is where I think your observation about liberalism is insightful.

Liberalism itself depends on abstractions.

Equal citizenship.

Individual rights.

The rule of law.

Neutral institutions.

Pluralism.

None of these are intuitive in the sense that kinship is intuitive.

A child understands "my family."

A child does not naturally understand "equal moral worth under impersonal constitutional procedures."

Those ideas require education, institutions, and cultural reinforcement.

So liberalism also depends on cultivating abstractions—but crucially, the abstraction is not *identity*. It is *procedure*.

That may be why liberal democracies are always somewhat unstable. They ask citizens to restrain impulses that are psychologically older than liberalism itself.

---

I also think your concern about teleology is doing a lot of work here.

Orthodox Marxism isn't merely saying:

> Capitalism creates certain incentives.

It's saying:

> History has a direction.

That direction legitimizes particular political actors.

Once history has a destination, disagreement becomes harder to tolerate because opponents are not merely mistaken—they are obstacles to historical necessity.

That's the move that worries thinkers like Karl Popper in *The Open Society and Its Enemies*. His criticism wasn't simply that Marx made false predictions. It was that grand historical narratives can become self-sealing because they reinterpret every setback as merely another stage in the inevitable process.

Whether one agrees with Popper or not, he's pointing to a structural danger: if you believe history has an objectively knowable endpoint, institutions begin to justify themselves by reference to that endpoint rather than by ongoing public accountability.

---

One place where I'd gently push back is on the inevitability of tribal capture.

You're right that movements are vulnerable to charismatic leaders and identity capture. But I don't know that this is unique to revolutionary movements.

Liberal democracies, nationalist movements, religious revivals, environmental campaigns—all exhibit the same tendency. Humans seem to repeatedly personalize institutions, rally around symbols, and divide into in-groups and out-groups.

So perhaps the problem isn't "revolutionary politics."

Perhaps it's mass politics itself.

Once politics involves millions of people, symbolic identities become indispensable because no individual can directly comprehend the complexity of the system they're participating in.

---

Reading your comments, I actually wonder if your skepticism points toward something like a *fallibilist left*. That is, a politics that accepts capitalism has structural problems, accepts the value of solidarity and collective action, but rejects any claim that there is a single historical subject or a predetermined direction of history.

Instead of saying:

> "The proletariat is the universal class destined to emancipate humanity."

it says:

> "Workers often share important material interests, but those interests compete with many other identities. Political coalitions must therefore be built provisionally, democratically, and with the expectation that they will remain contested."

That position gives up some of the certainty of orthodox Marxism. In return, it may be better equipped to explain why class solidarity is episodic, why coalitions fragment, and why no political identity—including class—should be treated as permanently privileged.

In a way, your intuition reminds me less of a rejection of Marx's analysis of capitalism than of a rejection of the confidence that any single social cleavage can permanently organize political life. That strikes me as a historically informed skepticism rather than a purely pessimistic one.

I actually think your last paragraph gets at something that is often overlooked: Marx can be read less as a provider of fixed categories and more as someone trying to describe a **dynamic system**.

If you read *Capital* almost like an engineer reads a systems model, it becomes much more compelling than if you read it as a taxonomy of society.

Marx is constantly asking questions like:

* How does capital accumulate?
* What feedback loops drive concentration?
* How do incentives shape firm behavior?
* Why do crises recur?
* How does competition transform the structure of production?

Those are systems questions. They're about flows, constraints, feedback, and emergent behavior.

The categories—bourgeoisie, proletariat, etc.—are almost secondary. In orthodox Marxism they became primary, but in *Capital* they're often tools for explaining the dynamics.

I also really like your intuition that class is better modeled as a **network or manifold** than as discrete categories.

Think about how sociologists often study class today. They don't simply ask, "Do you own capital?" They look at a whole constellation of variables:

* income
* wealth
* occupation
* education
* cultural capital
* social networks
* housing security
* geographic location
* political influence

Someone might score high on one dimension and low on another.

For example:

* A tenured professor may have immense cultural capital but modest wealth.
* A successful plumber may have substantial income and business assets but relatively little institutional prestige.
* A software engineer earning $300k has a high salary but may own very little productive capital relative to executives or major shareholders.
* A retiree may have millions in financial assets but little labor income.

Those positions aren't well captured by a binary.

Your point about "stake in capital" is especially interesting because it illustrates how twentieth-century capitalism complicated Marx's categories.

Through retirement accounts, pensions, index funds, and employee stock ownership, millions of wage earners own *some* capital. Yet the amount of control varies enormously.

Owning $80,000 in an index fund isn't remotely comparable to controlling a private equity firm or holding a controlling interest in a corporation. Economically, those are different kinds of ownership. So "capital ownership" itself has become graded rather than binary.

I also think your observation about perceived proximity is psychologically important.

> Someone making $100k feels closer to a millionaire than to someone in poverty.

This is fascinating because objectively, depending on the millionaire, they may be much closer in economic terms to someone earning $30k than to someone worth $100 million. Yet people rarely perceive economic distance linearly.

Status is relational.

People compare themselves to those immediately above or around them, not to the statistical distribution as a whole.

That reminds me less of Marx and more of work in social psychology on **reference groups**. People don't ask, "What percentile am I in?" They ask, "Who do I think I'm like?"

That subjective mapping may be politically more important than objective class position.

This connects back to your earlier skepticism about class consciousness.

If class itself is a high-dimensional network rather than a clean partition of society, then expecting a single unified "class identity" becomes even less plausible.

You don't have one proletariat.

You have overlapping clusters connected by varying degrees of shared interests.

That sounds much closer to a network than a partition.

In network terms, you might imagine communities that partially overlap rather than two disconnected components.

That framing also helps explain why coalitions are unstable. Different issues activate different edges in the network.

* Housing may unite renters across occupations.
* Healthcare may unite workers and retirees.
* Immigration may split workers.
* Education may divide professionals from manual laborers.
* Taxes may create entirely different coalitions.

There is no single partition that dominates every issue.

One thing I'd add, though, is that Marx himself was more flexible than many of his followers. He recognized groups like the petty bourgeoisie, peasants, the *lumpenproletariat*, and various intermediate strata. The stark bourgeoisie/proletariat binary became much more rigid in later orthodox interpretations than it always was in Marx's own empirical observations. Still, his framework tends toward polarization over time, whereas modern capitalist economies have often generated new intermediate positions rather than collapsing neatly into two camps.

The direction you're sketching actually reminds me of combining Marx with complexity science. Instead of asking, "Which class does this person belong to?" you'd ask questions like:

* Where are they located in a multidimensional socioeconomic space?
* Which identities are currently salient?
* What network connections shape their beliefs?
* Which feedback loops move people through that space over time?

That preserves what is powerful about Marx—the emphasis on structural dynamics and incentives—while replacing rigid categorical thinking with a model that better reflects heterogeneous, adaptive societies.

Ironically, that approach is arguably *more* faithful to Marx's method than to some later orthodox Marxisms. Marx was deeply interested in motion, contradiction, and transformation. Treating his categories as fixed ontological boxes can obscure the very dynamism that made his analysis so influential in the first place.

Initially, your argument was:

Those are distinct objections.

The first is about *identity formation*.

The second is about *collective agency*.

I actually think the second is even more damaging to orthodox notions of class consciousness.

Marxist discussions often move rather quickly from

> workers become conscious of their position

Suppose everyone in a country simultaneously understood:

* Which laws should change?

* How are disagreements resolved?

That's no longer a question of consciousness.

---

This reminds me of something from systems theory.

Awareness of a system is not equivalent to possessing a control policy.

Engineers distinguish between:

* observing a system
* modeling a system
* controlling a system

Each step is exponentially harder.

You can know a system is unstable without knowing how to stabilize it.

You can know markets generate inequality without knowing how to redesign incentive structures without unintended consequences.

So consciousness is, at best, an input into collective problem solving—not a solution.

---

I also think you're implicitly distinguishing between **diagnostic knowledge** and **operational knowledge**.

Class consciousness is largely diagnostic.

It says:

> Here is the problem.

But institutional change requires operational knowledge.

It asks:

* Which feedback loops should be modified?
* Which incentives should change?
* Which institutions should be decentralized?
* Which markets should remain?
* Which forms of ownership are desirable?
* How do we preserve innovation?
* How do we prevent bureaucratic sclerosis?

Those are questions of institutional design.

Knowing one is exploited does not answer them.

---

This also explains why revolutionary movements so often end up relying on relatively small groups of organizers, theorists, or party cadres.

Not necessarily because ordinary people are incapable, but because designing institutions is a specialized task.

Yet that specialization immediately creates another problem.

The people with operational knowledge become a new elite.

Which returns us to your earlier concern about ideological capture.

In other words:

> Consciousness → Organization → Expertise → Hierarchy

The hierarchy may be justified as temporary.

It may genuinely begin that way.

But once it exists, it develops interests of its own.

That's a classic organizational problem, not just a Marxist one.

---

I think this is also why unions are such an interesting counterexample.

They succeed because their objective is comparatively narrow.

They don't need to redesign an economy.

They need to negotiate:

* wages
* hours
* benefits
* safety
* grievance procedures

Those are bounded optimization problems.

Revolutionary transformation is an unbounded optimization problem.

The cognitive and organizational requirements are vastly different.

So unions can often succeed without requiring comprehensive theories of society.

---

There's another implication that I think is worth teasing out.

You said:

> most people don't understand economic structures.

I agree descriptively that few people possess a detailed understanding of macroeconomic systems. But I'd go one step further and say **no individual does**.

Modern economies are complex adaptive systems. Central banks, finance ministries, CEOs, labor leaders, economists, regulators—each sees only part of the whole. Knowledge is radically distributed.

That observation actually brings your thinking into conversation with thinkers who were otherwise ideological opponents. Friedrich Hayek emphasized dispersed knowledge as a critique of central planning, while Marx emphasized structural dynamics that individuals often fail to perceive. Those aren't identical claims, but they converge on one point: no single actor has complete knowledge of the system.

If that's right, then the challenge for institutional design becomes even harder. It's not just motivating collective action; it's creating institutions that can learn and adapt despite everyone having partial information.

This makes me think your critique is drifting away from debates about Marxism per se and toward a more general theory of political change.

It sounds something like this:

2. Tribal identities are psychologically more salient than structural abstractions.
3. Awareness of structural position is episodic and difficult to sustain.

Notice what's interesting about that sequence: none of those premises require denying that capitalism has structural problems or that class relations matter. They instead challenge whether *class consciousness* is the mechanism by which large-scale political transformation occurs.

I think this is where some versions of Marxism become totalizing.

---

Your concern about **obfuscation** is particularly important.

Abstractions don't just simplify—they also hide.

Suppose I classify society into two classes.

I've compressed enormous amounts of information.

Compression is useful.

But compression necessarily discards variance.

The question is whether the discarded variance matters for the problem at hand.

Sometimes it doesn't.

Sometimes it matters enormously.

If I'm studying long-run wealth concentration, a coarse class distinction may be informative.

If I'm trying to understand voting behavior, identity formation, or institutional trust, collapsing everyone into "capital" and "labor" might obscure more than it reveals.

So I think your point is less "class is false" than "class is a lossy compression."

That's a nice way to think about it.

There's also an epistemological principle lurking here that I like.

You're resisting the temptation to elevate an analytical category into an ontological one.

There's a difference between saying:

> "Class is a useful analytical construct."

and saying:

> "Class is the fundamental structure of social reality."

Those are very different claims.

Many theories quietly slide from the first to the second.

Your use of "resolution" also makes me think of an analogy from physics.

Neither description is wrong.

They answer different questions.

Perhaps class is like that.

It may describe certain macro-patterns while being insufficient for explaining micro-level cognition, coalition formation, or institutional evolution.

You aren't saying:

Once we create a category, we begin to treat it as if it were an object in the world rather than a modeling choice.

I actually think this language of **scale dependence** might help sharpen your critique. Rather than arguing that class is a weak concept in general, you could argue that its explanatory value is contingent on the spatial, temporal, and organizational scale of the phenomenon being studied. That strikes me as a stronger critique than simply saying Marx's categories are too crude. It's a critique of explanatory monism itself. Whether the lens is class, race, markets, institutions, culture, or psychology, any framework that insists it is the *primary* explanatory axis risks overfitting social reality to its own concepts. A complexity-oriented approach instead asks which abstractions preserve the information relevant to the question and which ones erase important structure.
