I would **not** call this “the end of science” in the literal sense. But your alarm is justified. The rule would not abolish universities, journals, or peer review; it would, however, **move federal science funding toward political-administrative control, make grants less stable, narrow international collaboration, and add ideological filters to award decisions**. That is a major structural threat to the postwar U.S. model of publicly funded science.

## What this document is

OMB-2026-0034-0001 is a proposed government-wide rewrite of the rules for federal financial assistance, especially **2 CFR Part 200**, the “Uniform Guidance” governing grants, cooperative agreements, cost principles, audits, subawards, and award termination. OMB says its objectives are transparency, accountability, oversight, regulatory clarification, and burden reduction. But the preamble also explicitly frames the rule as a response to what it calls “woke,” DEI, “gender ideology,” and prior-administration policy conditions. ([Federal Register][1])

This is not just about NIH or NSF. NACo summarizes the scope well: 2 CFR Part 200 governs federal grants, cooperative agreements, and pass-through awards across agencies and recipients, including states, counties, nonprofits, universities, hospitals, and Tribal governments. ([National Association of Counties][2])

Comments are due **July 13, 2026**, and OMB proposes an effective date of **October 1, 2026** for FY2027 awards. ([Federal Register][1])

## The main features that matter for science

**1. Political pre-issuance review of discretionary awards.**
Proposed §200.205 would require senior appointees to review discretionary awards before issuance to ensure consistency with law, agency priorities, and the national interest. The rule says discretionary awards must, where applicable, “demonstrably advance the President’s policy priorities.” It also says peer review may be used only if it remains advisory and is not treated as binding by senior appointees or their designees. ([Federal Register][1])

That is the core concern. Peer review was never mathematically binding, but the proposed text seems designed to prevent expert review from being treated as the real decision engine. AAS reads this as shifting authority from peer-review panels and agency subject-matter experts toward political appointees. ([American Astronomical Society][3])

**2. Program design must align with administration priorities.**
Proposed §200.202 says federal programs must have goals and objectives that are consistent with statutory purpose and “align with administration policies and priorities.” For science, that means the framing of research opportunities could be filtered through the administration’s current political program, not only statutory scientific missions. ([Federal Register][1])

**3. “Gold Standard Science” becomes an award criterion, but with political ambiguity.**
The rule tells agencies to prioritize “Gold Standard Science,” rigorous and reproducible scholarship, and institutions demonstrating commitment to research integrity. Those are good words on their face. The danger is that the term is imported from administration policy and can become a political label: research aligned with current executive preferences may be deemed “gold standard,” while disfavored fields or findings may be treated as suspect. ([Federal Register][1])

**4. Active grants could be terminated for changed priorities.**
Proposed §200.340 would allow termination of an award if the agency determines it is in the agency’s interest, including where the award no longer effectuates program goals, agency priorities, or the national interest “as they exist at the time of the termination.” The notice need only give a brief reason and need not provide a detailed analysis. ([Federal Register][1])

For science, this attacks reliance. A five-year grant supporting personnel, longitudinal data collection, clinical cohorts, field sites, instruments, or animal colonies becomes less bankable if a new political judgment can interrupt it midstream.

**5. International science gets a “domestic-first” framework.**
For R&D awards, the rule says awards must generally go to U.S.-organized entities, with foreign entities requiring statutory authorization or a senior appointee’s determination of compelling interest. It also requires agencies to apply a domestic-first framework for international elements. ([Federal Register][1])

There are exceptions, and foreign subrecipients or contractors are not categorically barred. But the default shifts from “international collaboration when scientifically justified” toward “international collaboration only when administratively justified as in the U.S. national interest.”

**6. Conferences, memberships, subscriptions, and publications are constrained.**
Conference attendance costs would be allowable only if expressly approved by the agency and included in the award terms. Publication costs, including article processing charges and open-access fees, would become unallowable unless required by statute or approved in advance case-by-case. ([Federal Register][1])

AAS argues this would weaken scientists’ ability to attend conferences, participate in professional societies, publish, and disseminate work, especially through nonprofit scientific society journals. ([American Astronomical Society][3])

## Scope beyond science

The science implications are severe, but the rule is broader. It affects the federal grant architecture for transportation, public health, broadband, disaster programs, education, county-administered funds, nonprofit pass-through awards, and more. NACo describes it as the largest revision to Uniform Guidance since 2013 and lists changes including expanded termination authority, senior agency review, new national policy requirements, restrictions on foreign collaborations, E-Verify requirements, and reclassification of the guidance into binding regulation. ([National Association of Counties][2])

Benton’s broadband-focused analysis makes the same structural point: the proposal rewrites the rules for how nearly every federal grant dollar is awarded, conditioned, and potentially terminated, and embeds current administration priorities into award terms. ([Benton Foundation][4])

## Ramifications for American science

The most likely effects are not an immediate collapse, but **chilling, filtering, and instability**.

First, investigators and institutions will self-censor. If funding decisions must align with presidential priorities and avoid vague categories like “anti-American values,” researchers may avoid politically sensitive questions in climate science, public health, race, gender, migration, environmental justice, reproductive health, health disparities, misinformation, and politically salient social science.

Second, peer review loses status. The proposal preserves peer review formally but subordinates it practically. That is different from ordinary programmatic discretion. It creates a mandatory political checkpoint.

Third, long-horizon research becomes riskier. Longitudinal studies, basic science, population studies, biodiversity monitoring, clinical networks, and major instrumentation all depend on continuity. The termination language makes continuity less secure.

Fourth, U.S. science becomes less international. The “domestic-first” framework does not ban global science, but it adds friction, scrutiny, and political approval to collaborations that are often essential because data, pathogens, ecosystems, telescopes, populations, or field sites are not located in the United States.

Fifth, dissemination is treated as peripheral rather than integral. The publication-cost provision is especially strange for science: publication is not reputational fluff; it is the mechanism by which federally funded knowledge becomes public, reviewable, and reusable.

## Is the fascism / Soviet analogy fair?

As a warning about **politicization**, yes, the analogy has force. Fascist and Soviet systems did not usually end science by closing every laboratory. They damaged science by subordinating truth-seeking to state ideology, rewarding politically acceptable conclusions, punishing disfavored fields, and making expert institutions answer to party priorities.

The analogy should be used carefully, though. This proposed rule is still a U.S. notice-and-comment regulation; it is subject to public comment, administrative-law constraints, statutory limits, appropriations limits, judicial review, and institutional resistance. It is not equivalent to Lysenkoism or Nazi racial science in its current legal form.

But the **direction of travel** is the part that should worry you: political appointees review awards; peer review is explicitly advisory; “national interest” and presidential priorities become funding filters; active awards can be ended when priorities change; certain lines of inquiry are ideologically marked. Those are classic ingredients of politicized science.

## My bottom line

This is best read as a proposed transformation of federal grants from a relatively expert-mediated, statute-and-mission-based system into a more centralized, presidential-priority-driven system. For science, that means **less independence, less stability, less internationalism, and less confidence that expert merit review will control funding outcomes**.

So I would not say “the end of science.” I would say: **if finalized substantially as written, it would be one of the most consequential moves in decades toward political control of federally funded science in the United States.**

# Misinformation Research is Targeted

## Named researchers especially emblematic of the risk

**Kate Starbird** is the clearest example you raised. Her work covers crisis informatics, rumors, misinformation, disinformation, and online information ecosystems; UW says the Center for an Informed Public works to strengthen democratic discourse against online misinformation, propaganda, and manipulation. ([Information School][7])

**Renée DiResta** is another emblematic case because her Stanford Internet Observatory work on online manipulation and election-related misinformation already became a political flashpoint. Reporting in 2024 described Stanford’s disinformation work as being put on hold after political and legal pressure, with DiResta and the Election Integrity Partnership accused by critics of censorship, allegations she denied. ([Le Monde.fr][9])

**Philip Howard and Samantha Bradshaw** are central to computational propaganda research. Oxford’s project focused on how bots, algorithms, and automation are used by political actors, and Bradshaw’s work specifically examines government use of social media for coordinated digital disinformation campaigns. ([Oxford Internet Institute][2])

**Joshua Tucker, Jonathan Nagler, and Zeve Sanderson** at NYU’s Center for Social Media and Politics / Center for Social Media, AI, and Politics are also in scope. NYU describes Sanderson’s work as using computational and experimental methods to study the digital information environment, and Tucker as co-director of a center focused on social media, AI, and politics. ([NYU Social Media & Politics Center][10])

**Joan Donovan and Yochai Benkler** represent the media-manipulation / networked-public-sphere side of the field. Harvard’s Berkman Klein page links Donovan and Benkler to work on the causes and effects of disinformation. ([Berkman Klein Center][11])

**John Cook and Stephan Lewandowsky** represent climate misinformation research. Their work is politically exposed because it directly studies climate denial, science denial, and misinformation countermeasures. ([Center for Climate Change Communication][5])

## The adjacent fields I would watch closely

The most vulnerable adjacent fields are not only “misinformation studies” by name. They include:

**Political communication and polarization research**, especially studies of asymmetric polarization, partisan media ecosystems, election denial, political violence, and democratic backsliding.

**Public-health communication**, especially vaccine misinformation, reproductive-health misinformation, gender-affirming care misinformation, pandemic response, and trust in agencies like CDC, FDA, and NIH.

**Climate communication**, especially research on fossil-fuel disinformation, climate denial, climate delay narratives, and environmental justice communication.

**AI safety and AI governance**, especially research on deepfakes, synthetic persuasion, bot networks, automated influence campaigns, and platform accountability.

**Extremism and terrorism studies**, especially domestic extremism, white nationalism, militia mobilization, anti-government movements, and online radicalization.

**Science communication**, especially research on public trust in science, expert credibility, conspiracy beliefs, and attacks on scientific institutions.

**Sociology of knowledge / science and technology studies**, especially studies of how institutions manufacture doubt, delegitimize expertise, or politicize evidence.

## How this could play out in practice

The danger is less likely to be a formal announcement saying “misinformation research is banned.” It is more likely to happen through softer mechanisms:

Grant programs might be rewritten so that “misinformation,” “disinformation,” “election integrity,” “platform accountability,” “DEI,” “climate justice,” or “online harms” are avoided. Review panels may still rank proposals highly, but political appointees could override them. Agencies may decline renewals, reject subawards, or terminate projects as inconsistent with current priorities. Universities may avoid submitting proposals that could attract scrutiny. Junior researchers may leave the field because the funding and harassment risks are too high.

That is exactly why your Kate Starbird example is apt: the issue is not only whether one scholar gets a grant. It is whether a whole research ecosystem becomes too politically dangerous to sustain.
