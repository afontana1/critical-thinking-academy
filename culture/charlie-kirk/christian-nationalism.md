# Stephen Wolfe

Stephen Wolfe is an American political theorist best known for his 2022 book *The Case for Christian Nationalism*. He argues for a version of Christian nationalism rooted in Reformed Protestant political theology, natural law, and a strong conception of the nation as a cultural and historical community. His work has been influential in some conservative Christian circles and sharply criticized by many other Christians, political theorists, and legal scholars. ([Wikipedia][1])

Here are the central elements of Wolfe's views:

### 1. A nation should explicitly be Christian

Wolfe argues that the ideal political order is not a religiously neutral liberal state. Instead, he believes a nation should consciously organize its public life around Christianity.

His definition of Christian nationalism is essentially:

> a Christian people governing themselves as a Christian people for both their earthly welfare and their spiritual good.

This does **not** mean, in his formulation, that the church directly rules the state. Rather, civil government should actively promote Christianity as the true religion and shape laws and customs accordingly. ([Wikipedia][1])

### 2. Government should favor Christianity

Wolfe argues that government has legitimate responsibilities beyond protecting life and property.

He believes civil authorities should:

* encourage Christian morality,
* promote Christian institutions,
* recognize Christianity as the nation's religion,
* discourage public promotion of false religions.

He generally distinguishes between:

* **internal belief**, which he says cannot legitimately be coerced, and
* **public religious practice**, which he argues governments may regulate for the common good.

This distinction comes from older Reformed political theology rather than modern liberal ideas about religious neutrality. ([Wikipedia][1])

### 3. Nations are natural communities

A major theme of Wolfe's work is that nations are not merely political arrangements.

He argues that people naturally develop:

* common language,
* customs,
* history,
* traditions,
* loyalties.

According to Wolfe, governments should preserve these national characteristics rather than treat all cultural identities as interchangeable.

He sees nationalism—understood as acting for the good of one's own nation—as a natural extension of human social life rather than an inherently dangerous ideology. ([Wikipedia][1])

### 4. Cultural homogeneity is generally desirable

One of the book's most controversial arguments is that greater cultural and ethnic cohesion generally helps political stability and the common good.

Wolfe argues that nations function best when they possess substantial cultural unity and that large-scale cultural fragmentation creates conflict.

Critics argue that this reasoning can justify exclusionary or ethnonationalist politics. Wolfe has rejected being labeled a white nationalist, but his emphasis on cultural homogeneity has remained one of the most disputed aspects of his work. ([Wikipedia][1])

### 5. Liberal neutrality is impossible

Wolfe rejects the classical liberal idea that governments can remain neutral regarding religion.

He argues every political order promotes some moral vision.

Therefore, he says:

* secular liberalism effectively promotes secular values,
* Christians should instead seek governments that openly promote Christian values.

This is one of his central philosophical arguments against modern liberal constitutionalism. ([SoBrief][2])

### 6. He draws on older Protestant political thought

Rather than presenting his ideas as novel, Wolfe argues he is recovering traditions from:

* the Protestant Reformation,
* early modern Reformed theologians,
* natural law philosophy.

He frequently cites figures such as Francis Turretin and other Reformed scholastics to argue that Protestantism historically accepted a larger role for civil government in supporting Christianity than many modern Protestants do. ([Wikipedia][1])

## Major criticisms

Wolfe's work has generated substantial criticism from both secular scholars and fellow Christians.

Some common criticisms include:

* **Religious freedom:** Critics argue that government preference for Christianity would undermine equal treatment of religious minorities.
* **Pluralism:** Many contend his model is incompatible with religiously diverse modern democracies.
* **Ethnicity and nationalism:** His positive discussion of cultural homogeneity has been criticized as lending support to exclusionary or ethnonationalist ideas.
* **Constitutional concerns:** Critics argue his vision conflicts with modern understandings of the U.S. Constitution, particularly the First Amendment.
* **Theological objections:** Many Protestant and Catholic theologians argue that Christianity should persuade through the church rather than through state power, and that Wolfe's account overstates the state's proper role. ([The Gospel Coalition][3])

## How Wolfe differs from some other Christian nationalists

Not everyone labeled a "Christian nationalist" agrees with Wolfe. His version is relatively systematic and academic.

Compared with some popular political movements, Wolfe is more likely to:

* ground his arguments in historical Protestant political theory,
* emphasize natural law,
* distinguish between coercing belief (which he opposes) and regulating public religion (which he supports).

At the same time, he is more willing than many American conservatives to question the liberal ideal of a religiously neutral state and to argue that the state should explicitly privilege Christianity. ([Wikipedia][1])

Stephen Wolfe's views on blasphemy laws and related questions flow from his broader premise that civil government should actively support Christianity rather than remain religiously neutral. While he generally does **not** advocate for rule by clergy or a direct theocracy, his proposals envision a state with significantly more authority over religion than exists in modern liberal democracies.

Here's a breakdown:

### Blasphemy laws

Wolfe has argued that a Christian commonwealth may legitimately prohibit **public blasphemy**. His reasoning is that:

* Civil government has a duty to preserve the moral and religious order of society.
* Public attacks on Christianity can undermine the common good by eroding the nation's shared religious foundations.
* Blasphemy differs from private disbelief because it is a public act with social consequences.

He generally distinguishes between:

* **Private unbelief**, which he argues cannot be coerced by the state.
* **Public blasphemy or profanation**, which he believes may be regulated or punished.

He draws this distinction from early modern Reformed political thought, where magistrates were often understood to have a role in protecting both "tables" of the Ten Commandments—that is, duties to God as well as duties to one's neighbor.

### Heresy

Wolfe is more cautious regarding **heresy** than **blasphemy**.

Historically, some Protestant states punished theological error. Wolfe's work discusses those traditions but generally does not argue for criminalizing every doctrinal disagreement.

Instead, he focuses on:

* protecting orthodox Christianity as the public religion,
* allowing churches to discipline doctrinal error internally,
* permitting civil authorities to intervene when religious teaching becomes socially disruptive.

Exactly where he would draw the line between protected dissent and punishable public error is not always specified in detail.

### Establishment of Christianity

One of his central proposals is that a Christian nation should formally recognize Christianity.

In practice, this could include:

* official recognition of Christianity,
* government support for Christian institutions,
* Christian symbols and ceremonies in public life,
* laws reflecting Christian moral teaching,
* preferential treatment for Christianity over other religions.

He argues that this is different from having clergy govern the state; instead, civil rulers remain distinct from the church while acknowledging Christianity as true.

### Religious minorities

Wolfe argues that non-Christians should generally be allowed to live peacefully within a Christian nation.

However, he does not advocate equal treatment of all religions in the public sphere. Rather, he argues that:

* Christians should receive certain public privileges.
* Other religions may be tolerated without being promoted.
* Governments may restrict public activities that conflict with the nation's Christian identity.

This departs from contemporary liberal principles of religious neutrality and equal public status.

### Sabbath laws

Wolfe has defended the legitimacy of so-called **blue laws** or Sabbath laws.

He argues that governments may require a common day of rest and may structure public life around Christian observance of Sunday, seeing this as beneficial for both religion and society.

### Public morality

More broadly, Wolfe believes governments should legislate morality—not only to prevent direct harm but to cultivate virtue.

This could justify laws concerning:

* marriage,
* family life,
* sexual ethics,
* obscenity,
* public indecency,
* education.

He rejects the liberal view that government should intervene only to prevent harm to others.

### Is this "theocracy"?

It depends on how the term is defined.

By **theocracy**, many people mean rule by religious officials (such as clergy directly governing the state). Wolfe explicitly rejects that model, arguing for distinct roles for church and civil government.

By a broader definition—a political system in which the state officially privileges one religion and enforces aspects of its moral or religious order—his vision shares important features with what many scholars would call a confessional or religious state. It includes an established Christian identity, state support for Christianity, and legal authority over some religious matters.

### Critiques

Supporters see Wolfe as reviving a historically common Protestant understanding of civil government before the rise of modern liberalism.

Critics, including many conservative Christians, argue that:

* blasphemy laws infringe freedom of conscience and expression,
* state enforcement of religious orthodoxy tends to corrupt both church and state,
* privileging one religion is incompatible with equal citizenship in a pluralistic democracy,
* his proposals are difficult to reconcile with the constitutional order of the United States.

In short, Wolfe's political theology is best understood as advocating a **confessional Christian state**: one that officially recognizes Christianity, promotes it through law and public institutions, and permits legal restrictions on at least some forms of public irreligion, while stopping short of clerical rule.

# Dale Patridge

You mean **Dale Partridge**, the Reformed pastor and online commentator. His Christian nationalism is less philosophically systematic than Stephen Wolfe’s, but it is often more bluntly political and socially hierarchical.

## Core Christian-nationalist outlook

Partridge rejects the idea that government can be religiously neutral. He argues that every political order reflects a religion or ultimate worldview, and that Christianity historically produced the best civilizations. He therefore favors a nation whose laws, institutions, and public morality openly recognize Christian truth. ([Relearn.org][1])

In his public material, this tends to mean:

* Christians should seek political and cultural dominance rather than accept permanent pluralism.
* Government should affirm and materially support Christianity.
* Biblical norms should govern not only churches and families, but public questions involving education, sexuality, gender, and national identity.
* Secular liberalism is treated not as a neutral framework but as a rival, false religion.

In June 2026, for example, he argued that government should support Christianity by supplying churches with land or construction funding. That goes beyond merely permitting religion; it implies state preference for, and financial sponsorship of, Christianity. ([People For][2])

## Women’s suffrage and “household voting”

Probably his clearest authoritarian position is his opposition to independent women’s suffrage.

Partridge has called the Nineteenth Amendment a “moral and political tragedy,” advocated returning to a household-voting model and argued that women are generally too emotional or susceptible to misplaced empathy to vote responsibly. He has also said wives should follow their husbands’ political direction. ([Instagram][3])

This is regarded as authoritarian because it would:

* remove an existing civil right from roughly half the adult population;
* assign political authority according to sex and family position;
* subordinate the individual citizenship of wives to male household heads;
* replace one-person-one-vote with a patriarchal corporate model of representation.

Partridge presents this as protective and orderly rather than hostile to women. Critics respond that benevolent language does not alter the coercive result: women would lose equal political agency.

## Patriarchy as a social order

Partridge does not merely endorse conventional evangelical “complementarianism,” where men and women have different roles within marriage or church. He explicitly promotes **biblical patriarchy**, a more comprehensive model in which male authority structures the household, church, and often society more broadly. His ministry contrasts patriarchy with softer complementarian approaches and promotes male headship, female submission, and sharply differentiated gender roles. ([Relearn.org][4])

His rhetoric portrays masculinity as naturally fitted for leadership, command, protection, and public responsibility, while femininity is associated more with following, nurturing, and emotional sensitivity. In a 2026 interview, he defended dominance-oriented masculinity as scripturally ordained and answered a challenge about threatening other people’s way of life by saying, “Because my way’s right,” grounding that certainty in Scripture. ([KJZZ][5])

The authoritarian concern is not simply that his family is organized traditionally. It is that he seeks to convert this gender hierarchy into a rule for political membership and public authority.

## Sexuality and enforcement of Christian morality

Partridge condemns same-sex relationships and transgender identity as rebellion against God rather than alternative private lifestyles. His ministry describes homosexuality in terms such as “sexual perversion” and treats contemporary gender ideology as something Christians must actively defeat rather than merely decline to practice. ([Relearn.org][1])

The authoritarian implications depend on what legal mechanisms he ultimately endorses. His general framework clearly rejects equal moral legitimacy for LGBT identities and favors a government ordered around biblical sexual ethics. However, I would distinguish that from claiming—without a specific statement—that he personally advocates every historical punishment associated with theonomy, such as execution for homosexual acts. The available material establishes his support for Christian legal and cultural supremacy more clearly than it establishes a detailed criminal code.

## State establishment without clerical rule

Like Wolfe, Partridge would probably reject the label **theocracy** when it means pastors directly running the government. His preferred model appears closer to a **confessional Christian state**:

* civil rulers remain civil rulers;
* churches remain formally distinct institutions;
* Christianity receives official recognition and state support;
* law is expected to conform to Christian moral standards;
* non-Christian worldviews are tolerated, if at all, from a subordinate position.

That distinction is real, but it does not eliminate authoritarian features. A state can avoid literal clerical rule while still restricting equal citizenship, religious neutrality, expression, sexuality, or political participation.

## Race, ethnicity, and interracial marriage

Partridge says interracial marriage is not sinful and notes that his own marriage is interracial. Nevertheless, he has argued that racial and cultural differences are providentially meaningful and that interracial marriages create additional difficulties, making them a matter requiring special prudence. ([Relearn.org][6])

Critics regard this position as legitimizing racial essentialism—treating racial categories as carrying stable moral or social significance—despite his rejection of an outright ban. His position is therefore better described as **racially differentialist** than as an explicit prohibition on interracial marriage.

It is important not to collapse those claims: saying he has voiced controversial racial-essentialist arguments is supported; simply calling him an advocate of legally enforced racial segregation would require stronger evidence.

## Education and control of cultural institutions

Partridge’s worldview is “totalizing” in the technical sense that he believes Christianity speaks authoritatively to every sphere of life, including government, education, family structure, nationalism, modesty, and economics. His writing explicitly rejects Christianity as merely private spirituality. ([Relearn.org][7])

Accordingly, he strongly favors Christian formation of children and opposes secular public education’s authority to define morality, sexuality, or identity. This overlaps with Christian nationalism because control of education is treated as necessary to rebuilding a Christian social order, not simply as an individual parental preference.

## Why critics call the program authoritarian

The label rests most strongly on five features:

1. **Political rights are unequal:** women would lose independent voting rights.
2. **Religious truth determines public legitimacy:** Christianity should receive government preference and support.
3. **Hierarchy precedes individual autonomy:** fathers, husbands, pastors, and male household heads possess institutionally superior authority.
4. **Pluralism is viewed as error, not a permanent political condition:** opposing ways of life are not merely disagreed with but treated as forces to be defeated or subordinated.
5. **Biblical certainty limits democratic negotiation:** when a political conclusion is presented as directly commanded by God, dissent becomes disobedience rather than an equally legitimate civic position.

Partridge would dispute the characterization. He would likely say liberal secularism is itself coercive, that hierarchy is unavoidable, and that biblical authority produces genuine freedom and order. The disagreement is therefore partly definitional: **he defines freedom as living under God’s proper order; liberal critics define it substantially in terms of equal citizenship, conscience, and individual political rights.**

Compared with Stephen Wolfe, Partridge is especially notable for turning patriarchal theology into a direct proposal to disenfranchise women. Wolfe offers a fuller theory of Christian magistracy and nationhood; Partridge’s distinctive emphasis is the Christian household, male rule, anti-feminism, and openly unequal political authority.

# Joel Webbon

Joel Webbon is a Texas Reformed Baptist pastor and media figure who **openly identifies as a Christian nationalist**. Compared with Stephen Wolfe, Webbon is less of a systematic political philosopher and more of a polemicist and movement organizer. Compared with Dale Partridge, he expresses a broader and often harsher vision of political coercion.

Webbon says that a statement he helped edit “fully encompasses” his Christian-nationalist views. That document defines civil rulers as authorities placed “over the people” under Christ, calls for national recognition of Christian orthodoxy and proposes the Ten Commandments as the nation’s foundational law. ([Right Response Ministries][1])

## His governing ideal

Webbon rejects religiously neutral liberal democracy. His preferred order is a **confessional Christian nation** in which:

* the state formally acknowledges Christ’s authority;
* Scripture supplies the ultimate standard of public justice;
* magistrates promote what Christianity defines as good and punish what it defines as evil;
* churches instruct rulers concerning their religious and political duties;
* citizens are expected to submit to civil authorities as authorities under Christ.

The associated statement explicitly says that making nations Christian includes calling civil authorities to obedience and that the church should instruct them regarding their duties. ([CN & the Gospel][2])

He would probably reject “theocracy” when it means pastors personally occupying every government office. But under the broader meaning—a government whose legitimacy and laws are grounded in an officially preferred religion—his position is plainly theocratic or **theonomic-adjacent**.

## Blasphemy and religious liberty

The statement associated with Webbon leaves signatories a choice between legislating all Ten Commandments or only their “second table,” concerning duties toward other people. It nevertheless expressly defends the legitimacy of laws against **public blasphemy**, asserting that such laws do not coerce private conversion or violate private religious liberty. ([CN & the Gospel][2])

That distinction is central to his worldview:

* A person might retain some freedom of inward belief.
* Public religious expression could be restricted when it contradicts the state’s Christian identity.
* “Religious liberty” would therefore not mean equal public standing for Christianity, Judaism, Islam, atheism and other systems.

This resembles older Protestant establishments more than modern First Amendment pluralism.

## Women and voting

One of Webbon’s best-documented anti-democratic positions is his opposition to women having an independent political vote. He has suggested that the Nineteenth Amendment was a mistake and endorsed a household model in which a wife’s political representation is mediated through her husband. Reporting on him describes his wife as voting according to his direction. ([People For][3])

This follows from what he calls **biblical patriarchy**, rather than merely a limited doctrine of male clergy. His ministry explicitly distinguishes patriarchy from softer evangelical complementarianism and treats husbands as possessing meaningful authority over wives. On his own “controversies” page, he defends having instructed his wife not to read a particular book and promotes patriarchy as the proper label for his position. ([Right Response Ministries][1])

Critics call this authoritarian because it would convert family hierarchy into civil inequality: married women would cease to possess political agency independent of male household heads.

## Democracy and elite rule

Webbon does not treat universal democratic participation as inherently just. His thought puts greater weight on:

* rule by qualified Christian men;
* household and patriarchal representation;
* religious fitness for leadership;
* obedience and hierarchy;
* substantive Christian outcomes rather than procedurally neutral elections.

His outlook is therefore not merely “socially conservative democracy.” It permits restricting the electorate or eligibility for office to produce a Christian political order. Right Wing Watch reports that he has opposed both women’s suffrage and Jews holding public office, although the latter claim should be understood through that organization’s openly adversarial monitoring and ideally checked against the full original recording. ([People For][3])

## Punishment and biblical law

Webbon is associated with a movement that rejects the modern liberal assumption that consensual private conduct should normally fall outside criminal law. His framework potentially allows the state to punish offenses including:

* public blasphemy;
* abortion;
* pornography and public obscenity;
* same-sex sexual conduct or its public promotion;
* adultery and certain sexual offenses;
* false accusations and perjury.

The critical distinction is between **what his general principles permit** and **which exact penalties he currently advocates as policy**. Online summaries frequently attribute proposed executions for adultery or false sexual-assault allegations to him. Some of those reports appear to derive from genuine recorded statements, but individual claims should be verified from complete recordings because short activist clips can omit qualifications or hypothetical framing.

The larger point is well established: Webbon does not accept the liberal distinction between “sin” and “crime” as fixed. He believes at least some offenses against God or biblical morality can legitimately become civil crimes.

## Race, nation and multiculturalism

Webbon’s nationalism has also acquired an explicitly racial dimension. In a 2025 recording, he told white parents that teaching children that racial groups are essentially alike was a failure of parental duty and characterized a hypothetical Black crowd as dramatically more dangerous than a white one. The *Houston Chronicle* reported the comments and quoted a Rice University scholar calling the claims inflammatory and unsupported. ([Houston Chronicle][4])

His ministry has also promoted material framing multiculturalism as a social problem, including a discussion with Stephen Wolfe titled “The Plague of Multiculturalism.” ([Right Response Ministries][5])

That places him beyond civic nationalism—the belief that anyone can belong through shared citizenship—and closer to a nationalism emphasizing inherited culture, ancestry, demographic continuity and group differences. Whether “white nationalist” is the most precise label depends on how narrowly it is defined, but the racial hierarchy and separationist implications of some of his rhetoric are difficult to dismiss as merely theological conservatism.

## Why “authoritarian” fits more strongly here

The authoritarian characterization is based not simply on his being devout or socially conservative, but on the combination of:

1. **Unequal citizenship:** women and possibly non-Christians would not possess equal political authority.
2. **State-enforced religious truth:** government would recognize Christianity and potentially punish public violations of its first principles.
3. **Patriarchal rule:** authority flows through husbands, fathers, pastors and qualified male magistrates.
4. **Restricted pluralism:** competing religions and sexual or moral systems would be tolerated only within boundaries set by the Christian state.
5. **Broad coercive punishment:** biblical morality supplies grounds for criminalization extending beyond preventing direct physical harm.
6. **Suspicion of democracy:** political legitimacy comes ultimately from conformity to God’s law, not equal participation or popular consent.

Webbon would answer that **liberalism is itself authoritarian**: it uses government, education and cultural institutions to impose secular assumptions. He sees Christian rule not as tyranny but as the restoration of legitimate moral authority.

The core disagreement is therefore about what counts as freedom. Liberal constitutionalism begins with equal citizenship, freedom of conscience and limits on government. Webbon begins with divine authority, hierarchical social roles and the obligation of rulers to enforce an objectively Christian conception of the good.

# Seth Gruber

Seth Gruber is primarily an **anti-abortion activist, speaker, filmmaker, and founder of the White Rose Resistance**, rather than a fully developed political theorist like Stephen Wolfe or an explicitly patriarchal pastor-politician like Joel Webbon. His politics nevertheless overlap substantially with Christian nationalism: he presents America’s conflicts over abortion, sexuality, gender, education, and government as parts of a civilizational struggle between Christianity and a hostile secular worldview. ([White Rose Resistance][1])

## Abortion as homicide and “child sacrifice”

Abortion is the organizing center of Gruber’s worldview. He argues that an unborn child is a human person from fertilization and that elective abortion is therefore not merely an undesirable medical procedure but **unjustifiable homicide**. He routinely describes it as “child sacrifice,” “genocide,” and the central evil of contemporary American society. ([Mustang News][2])

This framing leads him beyond the mainstream incremental pro-life position. Gruber has said that elective abortion should ultimately be treated under law like other forms of unjustifiable homicide. More recently, he has explicitly defended the possibility of prosecuting women who knowingly obtain abortions, arguing that loving women is compatible with holding them criminally accountable. ([Facebook][3])

That does **not necessarily mean** he believes every woman involved should automatically receive a murder conviction or the same sentence. Questions of intent, coercion, knowledge, age and evidentiary standards would still matter in any criminal system. But his governing principle is clear: he rejects laws that categorically immunize the pregnant woman while punishing only the provider.

This is the strongest basis for describing his politics as coercive or authoritarian. He is advocating the use of criminal law against conduct that a large portion of the population regards as a protected medical and reproductive decision.

## Capital punishment

Gruber does not accept the “consistent life ethic” argument that opposition to abortion should require opposition to capital punishment. He regards abortion as the killing of an innocent person, while regarding execution as potentially legitimate punishment of someone guilty of a grave crime. He has consequently defended the claim that the death penalty can be compatible with being pro-life. ([Instagram][4])

Applied to abortion, this creates an obvious question: if abortion is legally classified as murder, could it theoretically qualify for capital punishment? His general principles leave that possibility open in jurisdictions that retain the death penalty, although a specific statement that he demands executions of women who have abortions would require clearer evidence than the material reviewed here.

## Christian nationalism and the “Christian nation”

Gruber’s Christian nationalism is principally **civilizational and historical**, rather than a detailed blueprint for magistrates, establishments or blasphemy codes.

He argues that America’s political heritage is fundamentally Christian and challenges accounts that portray the country as principally the product of secular Enlightenment thought. His media also treats “Christian nationalism” as a position unfairly demonized by liberal institutions and parts of the evangelical church. ([Instagram][5])

His recurring political assumptions include:

* Christianity should shape public law, not remain confined to private devotion.
* Churches should directly confront political evils.
* Government should recognize the unborn as persons and legislate accordingly.
* America’s survival depends upon recovering Christian moral foundations.
* Political disputes over abortion, sex and gender are ultimately theological disputes.

He therefore rejects strict religious neutrality. However, I have not found a comparably detailed Gruber program for an established church, religious tests for office, blasphemy prosecutions or the disenfranchisement of non-Christians. It would be an overstatement to attribute all of Wolfe’s or Webbon’s proposals to Gruber merely because they share the Christian-nationalist label.

## The church must become politically militant

Gruber strongly condemns pastors who avoid abortion and related political controversies. The White Rose Resistance is intended to mobilize Christians, churches and activists rather than function only as an educational pro-life organization. Its stated mission is to expose abortion and organize opposition until legal protection extends to every unborn person. ([White Rose Resistance][6])

The name invokes the German White Rose students who resisted Nazism. Gruber uses that analogy to argue that American Christians face a moral test comparable in structure—though not necessarily identical in scale—to Christians living amid earlier atrocities: silence makes churches complicit.

Critics object that this analogy casts political opponents not simply as mistaken citizens but as collaborators in mass killing. That can make democratic compromise appear morally equivalent to compromise with genocide. For Gruber, however, this is precisely the point: because he believes abortion actually kills millions of persons, ordinary moderation appears to him like moral cowardice.

## Planned Parenthood, Margaret Sanger and *The 1916 Project*

Gruber’s book and film *The 1916 Project* portray Margaret Sanger, Planned Parenthood, eugenics, the sexual revolution, abortion and contemporary gender politics as interconnected parts of a long-running “culture of death.” He argues that Sanger’s birth-control activism helped establish institutions and ideas that devalue unwanted, disabled and socially inconvenient human beings. ([youtube.com][7])

There is a genuine historical basis for discussing Sanger’s involvement with the early twentieth-century eugenics movement. The controversy concerns how Gruber connects that history to later developments. Critics accuse *The 1916 Project* of exaggerating continuity between Sanger, Nazism, modern Planned Parenthood, abortion rights and transgender politics, and of presenting a complex history as a unified ideological conspiracy. ([Colorado Times Recorder][8])

Gruber’s interpretation is that these phenomena share deeper assumptions: human beings may remake themselves, sexual autonomy outranks created moral order, and unwanted life may be eliminated. His critics respond that grouping contraception, abortion, transgender identity, eugenics and Nazi extermination together obscures major moral and historical distinctions.

## Sexuality, gender and “Gnosticism”

Gruber regards the sexual revolution, same-sex relationships, transgender identity and abortion as connected rejections of the created order. He often uses **Gnosticism** as an umbrella explanation: modern people allegedly treat the body as raw material subordinate to subjective identity and personal desire. ([Calvary Tucson][9])

Under this analysis:

* biological sex is created and morally meaningful;
* gender identity cannot override the sexed body;
* sexual freedom detached from marriage produces abortion;
* abortion becomes necessary to preserve consequence-free sexual autonomy;
* transgender medicine represents a further attempt to subordinate the body to the will.

This places him firmly against legal and cultural recognition of transgender identities and same-sex sexual relationships. His rhetoric is frequently apocalyptic—he presents these issues as manifestations of a broad attack on humanity rather than as discrete policy disagreements.

## Feminism and women

Gruber is strongly anti-feminist and increasingly frames feminism as an important cause of family breakdown, sexual liberation and abortion. His show has questioned whether even first-wave feminism benefited women. ([youtube.com][10])

Still, there is an important distinction between Gruber and figures such as Webbon or Dale Partridge. Based on the material reviewed, Gruber’s opposition to feminism does **not by itself establish** that he favors repealing women’s suffrage, household voting or excluding women from public office. Those positions should not be attributed to him without direct evidence.

His better-established position is that feminism encouraged women to treat motherhood, pregnancy and traditional family responsibilities as constraints on individual fulfillment. Critics regard this as romanticizing restrictive gender roles and minimizing the reasons women sought legal, economic and political independence.

## Population and political conflict

Gruber sometimes speaks of politics in demographic and generational terms, suggesting that socially conservative Christians can ultimately prevail through larger families while progressive culture diminishes itself through abortion, sterilization, delayed childbearing or gender transition.

That rhetoric reflects a broader pronatalist Christian-right outlook. It is controversial because it treats children partly as participants in a long-term ideological contest and depicts political opponents as a self-extinguishing population rather than fellow citizens to be persuaded.

## How authoritarian is he?

Calling Gruber an authoritarian requires more qualification than it does with Webbon.

**The authoritarian case is strongest regarding abortion:** Gruber wants the state to classify abortion as homicide, remove blanket legal immunity from women obtaining it and potentially impose serious criminal penalties. He also favors a public order governed by Christian moral truth rather than liberal neutrality.

But the available evidence does not establish that he has produced a comprehensive authoritarian constitution. Unlike Webbon, he is not chiefly known for advocating male-only political authority or restricting women’s votes. Unlike Wolfe, he has not laid out an extensive theory of suppressing public blasphemy or formally subordinating minority religions.

The most accurate description is:

> **Gruber is a militant Christian anti-abortion abolitionist whose Christian nationalism is built around criminalizing abortion, mobilizing churches and restoring what he regards as America’s Christian civilizational order.**

His politics can produce authoritarian outcomes—particularly surveillance, investigation and punishment of pregnancies—without yet amounting to the fully specified confessional regime proposed by some Christian-nationalist theorists.

# Harrison Smith

Assuming you mean **Harrison H. Smith, the Infowars host of *The American Journal* and later *War Room***: he differs from Stephen Wolfe or Joel Webbon because he is not primarily a theologian and has not published a systematic Christian-nationalist political program. He is better understood as a **far-right, conspiratorial ethnonationalist broadcaster whose politics sometimes merge Christianity, Western identity, race, and nationalism**. Infowars itself describes him as a host presenting “anti-globalist” political analysis. ([Infowars][1])

## Christianity and national identity

Smith generally portrays America and the broader West as civilizations that ought to retain their historically Christian character. His rhetoric divides politics into a struggle between traditional Christian-Western society and forces he labels globalist, Marxist, secular, Islamist, or culturally revolutionary.

However, I have not found evidence that he has articulated a detailed position on:

* an established national church;
* blasphemy statutes;
* biblical criminal penalties;
* clerical control of government;
* women’s suffrage or household voting.

It would therefore be misleading to attribute Wolfe’s confessional-state theory or Webbon’s proposed Christian political hierarchy directly to Smith. His Christian nationalism is **more cultural and ethnocivilizational than theological or constitutional**.

## Immigration and demographic replacement

Smith’s most distinctive position is his treatment of immigration as an existential demographic threat to white Western populations.

He has described large-scale migration into Ireland as “white genocide” and “replacement migration.” In the same discussion, he said that he did not want his son to become “a despised minority in his own country” and endorsed the premise that white people possess a collective right to preserve their ethnic existence and demographic majority. ([Anarchist Federation][2])

This goes considerably beyond ordinary arguments for lower immigration. It contains three ethnonationalist assumptions:

1. A country belongs in a special sense to its historically dominant ethnic population.
2. Demographic change can constitute dispossession even without physical expulsion.
3. The preservation of a white majority is a legitimate political objective.

That is why critics associate Smith with the **“Great Replacement”** or “white genocide” narrative. Those terms describe the belief that immigration, interracial families, differential birth rates, or elite policy are deliberately replacing white populations.

## Race and national membership

Smith’s conception of nationality appears to be partly cultural but also meaningfully ancestral and racial. He does not consistently treat citizenship alone as sufficient for full national belonging.

In June 2026, discussing a Trinidad-born federal judge, Smith reportedly said she should not be in the country, accused foreign-born judges of retaining loyalty to their former countries and complained about “the browning of our country.” The judge in question had become a U.S. citizen and was exercising lawful judicial authority, so Smith’s objection was not merely to unlawful immigration. ([Media Matters][3])

This is an authoritarian tendency because it divides citizens into gradations of legitimacy:

* ancestral or culturally assimilated citizens are treated as the authentic nation;
* naturalized citizens remain presumptively foreign;
* racial or cultural difference becomes evidence of divided loyalty;
* equal legal citizenship can be overridden by ethnic belonging.

That model resembles **ethnocracy** more than civic nationalism.

## Antisemitic rhetoric

Smith has also used explicitly antisemitic language. During a 2023 campaign against the Anti-Defamation League, he referred to the organization as America’s “self-appointed, unelected Jewish overlords” and praised far-right activists who helped make the campaign trend online. ([Anarchist Federation][4])

Opposition to the ADL is not inherently antisemitic; it is a political organization that can be criticized. The problem is the collective and conspiratorial framing of “Jewish overlords,” which presents Jewish institutional influence as covert ethnic domination.

This fits a recurring pattern in radical nationalist politics: institutions and elites are not merely criticized for particular decisions but depicted as agents of an ethnically coordinated project against the nation.

## “Globalism” and conspiracy politics

Smith’s worldview is organized heavily around **anti-globalism**. In this framework, major institutions—media organizations, corporations, public-health agencies, courts, international bodies and immigration systems—are frequently interpreted as participating in a coordinated assault on national independence and traditional society.

Some skepticism toward concentrated power is compatible with democracy. Smith’s version becomes authoritarian or conspiratorial when it implies that:

* institutions have no legitimate independence when they oppose his political side;
* judges and officials can be dismissed as foreign or treasonous;
* election, health, demographic and cultural developments are coordinated parts of one hostile project;
* extraordinary countermeasures are justified because ordinary politics has supposedly been captured.

This type of narrative weakens democratic restraints by defining adverse rulings, electoral losses or expert findings not as potentially legitimate outcomes but as evidence of enemy control.

## COVID-19 and medical misinformation

Smith and the wider Infowars network promoted extensive claims portraying the pandemic response and vaccination campaigns as deliberate systems of population control, deception or mass injury. Infowars has repeatedly published claims about vaccines that independent fact-checkers found false or unsupported, while Smith has continued to describe the pandemic in conspiratorial terms such as a “psyop” or “plandemic.” ([PolitiFact][5])

The authoritarian element here is somewhat paradoxical. Smith describes himself as opposing government authoritarianism, especially mandates and emergency powers. But his conspiratorial framework can support a different form of authoritarian politics: purges, prosecutions or punishment of public servants and medical professionals who are presumed to have knowingly participated in mass crimes.

## Abortion, sexuality and gender

Smith adopts the familiar hard-right position that abortion is mass killing and that contemporary LGBT politics represents civilizational decline. An Infowars retrospective authored by him described abortion-rights politics as permitting “post-birth abortions” and characterized drag-related children’s events through allegations of sexual predation. ([Infowars][6])

The broader pattern is:

* abortion is portrayed as organized killing rather than a disputed question of personhood and bodily autonomy;
* transgender recognition is portrayed as indoctrination or corruption of children;
* drag performances and LGBT visibility are frequently merged rhetorically with pedophilia;
* cultural compromise is treated as complicity with evil.

This framing is compatible with broad criminal restrictions, although I have not located a detailed Smith-authored penal code explaining precisely what punishments he favors.

## Relationship to democracy

Smith is not best described as offering an orderly alternative constitution. His authoritarianism is more **populist and exclusionary**:

* “the people” are implicitly the culturally authentic, historically rooted population;
* immigrants, naturalized citizens and minority institutions may be treated as less legitimately American;
* independent courts are suspect when they obstruct nationalist political goals;
* journalism and expertise are interpreted as arms of a coordinated regime;
* political opposition becomes an existential enemy rather than a legitimate competing constituency.

He would deny being authoritarian. From his perspective, unelected institutions, public-health mandates, immigration policy and international organizations are the true authoritarian forces. He presents nationalism as democratic self-defense.

The criticism is that his concept of “the people” is too narrow. A movement can oppose bureaucratic or global power while simultaneously favoring the exclusion, denaturalization, subordination or suppression of people it does not regard as authentic members of the nation.

## Comparison with the other figures

**Stephen Wolfe** provides the most systematic argument for a Protestant confessional nation and Christian magistrate.

**Joel Webbon** more explicitly connects Christian rule with patriarchy, unequal political authority and possible enforcement of biblical morality.

**Seth Gruber** centers his politics on abortion abolition and Christian civilizational restoration.

**Harrison Smith** is principally a conspiratorial ethnonationalist media figure. Christianity forms part of the civilization he wants to preserve, but his defining themes are demographic replacement, racial-cultural continuity, anti-globalism, suspicion of foreign-born citizens and conspiratorial opposition to governing institutions.

The most accurate summary is that Smith’s authoritarian tendencies arise **less from a developed theology of the state than from an ethnically bounded conception of the nation and a belief that hostile elites have captured normal democratic institutions**.

# Alex McFarland

**Alex McFarland is a conservative evangelical apologist and political commentator**, not a systematic theorist of Christian government like Stephen Wolfe or an openly anti-democratic patriarchalist like Joel Webbon. He embraces the Christian-nationalist label in a relatively **constitutional and culture-war-oriented form**: America should recover its Christian moral foundations, elect “godly” leaders and enact laws shaped by a biblical worldview. He simultaneously insists that this should not mean forced conversion or formal clerical rule. ([Alex McFarland][1])

## His version of Christian nationalism

McFarland argues that America was profoundly shaped by Christianity and that its constitutional freedoms cannot survive without a religious and virtuous population. He rejects the idea that faith should be confined to private life and says Christians have a duty to elect people who will make “godly laws.” His voter materials ask whether candidates interpret public questions through a Christian worldview and support biblical positions on family, abortion and gender. ([Alex McFarland][2])

His preferred model appears to be:

* constitutional government rather than direct rule by clergy;
* robust Christian influence over legislation and public culture;
* official accommodation of prayer, Christian expression and religious organizations;
* laws protecting what he understands as biblical morality;
* continued formal religious liberty.

McFarland explicitly denies that Christian nationalism necessarily means “forcing Christianity on others.” He characterizes it as preserving America’s Judeo-Christian foundations and constitutional freedoms. ([Alex McFarland][1])

That makes him closer to **religious majoritarianism** than to a completely worked-out theocracy. Nevertheless, critics would ask whether religious liberty remains equal when one tradition supplies the assumed moral identity of the state.

## Abortion

McFarland is strongly anti-abortion and describes abortion as violence against unborn children. He supports political action to restrict it and praises politicians who use federal power against abortion policy. His voting guidance treats abortion as a central test of whether a candidate governs according to biblical truth. ([Alex McFarland][3])

Unlike Seth Gruber, however, McFarland is not primarily known for developing a detailed “abortion abolitionist” penal framework. I did not find clear evidence that he advocates prosecuting women who obtain abortions or imposing biblical homicide penalties on them. His public role is closer to conventional religious-right advocacy for legal protection of fetal life.

## Same-sex marriage and LGBT rights

McFarland believes marriage is exclusively between a man and a woman. After same-sex marriage became legally recognized nationwide, he argued that civil law could not make it legitimate before God. His political materials urge voters to support candidates who affirm heterosexual marriage as the proper basis of family life. ([Alex McFarland][4])

He also treats homosexuality and transgender identity as violations of objective moral and biological order rather than identities the state must affirm. He has supported government efforts restricting gender-transition interventions and argues that transgender women should not be legally treated as women in at least some contexts. ([Alex McFarland][5])

The potentially authoritarian aspect lies in converting those theological conclusions into public restrictions. But the evidence reviewed does not establish that he supports criminalizing private same-sex relationships, executing sexual offenders under Old Testament law or suppressing all LGBT speech. His documented positions center more on marriage law, education, parental authority, medical policy and institutional recognition.

## Parental authority and education

McFarland views parents—not schools, medical institutions or the state—as possessing primary authority over children’s moral and sexual formation. He strongly opposes policies that could treat a parent’s refusal to affirm a child’s gender identity as abuse or as grounds for custody decisions. He characterizes such measures as unconstitutional attacks on parental rights. ([Alex McFarland][6])

He favors explicitly Christian worldview education and believes young people are being subjected to secular or progressive indoctrination. This is a major feature of his political project: transforming culture through churches, schools, apologetics programs and voting rather than chiefly through a new constitutional order. ([Alex McFarland][7])

## Islam and religious pluralism

McFarland formally affirms constitutional religious freedom, but his rhetoric concerning Islam is substantially more exclusionary. He warns that Islamic institutions seek political and cultural supremacy, calls for Christians to reverse Islam’s growing influence and presents Islamic schools and mosques as possible threats to America’s Christian foundations. In an earlier controversy, he called officials who approved a mosque and cemetery without sufficient public input “traitors” to the Constitution. ([Alex McFarland][8])

This does not amount to a clearly stated proposal to outlaw Islam. Still, it creates tension within his account of religious liberty: Christianity is depicted as the foundation of American freedom, whereas Muslim institutional growth is often treated as a civilizational danger.

That is one place where critics may reasonably identify a **Christian-supremacist tendency**, even though McFarland would frame his position as defending constitutionalism against an allegedly anti-constitutional ideology.

## Trump and partisan politics

McFarland has been a strong defender of Donald Trump, generally arguing that Christians may support a morally imperfect leader when that leader advances policies on abortion, religious liberty, courts and cultural issues. He has criticized evangelical efforts to detach Christianity from Republican partisan politics and portrayed voting for conservative candidates as an urgent defense of biblical ideals. ([Alex McFarland][3])

This reflects an instrumental view of political leadership: personal moral character matters, but policy outcomes affecting abortion, religious institutions and sexuality can outweigh a politician’s personal conduct.

## His most alarming authoritarian-adjacent statement

The strongest evidence for genuinely authoritarian or potentially violent politics comes from January 2021. Shortly after the attack on the U.S. Capitol, McFarland reportedly compared the contemporary political crisis to the American Revolution and argued that people who believe the Revolution’s use of force was biblically justified would have grounds for saying force could be justified under contemporary conditions. ([The Roys Report][9])

That is more serious than ordinary “take America back” rhetoric because it potentially legitimizes political violence when believers conclude that government has become tyrannical.

Context and precision matter here. This does not by itself prove that McFarland endorsed the Capitol attack, called for immediate insurrection or developed an organized revolutionary program. But invoking the Revolution and the biblical permissibility of force immediately after political violence substantially weakens his claim to represent only normal constitutional engagement.

## Does he favor theocracy?

The available evidence does **not** show McFarland advocating:

* government by pastors;
* abolition of elections;
* repeal of women’s suffrage;
* religious tests limiting office to Christians;
* blasphemy prosecutions;
* Old Testament civil penalties;
* suppression of all non-Christian worship.

He repeatedly invokes the Constitution, religious liberty, elections and separation of governmental powers. ([Alex McFarland][10])

However, his vision is not religiously neutral. He wants Christianity to furnish the nation’s moral vocabulary and expects Christians to use elections and law to institutionalize biblical positions. The most fitting description is therefore **Christian majoritarian constitutionalism**: formally retaining democratic institutions and religious liberty while seeking enduring Christian dominance over public norms.

## Why critics might still call it authoritarian

The authoritarian critique rests on several tendencies:

1. **Biblical truth is treated as the proper basis of law**, leaving less room for citizens who reject his theology.
2. **LGBT equality is framed as moral disorder**, supporting restrictions on marriage, education, healthcare and legal recognition.
3. **Islam is treated differently from Christianity**, despite formal commitments to religious liberty.
4. **Partisan victory becomes a spiritual imperative**, encouraging Christians to view political opponents as enemies of God’s order.
5. **Political force has been described as potentially justifiable** under sufficiently revolutionary conditions.

McFarland is therefore **less openly authoritarian than Webbon and less theocratic than Wolfe’s ideal state**. His project works principally through elections, constitutional rhetoric, education and conservative legislation. The danger critics identify is not a fully specified dictatorship but the gradual transformation of equal religious citizenship into a system where Christianity remains legally free and politically privileged while dissenting identities retain rights only within boundaries set by conservative Christian morality.

# David Barton

**David Barton is one of the most influential popularizers of the idea that the United States was founded as an explicitly Christian nation.** He is the founder of WallBuilders, an organization that says its mission is to recover America’s “moral, Christian, and constitutional foundation.” Unlike Stephen Wolfe, Barton does not present a detailed theory of a future Christian state. His main contribution is a historical narrative meant to show that Christian political dominance is not a radical departure but a restoration of the founders’ original design. ([WallBuilders][1])

## His central argument

Barton argues that the founders intended Christianity and biblical principles to play a privileged role in American public life. He rejects the modern understanding of a strong “wall of separation” between church and state, portraying it as a later judicial invention that gradually secularized government, schools and public institutions. ([WallBuilders][2])

His preferred political order generally includes:

* public acknowledgment of God and Christianity;
* greater freedom for government officials and public schools to promote prayer, the Bible and Christian teachings;
* legislation informed by what he considers biblical morality;
* active political organization by pastors and churches;
* judges who interpret the Constitution according to what Barton identifies as the founders’ original Christian intentions.

He does not ordinarily advocate pastors directly ruling the country or forced conversion. His model is better described as **Christian constitutional restorationism**: existing American institutions remain, but their legitimate meaning is reinterpreted through his account of a Christian founding.

## Christianity and citizenship

Barton generally affirms formal religious liberty and does not publicly propose stripping Jews, Muslims or atheists of citizenship. His argument is instead that Christianity has a special historical and moral status because it supplied the principles on which American freedom rests.

That produces an important tension. Under his framework, non-Christians may possess legal rights, but Christianity remains the culture that defines the nation’s fundamental identity. Religious minorities therefore risk being treated as beneficiaries of a Christian constitutional order rather than as equal authors of a genuinely pluralistic one.

This is a softer form of religious hierarchy than Joel Webbon’s proposed patriarchal Christian regime, but it still departs from a state that remains neutral among competing religions.

## The “Seven Mountains” question

Barton has participated in circles associated with the **Seven Mountains** idea—the goal of increasing Christian influence over government, education, media, business, family, religion, and arts and entertainment. Critics interpret that movement as a strategy for Christian control of society. ([People For][3])

Barton, however, explicitly denies being a “Dominionist” in the theological sense of believing that Christians must establish God’s kingdom before Christ returns. He says he disagrees with Dominion or “Kingdom Now” theology. ([WallBuilders][4])

Both facts can be true. He may reject a particular end-times doctrine while still advocating aggressive Christian institutional influence. The strongest supportable claim is therefore that Barton promotes **Christian cultural and political ascendancy**, not necessarily that he endorses every theological doctrine associated with dominionism.

## Abortion, sexuality and public morality

Barton belongs firmly to the conservative Christian political movement on abortion, same-sex marriage, gender and education. He treats these not merely as private moral questions but as issues on which biblical principles should shape law and public policy.

His approach typically emphasizes:

* legal protection of unborn life;
* heterosexual marriage as the proper public definition of marriage;
* opposition to government recognition or promotion of transgender identities;
* parental and Christian influence over education;
* resistance to secular court decisions that displace traditional Christian norms.

He is not chiefly known for advocating the criminalization of private homosexual conduct, biblical death penalties or prosecution of women for abortion. His influence operates more through constitutional argument, electoral activism and educational policy than through a detailed theonomic penal code.

## Pastors and partisan political power

Barton argues that pastors should not avoid politics. WallBuilders encourages Christians to vote, influence public policy and understand political participation as part of their religious responsibility. He has worked extensively with Republican politicians and conservative ministries, presenting electoral victories and judicial appointments as means of restoring biblical constitutional principles. ([TIME][5])

This differs from merely saying that Christians may vote according to conscience. Barton presents organized Christian political engagement as necessary to recover the nation’s authentic identity.

Critics say this sacralizes partisan politics: Republican policy goals become associated with the founders, the Bible and divine providence, while opponents are portrayed as undermining both Christianity and the Constitution.

## His theory of religious liberty

Barton often argues that religious liberty originally meant protecting religion—especially Christianity—from federal interference, not removing religion from government. He emphasizes that early public officials issued prayer proclamations, that religious expression appeared in government settings and that some states maintained religious establishments after ratification of the Constitution.

Those facts complicate simplistic claims that the early republic was thoroughly secular. But most historians distinguish them from the stronger claim that the federal Constitution established a Christian nation. The Constitution does not identify Christianity as an official religion, prohibits religious tests for federal office and, through the First Amendment, prevents a federal religious establishment. ([AP News][6])

## The historical controversy

Barton’s political influence depends heavily on his presentation of history, and this is also the most controversial part of his work.

He uses large collections of founding-era documents and quotations to argue that modern secular accounts conceal the founders’ Christianity. Critics—including conservative evangelical historians—accuse him of:

* selecting quotations that support his conclusions while omitting contrary context;
* treating generic references to Providence or morality as evidence of orthodox Christianity;
* conflating the religious beliefs of individual founders with the constitutional powers of government;
* presenting disputed interpretations as settled historical facts;
* making factual or sourcing errors.

The most prominent controversy involved *The Jefferson Lies*. Its original publisher, Thomas Nelson, withdrew the book in 2012 after concluding that its historical claims were not adequately supported. Christian scholars Warren Throckmorton and Michael Coulter were among those who documented errors and misleading omissions. ([The Christian Science Monitor][7])

Barton and WallBuilders reject the broader charge that his work is fraudulent. They argue that critics selectively attack him, privilege academic authority over original documents and apply inconsistent standards. WallBuilders has also acknowledged that some quotations formerly used in its materials could not be confirmed and has labeled them accordingly. ([WallBuilders][8])

The fairest conclusion is not that everything Barton says about religion in early America is false. Christianity clearly influenced colonial and founding-era culture. The more serious problem is that he often moves from **“Christianity was deeply influential”** to **“the founders created a specifically Christian constitutional state,”** a conclusion most professional historians regard as unsupported or substantially overstated. ([AP News][6])

## Is Barton authoritarian?

Barton is less openly authoritarian than Webbon, Wolfe or Dale Partridge. He does not generally advocate:

* abolishing democratic elections;
* repealing women’s suffrage;
* rule by clergy;
* public blasphemy prosecutions;
* explicit religious tests for citizenship;
* a comprehensive biblical criminal code.

The authoritarian concern is more indirect.

First, Barton treats one religious tradition as the authentic source of national legitimacy. Second, he supplies a historical justification for policies that weaken church–state separation. Third, by claiming that conservative Christian policies represent the founders’ “original intent,” he can portray pluralistic or secular policies not merely as political alternatives but as illegitimate departures from the real Constitution.

His work can therefore support **majoritarian and exclusionary government while retaining the outward structure of constitutional democracy**.

## Compared with the other figures

**Stephen Wolfe** offers a sophisticated argument for an explicitly Protestant nation and Christian magistrate.

**Joel Webbon** more openly supports patriarchal hierarchy, unequal political participation and coercive Christian government.

**David Barton** supplies the historical story that makes such projects seem traditionally American. His distinctive claim is essentially:

> America was founded on biblical Christianity; secularism displaced that order; Christian political action is therefore restoration rather than revolution.

That makes Barton less a theorist of authoritarian machinery than an **intellectual and historical legitimizer of Christian nationalism**. His influence lies in teaching politicians, pastors and voters to see Christian political supremacy as the recovery of the constitutional founding rather than the creation of a new religious state.

## Lance Wallnau

Lance Wallnau is one of the most influential advocates of the **Seven Mountain Mandate**. This teaching says Christians should gain commanding influence within seven major spheres: government, education, media, business, arts and entertainment, family, and religion. Wallnau helped popularize this program through *Invading Babylon* and his political organizing. ([The Gospel Coalition][3])

### His idea of Christian power

Wallnau argues that evangelism alone is insufficient if secular or anti-Christian forces control the institutions that form society. Christians must therefore enter, influence and ultimately lead those institutions.

Supporters often prefer the word **influence** rather than domination. Yet Wallnau’s strategy is not simply for Christians to participate as equals. It aims to displace the prevailing liberal or secular leadership of major institutions and replace it with “Kingdom” leadership.

He also popularized the comparison of Trump to the Persian king Cyrus: an imperfect, non-devout ruler whom God nevertheless chooses to protect or restore God’s people. This provides a theological justification for supporting Trump despite personal conduct that would ordinarily violate evangelical moral expectations.

### Spiritual warfare and political opponents

Wallnau interprets politics through charismatic concepts of spiritual warfare, prophecy and demonic influence. During the 2024 campaign, he described Kamala Harris’s political rise in terms connected to witchcraft and led a pro-Trump revival-and-mobilization tour in swing states. ([The Guardian][4])

This framing changes the status of political opposition. A rival is no longer merely wrong about policy; the rival may represent a demonic structure that Christians have a spiritual duty to overthrow.

### Why critics call it dominionist or authoritarian

The authoritarian concern is strongest in the Seven Mountains model:

* institutions are viewed as territories to be captured;
* religious victory takes priority over institutional neutrality;
* pluralism is provisional rather than intrinsically valuable;
* political opponents can be classified as agents of spiritual evil;
* charismatic leaders may claim insight into God’s plans that cannot be democratically verified.

Wallnau does not normally present a detailed criminal code or explicitly demand abolition of elections. His project is closer to **institutional capture through elections, appointments, media and religious mobilization**. But its desired endpoint is not a balanced pluralistic system; it is sustained Christian leadership over the culture-forming centers of society.

Among these four, Wallnau supplies the clearest **strategy for acquiring Christian political and cultural power**.

## Franklin Graham

Franklin Graham is the president of Samaritan’s Purse and the Billy Graham Evangelistic Association. He is a highly influential conservative evangelical and longtime Trump ally. Unlike Wallnau or Beckwith, however, he **rejects “Christian nationalism” as a media-created label** intended to divide or discredit conservative Christians. ([Christian Post][5])

### His actual political outlook

Even while rejecting the label, Graham advances several ideas commonly associated with Christian nationalism:

* America has turned away from God and must recover biblical values;
* Christians must vote and use political power to protect the nation;
* abortion, same-sex marriage and transgender recognition are signs of moral decline;
* conservative Christians face growing persecution from secular institutions;
* Trump has been an unusually important defender of Christianity.

Graham has repeatedly defended Trump and treated Republican electoral success as crucial to protecting religious liberty, Israel, conservative judges and opposition to abortion. His political partnership with Trump has been much closer than his father Billy Graham’s later posture of bipartisan pastoral access. ([The New Yorker][6])

### Islam

Graham has made sweeping and inflammatory statements about Islam and previously supported stopping Muslim immigration into the United States. ([The Washington Post][7])

This is one of the clearest exclusionary dimensions of his politics. Although he supports evangelizing and providing humanitarian assistance to Muslims, he has also treated Islam collectively as a security and civilizational threat. That creates a tension between his stated defense of religious freedom and his willingness to support restrictions directed at a particular religion.

### Abortion and LGBT issues

Graham supports extensive legal restrictions on abortion and opposes same-sex marriage and transgender recognition. His organizations require employees and volunteers to subscribe to conservative evangelical doctrines concerning marriage and sexuality. During Samaritan’s Purse’s operation of a temporary hospital in New York, its faith requirements generated criticism that LGBT people were being excluded from volunteering. ([Them][8])

Those policies do not by themselves prove a desire to criminalize private homosexuality or establish theocracy. Graham’s documented program remains closer to conventional religious-right lawmaking: abortion restrictions, conservative judicial appointments, religious exemptions, parental control and opposition to LGBT-inclusive policy.

### Democracy and authoritarianism

Graham is more institutionally conventional than Vaughn or Wallnau. Importantly, he ultimately acknowledged that Trump lost the 2020 presidential election. ([Religion Media Centre][9])

I have not found evidence that Graham advocates:

* canceling elections;
* repealing women’s suffrage;
* criminalizing blasphemy;
* barring all non-Christians from office;
* replacing the Constitution with biblical law.

The authoritarian criticism is therefore more qualified. It concerns his willingness to:

* grant extraordinary moral indulgence to a favored strongman;
* portray one political party as the principal protector of Christianity;
* encourage fear of Muslims and LGBT people;
* blur evangelism, national identity and partisan loyalty;
* support a privileged public position for conservative Christianity.

The best description is **partisan Christian nationalism in practice, coupled with denial of the label**. He seeks Christian conservative dominance through existing constitutional institutions, not an openly declared Christian autocracy.

## Micah Beckwith

The spelling is **Micah Beckwith**. He is a pastor and, as of August 2026, Indiana’s lieutenant governor. Of these four, he is the clearest example of an elected official who **personally accepts the Christian-nationalist identity**. ([Indiana Citizen][10])

### Christian nationalism

Beckwith argues that America’s rights and constitutional order come from God and are grounded in a biblical worldview. He rejects the idea that officials can separate their religious commitments from public office and treats pastoral and political leadership as closely connected.

His version is not as theoretically developed as Wolfe’s. In practice, it emphasizes:

* restoring Christianity to government and public education;
* opposition to abortion and LGBT equality;
* aggressive parental control over school and library content;
* resistance to secularism and diversity initiatives;
* treating political conflict as spiritual warfare.

Beckwith’s selection as lieutenant governor was unusual because Indiana Republican delegates rejected the gubernatorial nominee’s preferred running mate in favor of Beckwith, who had cultivated the party’s activist base with uncompromising positions on abortion, gender and sexuality. ([AP News][11])

### Abortion and sexuality

Beckwith treats abortion not simply as an erroneous policy but as demonic evil. During the 2024 campaign, he associated abortion-rights politics with the “Jezebel spirit” and described pro-choice positions in explicitly spiritual terms. ([Wikipedia][12])

He also opposes gender-transition policies and LGBT-inclusive material for minors. During his service on a public-library board, he backed rules that resulted in contested books being removed or relocated from young-adult sections.

These positions are authoritarian-adjacent when government suppresses lawful material according to one official’s religious doctrine, especially where standards such as “immorality” or “indoctrination” are applied selectively.

### Threatening a student newspaper

One of the strongest examples came after Indiana University’s student newspaper published a critical front page about Trump. Beckwith called it “woke propaganda” and said it needed to stop or officials would stop it for them. He subsequently threatened its taxpayer support, while saying he would defend its speech only when its reporting was “fair.” ([Wikipedia][12])

That is a straightforward authoritarian tendency: the government official treats press freedom as conditional on favorable or ideologically acceptable coverage.

### Islam and unequal religious citizenship

In May 2026, Beckwith said, **“I hate Islam”**, called it a “demonic death cult” and said Americans needed “permission to hate again.” He later defended the remarks and said he wanted Muslims to convert to Christianity. ([WFYI][13])

A distinction is necessary: criticizing Islamic doctrine, even harshly, is protected political and religious speech. The democratic concern becomes much greater when such language comes from a sitting state official who openly identifies with Christian nationalism. Muslim citizens may reasonably question whether they will receive equal treatment from an official who portrays their religion as demonic and deserving hatred.

I have not found a specific Beckwith proposal to outlaw Islam. But his rhetoric creates a clear **Christian-supremacist implication**: Christianity is the rightful public faith, while Islam is treated as an alien and malignant presence.

### Race and history

Beckwith also called the Constitution’s Three-Fifths Compromise a “great move,” arguing that it limited slaveholding power and helped end slavery. Historians and civil-rights groups criticized this as misleading because the compromise increased the congressional and presidential power of slave states by counting enslaved people for representation while denying them political rights. ([The Washington Post][14])

The authoritarian relevance is indirect but important: historical revision can legitimize existing hierarchies by portraying arrangements that denied equal citizenship as prudent or benevolent.

### How authoritarian is Beckwith?

Unlike Wallnau, Beckwith already exercises government power. His authoritarian tendencies include:

1. **Treating Christianity as the authentic basis of government.**
2. **Using demonic language for political and religious opponents.**
3. **Threatening hostile press with government retaliation.**
4. **Favoring restrictions on library and educational material.**
5. **Undermining equal religious citizenship through attacks on Islam.**
6. **Presenting public policy as obedience to divine truth rather than negotiation among equal citizens.**

He has not, so far as the evidence reviewed shows, proposed abolishing elections, repealing women’s suffrage or imposing a comprehensive biblical penal code. His position is best called **electoral Christian nationalism with majoritarian and authoritarian tendencies**.

**Lance Wallnau** supplies a dominion-oriented strategy for Christians to capture the institutions that govern society, reinforced by prophecy and spiritual-warfare rhetoric.

**Franklin Graham** remains inside conventional electoral and constitutional politics, but helps sacralize Trump, Christianize national identity and cast religious or sexual minorities as threats. His authoritarianism is therefore more enabling and majoritarian than programmatic.

# Joshua Haymes

**Joshua Haymes is among the more openly coercive figures in this Christian-nationalist milieu.** He hosts the *Reformation Red Pill* podcast and explicitly describes his project as helping to “reChristianize America.” He is influenced by the Reformed, postmillennial and Christian-reconstructionist circles associated with the Communion of Reformed Evangelical Churches, although he is now principally a media commentator rather than a church pastor. ([Reformation Red Pill][1])

Unlike David Barton, who usually frames Christian politics as constitutional restoration, Haymes openly entertains **severe criminal penalties, religiously defined law, patriarchal authority and ethnocultural nationalism**.

## Christian government and “God’s law”

Haymes rejects liberal religious neutrality. His politics begin with the proposition that civil government is accountable to God and should enforce justice as defined by Scripture—not merely protect individual choice.

He has placed his own politics beyond the conventional “far right,” labeling his preferred position **“God’s law.”** His stated policy program has included abolishing abortion and IVF, banning pornography and gay adoption, overturning *Obergefell v. Hodges*, outlawing no-fault divorce, banning Pride parades as public indecency and restoring public executions. ([The Guardian][2])

This is not clergy directly administering government, but it is theocratic in the broader sense: **one religious interpretation supplies the state’s binding moral and penal standard.**

## Public executions and capital punishment

Haymes has stated:

> “Public executions are Biblical. Therefore public executions are good.”

He has advocated their return, including a preference for public firing squads in which accusers or witnesses participate. He supports capital punishment for abortion and has said that gender-transition surgery should be treated as a capital crime. Regarding adultery, he has said he is not presently eager to impose execution but is not opposed to it “in principle”; he clearly favors criminalizing adultery and imposing serious penalties. ([The Guardian][2])

This is a particularly strong example of authoritarianism because the state would not merely regulate conduct to prevent immediate harm. It would enforce a comprehensive religious morality using highly visible violence intended partly to deter and morally instruct the population.

## Abortion and IVF

Haymes regards abortion as the murder of a child and says it should receive capital punishment. His proposed abolition of IVF likely rests on the destruction, indefinite storage or selective use of embryos involved in many IVF procedures. ([The Guardian][2])

An important ambiguity is **who** would face execution in abortion cases—the provider, the pregnant woman or both—and how intent and coercion would be assessed. His public rhetoric establishes support for capital punishment for “abortion,” but not a carefully drafted criminal statute.

## Sexuality and LGBT people

Haymes describes homosexuality as “uniquely evil.” His proposed political order would:

* overturn nationwide recognition of same-sex marriage;
* prohibit same-sex couples from adopting;
* ban Pride events as indecent;
* potentially treat gender-transition surgery as a capital offense.

After posting a biblical allusion involving millstones and drowning in response to a Pride event, he denied advocating violence against marchers and said he meant that divine judgment awaited those he believed were corrupting children. That qualification matters, but it exists alongside his explicit endorsement of state executions for other sexual and moral offenses. ([The Guardian][2])

His program therefore goes well beyond refusing to affirm LGBT identities. It would use state authority to eliminate their legal recognition, family rights, public visibility and some medical practices.

## Patriarchy and women

Haymes advocates a strong form of marital patriarchy. In 2026, he advised men to seek wives who would obey them and submit to their authority, adding that a husband could “cure” a godly woman of feminism if she were willing to obey. ([People For][3])

He has also said voting is not a sacred God-given right. That statement does not by itself prove that he personally supports repealing the Nineteenth Amendment, but it places him within a network where household voting and male political representation are openly debated.

The authoritarian issue is that submission is not treated simply as a voluntarily chosen marital arrangement. It becomes part of a larger social ideal in which male household, church and political authority are considered divinely ordained.

## Slavery

One of Haymes’s most controversial positions is that **slavery is not inherently sinful**. He has said that owning another human being is not intrinsically evil and that Christians should be prepared to defend that conclusion because the Bible regulates slavery without categorically prohibiting it.

He distinguishes allegedly “biblical” slavery from abuse and says cruel or dehumanizing treatment is wrong. He also argues that slaveholding founders or theologians should not automatically be condemned as unrepentant sinners merely for owning slaves. ([People For][4])

That distinction does not remove the central implication: his position denies that one person’s legal ownership of another is inherently unjust. It subordinates modern principles of equal personhood and freedom to his reading of biblical authority.

I have not found evidence that Haymes has proposed reinstating American slavery as an immediate policy. The accurate claim is that **he morally defends the institution in principle**, not necessarily that he has published a plan to restore plantation slavery.

## Race, immigration and national identity

Haymes’s nationalism is not merely civic or religious. He has described the culture he wants to preserve as **Anglo-Protestant** and argued that liberal globalists intentionally encourage nonwhite and non-Christian immigration to replace the white voting population.

He has endorsed “diversity is not our strength,” praised the preservation of a people and place as a Christian impulse and favorably reposted arguments associated with white-nationalist writer Jared Taylor. Haymes denies being a white nationalist or supremacist and says no one should be hated because of race. ([The Guardian][2])

The fairest characterization is that his rhetoric contains the major components of **white ethnonationalism or demographic-replacement politics**, even though he rejects the label:

* white demographic continuity is treated as politically significant;
* America is identified especially with Anglo-Protestant culture;
* nonwhite immigration is described as an intentional replacement mechanism;
* racial and religious diversity are portrayed as sources of national dissolution.

## Immigration and ICE

Haymes says the Bible supports ICE raids. He argues that Christian nations have no obligation to finance immigration that damages their culture and that government may aggressively remove unauthorized migrants to preserve national cohesion.

His argument is not limited to legality or border administration. It connects immigration enforcement to protecting Anglo-Protestant identity against demographic replacement. He reaffirmed that view when questioned by reporters. ([The Guardian][2])

This makes his immigration politics simultaneously **theological, punitive and ethnocultural**.

## Non-Christian religions

Haymes expects Christianity to occupy a superior public position. He has celebrated Trump’s Muslim travel restrictions, portrayed the construction of a Hindu monument as evidence of national conquest and called yoga demonic. ([The Guardian][2])

He may distinguish private existence from public religious equality, but his rhetoric does not suggest a pluralistic state in which Christianity, Islam, Hinduism and atheism have equal civic legitimacy. Non-Christian religions are commonly depicted as false, demonic or alien to the nation.

I have not found a detailed Haymes proposal for banning all non-Christian worship or establishing blasphemy courts. His principles, however, would provide substantially weaker protections for religious pluralism than contemporary First Amendment doctrine.

## Antisemitism and Jews

Haymes says he opposes hatred of Jews. At the same time, he has argued that “antisemitic” is no longer a useful category and has minimized neo-Nazi influence compared with the threat he attributes to liberalism. He also emphasized collective Jewish responsibility in the death of Jesus in a way that critics regard as historically dangerous. ([The Guardian][2])

The careful conclusion is not that every criticism he makes is antisemitic. It is that his rhetoric **weakens the conceptual safeguards used to identify antisemitism while minimizing an explicitly antisemitic movement**.

## Confederacy and federal power

Haymes has spoken sympathetically about the Confederacy’s defeat as a loss of federalism and localism. He portrays the Civil War substantially as the federal government denying Southern states the ability to secede and subsequently expanding beyond its proper limits. ([The Guardian][2])

That framing minimizes slavery’s central role in secession. Combined with his defense of slavery as not inherently sinful, it reflects a broader **neo-Confederate or paleo-Confederate interpretation** of American history.

## Elections and political opposition

Haymes does not treat universal voting as an inviolable principle. More broadly, his religious worldview makes democratic legitimacy conditional: a majority cannot legitimately authorize what he believes God has forbidden.

In March 2026, discussing Democratic politician James Talarico, Haymes said, “I pray that God kills him,” before explaining that he meant the death of Talarico’s sinful heart and spiritual rebirth in Christ. That theological clarification is relevant, but the episode also involved describing an elected opponent through imprecatory and enemy-destruction language. ([TPR][5])

This illustrates a recurring problem in his politics: opposition is not merely mistaken but spiritually wicked, making ordinary democratic compromise look like complicity with evil.

## Why “authoritarian” strongly applies

The label is not based merely on Haymes being socially conservative. It follows from his explicit support for:

1. **Public state executions** as instruments of biblical justice.
2. **Capital punishment for religiously defined moral offenses**, including abortion and transition surgery.
3. **Criminalizing adultery and suppressing lawful LGBT public life.**
4. **Patriarchal authority and female obedience.**
5. **Unequal public standing for non-Christian religions.**
6. **An ethnocultural conception of America centered on white Anglo-Protestant continuity.**
7. **Treating individual rights and voting as subordinate to his interpretation of divine law.**
8. **Defending slavery as morally permissible in principle.**

Among the figures discussed, Haymes is closer to **Joel Webbon’s coercive Christian nationalism** than to Franklin Graham’s electoral religious conservatism or David Barton’s historical restorationism. His politics are best summarized as **Christian reconstructionist authoritarianism with patriarchal, punitive and ethnonationalist elements**.

He does not merely want Christians to influence policy. He wants civil law to suppress rival moral systems and visibly punish violations of the social order he believes Scripture requires.

# Doug Wilson

**Doug Wilson is one of the most developed and institutionally influential theorists of American Christian nationalism.** He is the longtime pastor of Christ Church in Moscow, Idaho; a co-founder of the Communion of Reformed Evangelical Churches; and a central figure behind Canon Press, New Saint Andrews College and a network of classical Christian schools. He openly accepts the labels **Christian nationalist** and **theocratic libertarian**. ([RNS][1])

His project is not an immediate military dictatorship. It is a long-term effort to build Christian families, churches, schools, businesses and political influence until an explicitly Christian social order becomes culturally dominant.

## His ideal: “mere Christendom”

Wilson wants nations, including the United States, eventually to acknowledge Jesus Christ publicly and organize civil life according to broadly orthodox Christianity. He has suggested that a Christian nation could recognize a creed such as the Apostles’ Creed and described the desired system as a form of theocracy, although not government directly administered by clergy. ([Financial Times][2])

In his model:

* church and state remain separate institutions;
* both are explicitly accountable to Christ;
* government recognizes Christianity as true;
* civil law reflects biblical and natural-law morality;
* religious neutrality is rejected as impossible;
* non-Christians may retain substantial personal liberty but would live under an officially Christian constitutional order.

Wilson argues that all governments enforce some ultimate moral commitments. He regards secular liberalism as an unofficial religion, so replacing it with Christian public authority is, in his view, not introducing religious government but replacing one religious establishment with another.

## “Theocratic libertarianism”

Wilson combines theocracy with a strong rhetorical commitment to limited government, property rights, local control and free speech. He calls this **theocratic libertarianism**. He envisions a decentralized Christian order rather than an enormous religious bureaucracy. ([Blog & Mablog][3])

This produces a real tension. He says a Christian society should preserve robust freedom of debate, yet he also believes the state can enforce Christian standards concerning worship, sexuality, marriage and public morality. The system may be economically or administratively libertarian while remaining religiously and socially coercive.

## Blasphemy laws

Wilson believes **blasphemy laws are inescapable** because every society protects certain sacred ideas from intolerable attack. He argues that Christians should eventually seek observance of both “tables” of the Ten Commandments—duties toward God as well as duties toward other people. ([YouTube][4])

However, he adds several qualifications:

* the state itself is often history’s greatest blasphemer;
* blasphemy statutes are easily abused;
* Christians should not hand such powers casually to present governments;
* social and religious transformation should precede strong enforcement.

This makes him more cautious than some advocates of immediate blasphemy prosecutions, but he does not reject their legitimacy in principle. His objection is often prudential—**who will enforce them, under what conditions?**—rather than a liberal objection based on an absolute right to religiously equal expression. ([Christ Overall][5])

## Women, patriarchy and voting

Wilson teaches **biblical patriarchy**. Men are to lead churches and households; wives owe submission to husbands; and public life should recognize the household as a fundamental political unit.

He has supported replacing universal individual voting with a form of **household suffrage**, under which the household casts a unified vote ordinarily represented by its male head. In practical terms, married women would not possess an independent vote separate from their husbands. Recent presentations by Wilson and pastors in his network have explicitly described an ideal order with no independent voting rights for married women. ([Baptist News Global][6])

Wilson sometimes emphasizes that unmarried women, widows or female heads of households might still vote. But this does not preserve equal citizenship: a woman’s independent political voice would depend on marital status, while a husband’s would not.

That is among the clearest authoritarian features of his system because it converts theological family hierarchy into unequal civil representation.

## LGBT people and sexual law

Wilson believes same-sex sexual relations are sinful and that *Obergefell v. Hodges*, which established nationwide same-sex marriage, should be overturned. He has supported the legitimacy of anti-sodomy laws and a Christian public order in which LGBT relationships would not receive equal legal or cultural recognition. ([Financial Times][2])

His envisioned system could involve:

* no legal same-sex marriage;
* criminal restrictions on consensual same-sex conduct;
* no official affirmation of transgender identity;
* restrictions on pornography and sexual expression;
* laws reinforcing heterosexual marriage and traditional family authority.

Wilson generally portrays this not as hostility toward people but as enforcement of objective morality. Critics respond that the consequence would still be the loss of equal privacy, marriage and citizenship rights for LGBT people.

## Marriage, divorce and adultery

Wilson regards marriage as a covenantal institution governed by Christian law rather than principally an agreement between autonomous individuals. His political ideal would make divorce much harder and could allow civil punishment of adultery. Recent reporting on his views describes an envisioned Christian government imposing penalties for adultery and eliminating contemporary no-fault-divorce norms. ([Financial Times][2])

This reflects his larger opposition to liberal individualism. The family, not the autonomous individual, is the central social unit, and the government may preserve that unit by restricting personal exit and sexual conduct.

## Slavery

Wilson’s most notorious historical controversy concerns *Southern Slavery As It Was*, a booklet he co-authored with Steve Wilkins. It portrayed antebellum Southern slavery in strikingly benign terms, claiming that many enslaved people experienced material plenty, affection across racial lines and conditions less brutal than commonly depicted. Historians and Christian scholars criticized the work as inaccurate, selective and deeply minimizing of enslavement’s violence and coercion. ([Faith and History][7])

Wilson’s current position is more carefully framed:

* the kidnapping and racialized slave trade were sinful;
* American slavery contained serious cruelty and injustice;
* racism and racial superiority are unbiblical;
* slaveholding is not, in every conceivable biblical context, intrinsically sinful;
* the Civil War was not the only imaginable or necessarily best route to emancipation.

The controversy is therefore not accurately summarized as “Wilson wants to restore American slavery.” There is no clear current program to do that. The problem is that he has repeatedly resisted saying that ownership of another human being is categorically immoral and has promoted an account that substantially softened the realities of Southern slavery.

His reconstruction of slavery also fits his broader rejection of modern equality as the highest political value: hierarchical relationships can, in his framework, be legitimate if properly regulated by Christian duties.

## Race and nationhood

Wilson explicitly rejects white supremacy and says Christian nationalism must welcome believers of all races. His church network is not formally built around a racial membership test.

Nevertheless, critics identify racial implications in:

* his revisionist treatment of Southern slavery;
* sympathy for aspects of the Confederate or Southern cause;
* skepticism toward modern civil-rights frameworks;
* opposition to egalitarianism as a governing social principle;
* an idealized vision of historic Anglo-Protestant culture.

It is more precise to call Wilson a **Christian civilizational nationalist with serious racial revisionism** than simply a biological white nationalist. His primary boundary is confessional—Christian versus non-Christian—but his historical and cultural model is strongly shaped by traditional white Anglo-American institutions.

## Education and building a parallel society

Education is one of Wilson’s most consequential projects. He helped create Logos School, New Saint Andrews College and the classical Christian school movement associated with the Association of Classical Christian Schools.

Wilson sees public education as a primary instrument of secular ideology. His strategy is to remove Christian children from that system, educate them within a comprehensive biblical worldview and build a multigenerational leadership class capable of governing institutions later. His Moscow network also includes publishing, businesses, property ownership and professional organizations. ([RNS][1])

This is why his movement is more durable than a typical online personality’s following. He has built institutions intended to reproduce his worldview across generations.

## Democracy and political change

Wilson does not treat democracy or universal suffrage as sacred. For him, democratic procedures are legitimate only within the higher authority of God’s law. A majority cannot make abortion, same-sex marriage or blasphemy morally legitimate.

At the same time, he generally advocates **gradual transformation through persuasion and institution-building**, not an immediate coup. He predicts that Christian schools, large families, evangelism, local political participation and cultural confidence could produce Christendom over decades or centuries. ([Financial Times][2])

This is sometimes described as peaceful, “bottom-up” dominion. But peaceful means do not necessarily imply a liberal endpoint. The society he seeks would remain structurally unequal regarding sex, religion and sexuality.

## Trump and political influence

Wilson treats Donald Trump as a deeply flawed but useful instrument against progressive cultural power. He has compared Trump’s effect to a harsh medicine: personally toxic but politically necessary under current conditions. ([Financial Times][2])

Wilson’s influence became more nationally significant through the CREC network and its connections to senior government figures, especially Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who has publicly promoted Wilson-related material and attends a CREC congregation. Wilson’s ideas are therefore no longer confined to an isolated Idaho church network. ([AP News][8])

## Is Wilson authoritarian?

**Yes, in the ordinary political-science sense, although his authoritarianism is decentralized, patriarchal and confessional rather than conventionally fascist or bureaucratic.**

The strongest evidence is his support for:

1. unequal voting authority based on sex and household status;
2. an officially Christian civil order;
3. potential blasphemy laws;
4. criminal restrictions on consensual same-sex conduct;
5. legal reinforcement of patriarchal marriage;
6. punishment of religiously defined sexual offenses;
7. subordinating popular sovereignty to his interpretation of biblical law;
8. denying that modern individual equality is the proper basis of political society.

Wilson would reject the label because he favors limited government, property rights, localism, due process and considerable freedom of speech. But authoritarianism is not measured only by the overall size of government. A small government can still enforce severe religious, sexual and gender hierarchies.

## Best placement among the people discussed

Wilson is more intellectually and institutionally important than Joel Webbon or Joshua Haymes. Webbon and Haymes often express harsher and more immediately punitive proposals, but Wilson supplies much of the **theological ecosystem, publishing structure, educational strategy and long-term theory** in which those figures operate.

A concise description would be:

> **Doug Wilson advocates a decentralized Protestant Christendom governed by Christian law, patriarchal households and restricted religious and sexual pluralism, to be achieved primarily through multigenerational cultural and institutional conquest rather than an immediate seizure of power.**

That makes him not merely a culture-war conservative but one of the clearest contemporary architects of a **post-liberal Christian social order**.

# Their influence

The best way is to treat **influence as multidimensional and relational**, not as a single follower count or a simple “important/not important” judgment.

A person with 40,000 followers may matter more than someone with two million if the smaller figure trains legislators, supplies model legislation, controls schools or churches, or serves as a bridge between fringe intellectuals and government officials. Conversely, a large influencer may generate attention without producing durable institutional change.

## A six-part influence framework

### 1. Mass reach

This is the most visible layer:

* followers and subscribers across platforms;
* average views, listens and engagement;
* mailing-list size;
* book sales;
* conference and rally attendance;
* appearances on larger media platforms.

The important measure is **effective reach**, not nominal followers. We should examine typical—not exceptional—views, audience overlap, engagement quality and whether larger accounts repeatedly amplify the person. Pew, for example, uses a threshold of 100,000 followers on at least one major platform to identify prominent online “news influencers,” but that is a research convention rather than proof of political power. ([Pew Research Center][1])

Social reach is best described with terms such as:

* **mass influencer**;
* **niche influencer**;
* **movement-media personality**;
* **micro-influencer with elite reach**;
* **viral but institutionally weak**.

### 2. Network position

We should map **who connects to whom**, not merely who is popular.

For each person, record relationships such as:

* podcast appearances and interviews;
* conference participation;
* church or denominational ties;
* endorsements and shared statements;
* publishing relationships;
* employment and advisory roles;
* campaign appearances;
* donor and nonprofit connections;
* school, ministry and activist networks;
* mutual reposting or citation.

This allows several different forms of centrality to be identified:

**Degree centrality:** how many direct connections someone has.

**Betweenness centrality:** whether someone acts as a bridge between otherwise separate networks—for example, between postmillennial theologians and elected Republicans.

**Eigenvector centrality:** whether someone is connected to other highly influential actors, rather than merely to many minor accounts.

**Brokerage:** whether someone translates ideas from intellectual or religious subcultures into movement media, electoral politics or legislation.

Political-network scholarship uses precisely these distinctions because influence may come from occupying a strategic network role rather than from having the largest audience. ([Cambridge University Press][2])

This is where someone like **Doug Wilson** may rank especially high: his influence is not just his direct audience but his position within a network of churches, schools, publishers, pastors, public officials and younger commentators.

### 3. Institutional capacity

This measures whether the person has durable organizations capable of acting without constant online attention.

Relevant assets include:

* churches and denominations;
* schools and colleges;
* publishing houses;
* nonprofits and political organizations;
* candidate-training programs;
* legal groups;
* activist mailing lists;
* donor networks;
* recurring conferences;
* media studios and distribution systems.

Institutional influence is often more durable than celebrity. A viral personality may disappear, while a school network can reproduce leaders for decades.

Useful classifications would include:

* **institution builder**;
* **organizational executive**;
* **network pastor**;
* **movement entrepreneur**;
* **institutional node without mass visibility**.

Doug Wilson again is a strong example because his ecosystem includes Christ Church, the CREC, Canon Press, New Saint Andrews College and the classical Christian education network. David Barton’s WallBuilders serves a different institutional role: distributing a historical narrative to pastors, activists and political officials.

### 4. Elite and governmental access

This is often more consequential than audience size.

We should track:

* meetings with presidents, governors and cabinet officials;
* appointments to commissions or advisory boards;
* testimony before legislatures;
* relationships with elected officials;
* invitations to government events;
* campaign advisory roles;
* access to party organizations;
* staffers or alumni entering government;
* quotations in official speeches or policy documents.

Think tanks and advocacy organizations exert influence partly by providing ideas, personnel and meeting places for policymakers, not simply by persuading mass audiences. They can function as recruitment networks or “farm teams” for government. ([Brookings][3])

This produces categories such as:

* **elite adviser**;
* **government insider**;
* **party-network influencer**;
* **legislative ally**;
* **outside agitator with limited official access**.

Franklin Graham, for example, has much greater presidential and elite access than many intellectually more radical figures. That does not mean he is ideologically more extreme; it means his **access influence** is larger.

### 5. Policy conversion

This is the strongest evidence of concrete influence: can an idea be traced into government action?

We should look for:

* legislation introduced or enacted;
* executive orders;
* school-board or library policies;
* party-platform language;
* judicial arguments;
* model bills;
* administrative appointments;
* official investigations;
* budget appropriations;
* policy language copied from affiliated organizations;
* similar bills spreading across states.

Policy-diffusion research emphasizes that influence must be traced through networks and mechanisms rather than inferred merely because similar policies appear in multiple places. ([Cambridge University Press][4])

The evidence can be graded:

**Direct:** an official says the person or organization supplied the proposal.

**Strongly supported:** shared text, documented meetings or organizational drafting links.

**Probable:** close timing and network connections, but no explicit acknowledgment.

**Speculative:** ideological resemblance without evidence of transmission.

That last distinction is crucial. We should not say Doug Wilson “caused” a policy merely because an official attends a related church. We would document the relationship and then separately assess whether his ideas appear in the official’s conduct.

### 6. Agenda-setting and ideological influence

Some figures influence politics without writing legislation. They change:

* what subjects become discussable;
* what terminology activists use;
* which policies move from fringe to acceptable;
* how followers interpret events;
* which historical narratives justify political action;
* what younger leaders regard as possible.

This is **agenda-setting influence**. A person may make proposals such as household voting, blasphemy laws or explicit Christian establishment seem less unthinkable even when no legislature adopts them immediately.

Recent policy research distinguishes getting an issue onto the agenda from achieving final adoption; these are related but separate stages of political influence. ([Brookings][5])

Useful descriptions include:

* **ideological architect**;
* **concept popularizer**;
* **radicalizer of the policy menu**;
* **movement legitimizer**;
* **historical mythmaker**;
* **rhetorical amplifier**.

Stephen Wolfe is primarily an ideological architect. David Barton is a historical legitimizer. Lance Wallnau is a strategist and popularizer. Joel Webbon is a radicalizing media pastor. An elected official like Dusty Deevers can be both an ideological adopter and a policy converter.

## A practical scoring model

For each figure, we could assign a score from **0 to 5** on eight dimensions:

| Dimension              | What it measures                                     |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Audience reach         | Size and consistency of direct audience              |
| Engagement             | Ability to mobilize, not merely attract views        |
| Network centrality     | Connections to influential actors and networks       |
| Institutional capacity | Durable organizations, schools, churches and media   |
| Elite access           | Direct relationships with political decision-makers  |
| Policy impact          | Observable effect on laws or official actions        |
| Ideological impact     | Influence on vocabulary, doctrine and movement goals |
| Geographic scope       | Local, state, national or transnational influence    |

The total could be shown, but I would **not reduce everything to one number**. A radar chart or profile is more informative because two people can receive the same total through completely different kinds of influence.

## The most useful role taxonomy

Rather than ranking everyone from “most influential” to “least influential,” we can classify them by function:

### Intellectual architects

They formulate the ideas and supply arguments.

Examples: Stephen Wolfe, Doug Wilson.

### Historical and theological legitimizers

They tell audiences that the program is biblical, traditional or authentically American.

Examples: David Barton; Wilson in a different register.

### Institutional builders

They create schools, churches, publishing networks and leadership pipelines.

Examples: Doug Wilson, Franklin Graham, Sean Feucht in movement-organizing terms.

### Brokers and translators

They move ideas from niche theology into broader conservative media and politics.

Examples: Joel Webbon, Lance Wallnau, potentially figures who host cross-network conferences and podcasts.

### Political implementers

They possess state authority and turn ideas into bills, regulations or administrative action.

Examples: Dusty Deevers, Monty Fritts, Micah Beckwith, Mark Baisley.

### Mass mobilizers

They can convene rallies, solicit donations, generate voter activity or command large media audiences.

Examples: Sean Feucht, Franklin Graham, Lance Wallnau.

### Cultural normalizers and amplifiers

They repeat or dramatize ideas, making them familiar to broader audiences without originating or implementing them.

Examples: some reactionary social-media figures such as Lilly Gaddis, Isabella Moody or Carrie Prejean.

A person can occupy several roles simultaneously.

## What the network itself should look like

The eventual project could have four linked layers:

**People:** pastors, authors, officials, influencers, donors and activists.

**Organizations:** churches, ministries, publishers, schools, nonprofits, PACs and media outlets.

**Events and media:** conferences, podcasts, rallies, documentary films and joint statements.

**Policies and ideas:** Christian establishment, abortion abolition, household voting, Seven Mountains, anti-LGBT legislation, anti-pluralism and biblical-law proposals.

Connections should be labeled by type:

* employed by;
* founded;
* published by;
* appeared with;
* endorsed;
* advised;
* funded;
* attended church;
* cited;
* shared board membership;
* introduced related legislation.

A labeled edge is much more useful than a vague assertion that two people are “connected.”

## Evidence standards

Each influence claim should include:

1. **A date**, because networks change.
2. **A source**, preferably primary.
3. **The type of relationship**, not merely “ties to.”
4. **Its strength and frequency.**
5. **Whether the connection implies agreement**, which it often does not.
6. **Whether influence is demonstrated or only plausible.**

We should avoid three common errors:

* equating followers with power;
* equating association with ideological agreement;
* equating ideological similarity with causal influence.

The final product could describe someone like this:

> **Doug Wilson is a high-level ideological and institutional influencer with moderate direct mass reach, strong network centrality in Reformed post-liberal circles, substantial leadership-pipeline capacity and growing indirect access to national officials. His direct authorship of specific public policies is less clearly demonstrated than his role in forming the people and concepts entering politics.**

Or:

> **Lilly Gaddis has comparatively high attention visibility but low demonstrated institutional, elite or policy influence. Her principal role is amplification and normalization rather than intellectual production or policy implementation.**

That kind of profile is much more defensible than simply calling both “influential.”

As you continue listing names, we can build a **living influence inventory** with columns for role, audience, organizations, political access, network connections, policy effects, geographic scope, evidence quality and an overall confidence rating.
