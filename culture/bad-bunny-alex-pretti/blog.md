## Introduction: The Interpretation Comes First

During Bad Bunny’s Super Bowl halftime performance, I watched people react to scenes that, to me, were perfectly legible as representations of Puerto Rican history and contemporary life. Sugarcane workers appeared on screen, and someone immediately suspected an attempt to make America look like a slaveholding villain. Dancers climbed utility poles, and the response was not curiosity about Puerto Rico’s electrical infrastructure but a complaint that Bad Bunny was a hypocrite for using electricity while criticizing power failures. Someone thought they had found a rainbow on a uniform. Apparently there was an agenda hiding there, too.

What stuck with me was not merely that the interpretations were bad. People are allowed to misunderstand a piece of art. What struck me was the speed and familiarity of the responses. It felt as though the interpretations had arrived before the images. The symbol appeared; someone selected a ready-made argument; the argument delivered the familiar moral verdict. One could practically predict the next line: *woke agenda, anti-America, protect the kids, what a hypocrite*. The room was not exactly reasoning from scratch.

That experience brought me back to something much darker: the killing of Alex Pretti by federal agents in Minneapolis on January 24, 2026. I know people whose descriptions of comparable encounters with police used to center on brutality, abuse of power, and whether lethal force was justified. Now, in the case of Pretti, I have heard categorical descriptions like “terrorist” or “would-be assassin” take priority over the encounter itself. The connection I am identifying is that in both settings, an interpretive frame can determine what people notice, which questions they ask, and which evidence they will permit to matter.

There is something deeply off-putting about our relationship with media. Many people can denounce “bias” on command but struggle to specify what the bias is: source selection, framing, missing context, incentives, measurement error, a conflict of interest, or an outright falsehood. “Liberal bias” or “conservative propaganda” becomes a team password rather than an analytical concept. And because saying “I know the media are biased” feels like an accomplishment, it can produce precisely the false confidence that prevents further scrutiny.

My claim is not that every consumer is a helpless puppet, that the public once reasoned perfectly, or that a single algorithm made everyone stupid. It is that **media ecosystems help shape the categories through which events become intelligible, the argumentative scripts people reach for, and the standards by which they accept or reject evidence**. These dynamics can operate through explicit influence campaigns; they can also emerge organically from incentives, identity, repetition, and group participation. Once a script becomes self-protecting, correction is no longer simply a matter of presenting an additional fact.

The argument proceeds in three movements. First, I use the Bad Bunny performance and Pretti’s killing to show how classification can precede analysis. Then I examine how classifications harden into portable causal scripts and, often, self-sealing discourse. Finally, I ask why the contemporary media environment is so good at producing and circulating those scripts. This is less a complaint about individual stupidity than an attempt to explain why confident, low-quality arguments are so easy to manufacture—and so difficult to dislodge.

> **Part I · Two cases — How Frames Shape Interpretation**
>
> The cases differ enormously in stakes. They help isolate the same upstream problem: what we see is partly organized by what we already believe we are looking at.

<a id="bad-bunny-heading"></a>

## 1. Bad Bunny: When Symbolism Becomes a Threat

The political spectacle began before the artist stepped onto the stage. What should have been a fairly ordinary conversation about a globally popular musician became a contest over who was allowed to represent America.

<a id="counterprogramming"></a>

### A counter-show for the “real America”

Bad Bunny’s selection to headline the 2026 Super Bowl halftime show was denounced in some conservative commentary as a “DEI” decision, an offense to patriotism, or evidence that the NFL had surrendered to an ideological agenda. It was an especially ridiculous place to deploy the DEI reflex: Bad Bunny was already a major global commercial star, and the NFL had obvious reasons to appeal to Spanish-speaking audiences. Puerto Ricans are U.S. citizens, and Spanish is not some foreign contaminant of American life. Yet the public argument repeatedly treated his language and politics as though they required a special defense of his Americanness. Contemporary coverage describes the pre-show backlash and its immigration-politics framing ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/feb/08/bad-bunny-super-bowl-trump-maga); [Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/music/story/2026-02-08/bad-bunny-super-bowl-lx-halftime-show-review)).

Turning Point USA responded with an “All-American Halftime Show,” presented as a values-oriented alternative and featuring Kid Rock, among others. There is nothing inherently wrong with mounting an alternative concert. The revealing part was the political logic of the contrast: their show was coded as *faith, family, country, real America*; Bad Bunny’s was treated as foreign, divisive, political, or insufficiently American. This was not an escape from identity politics. It was **identity politics rebranded as the absence of identity politics**. One audience’s symbolism was called tradition; another’s was called an agenda ([Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/entertainment/music/2026/02/08/bad-bunny-super-bowl-halftime-show/); [Deseret News](https://www.deseret.com/sports/2026/02/08/tpusa-halftime-show-review-super-bowl/)).

Not everyone who dislikes Bad Bunny is racist, and not every objection to his music or political views is xenophobic. The problem is with a recognizable *pattern of justification*. When singing in Spanish is treated as disqualifying evidence, when criticism of a president is confused with disloyalty to the country, or when a Puerto Rican artist’s presence on a national stage is presumed to be a diversity concession, the boundary being policed is cultural as well as political. Criticizing a government is not treason; refusing to equate a political movement with the nation is not an assault on patriotism. But the culture-war frame needs an enemy, so ordinary distinctions get flattened.

Notice the reversal. The complaint is “keep politics out of entertainment,” and the proposed solution is an overtly partisan replacement built around political identity. The objection plainly cannot be to politics *as such*. At best it is to a specific politics; at worst it is to a public stage that no longer feels exclusively owned by the complaining group. This is why I think the alternative-show episode matters: it makes visible the machinery of belonging that is usually disguised as neutrality.

<a id="symbols-puerto-rico"></a>

### Sugarcane, blackouts, and the refusal to ask what an image means

The performance began with an image of sugarcane fields and workers. It is hard to represent Puerto Rico without encountering sugarcane: plantation agriculture, colonial extraction, labor, and U.S. economic involvement after 1898 are part of the island’s history. The stage imagery has been described in contemporary coverage as a central part of the show’s Puerto Rican setting ([ABC News](https://abcnews.com/culture/story/super-bowl-2026-bad-bunny-halftime-show/?id=129787954)). Whether every dancer was meant to signify a particular period of colonial labor is a question for closer artistic interpretation. But “they’re trying to make it look like slavery” is not such an interpretation. It is a defensive accusation made before the historical context has even been considered.

And suppose the performance *was* deliberately invoking exploitation. So what? Sugar production across the Americas is tied to colonialism and coercive labor. Acknowledging that history does not require hating Americans, and art is under no obligation to spare its audience historical discomfort. The strange demand is that a Puerto Rican musician should represent Puerto Rican history without depicting the conditions that shaped it, lest someone feel accused.

Later, the utility-pole imagery evoked Puerto Rico’s power failures, a connection made explicit by the performance of “El Apagón” (“The Blackout”). The symbolism is not a private decoding I invented: reviewers also identified the imagery with the island’s prolonged electricity problems ([The New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/the-lede/2026-super-bowl-halftime-show-bad-bunny); [Pitchfork](https://pitchfork.com/news/watch-bad-bunnys-super-bowl-lx-halftime-show/)). When I explained that to the people watching with me, one person responded that Trump would not give Puerto Rico money because its government was corrupt. Another offered the astonishing argument that Bad Bunny was using electricity in America while criticizing electricity failures somewhere else.

The first response changes the subject from a population’s needs to an allegedly defective population or government. Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory; its residents are U.S. citizens. Disaster assistance is not a personal reward for a government having perfect officials. Corruption and fraud in large recovery programs are real risks, which is why governments have inspectors general, audits, controls, and prosecution. The proper policy question is how to deliver assistance competently while limiting misuse—not whether people deserve functioning hospitals, water systems, and power grids. The [Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/usao-pr/pr/fema-deputy-regional-administrator-former-president-cobra-acquisitions-llc-and-another), [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-24-105557.pdf), and [HUD inspector general](https://www.hudoig.gov/sites/default/files/2021-04/HUD%20OIG%20Final%20Report_2019SU008945I.pdf) provide a more complicated picture of fraud, oversight, delay, and administrative barriers than the slogan “they’re corrupt, so no help.”

I am not using this anecdote as evidence that every critic of the show believes the same thing. My point is the *direction of the attention*. A visual cue prompted a ready explanation that defended a preferred political figure, blamed a distant population, and avoided the original question: what happened to Puerto Rico’s infrastructure, and who had the institutional capacity and responsibility to respond? For some people, “president” and “government” have become interchangeable ideas, as if public administration were nothing more than the personal generosity or spite of one man.

<a id="woke-spotting"></a>

### “Woke spotting” as an interpretive filter

The sugarcane reaction and the alleged rainbow on a uniform belong to the same family of responses. I call it **woke spotting**: scanning an ambiguous scene for any cue that can be classified as evidence of the left’s hidden agenda. A field is not a field; it is an accusation. A rainbow cannot simply be a design element; it must be a political signal. A Spanish-language performance is no longer a performance; it is a campaign against America.

There is a difference between interpreting symbolism and hunting for a preselected offense. A serious interpretation asks what the image depicts, what historical or artistic context might explain it, and what alternative readings remain plausible. Woke spotting begins with the presumed existence of an enemy message and treats the image as an opportunity to find it. The method is almost impossible to falsify. If the symbol is ambiguous, ambiguity is offered as proof that the agenda is being smuggled in; if the artist explains the symbol, the explanation becomes an excuse or a cover story.

Several incentives can make that habit feel rewarding. There is the pleasure of spotting something supposedly hidden, the chance to perform loyalty in front of an in-group, and the gratification of placing oneself on the side of an embattled “real America.” No elaborate conspiracy is required for the habit to reproduce itself. A person learns that detecting threat earns status, so more situations get filtered through threat detection. “They’re everywhere” becomes not a conclusion from the evidence but a rule for reading evidence.

This a form of anti-intellectualism. It substitutes a moralized reflex for contextual interpretation. But the more interesting question is why the reflex is so portable. People who have never studied the history of Puerto Rican sugar production somehow have a highly accessible response to a stage full of sugarcane. The explanation cannot end with “these people are stupid.” We need to account for the social availability of the script itself.

<a id="critique-nullifiers"></a>

### Critique-nullifiers: the genius of not answering the question

The electricity objection is a near-perfect example of a **critique-nullifier**: an argument that appears to answer criticism but actually shifts attention from the criticized system to the personal purity of the critic. “He uses electricity, so he cannot criticize power outages” is not an analysis of the electrical grid. It is a cheap rhetorical shortcut: *you participate in the world; therefore you have no standing to criticize the world*.

One version is *tu quoque*, an appeal to the speaker’s alleged hypocrisy. Another is the personal-remediation test: “If he cared, he would fix it himself.” A third collapses scale, as when a person advocating taxes on extraordinary concentrations of wealth is dismissed because they own a car or smartphone. Each confuses distinct questions: whether a proposition is true, whether its speaker acts consistently, and who has the authority or resources to address the underlying problem.

| Pattern | Shortcut | Question it evades |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tu quoque** | You participate in the system, so your criticism is invalid. | Is the criticism true, regardless of the speaker’s consistency? |
| **Personal-remediation test** | If you cared, you would personally repair the problem. | Which institutions actually have responsibility, authority, and capacity? |
| **Scale collapse** | You possess something, so you cannot object to extreme wealth concentration. | What difference do scale, power, and economic structure make? |

An artist can raise awareness, donate, organize, and apply public pressure without personally rebuilding a power grid. Electricity restoration requires utilities, engineers, contractors, regulation, procurement, long-term capital, and a functioning state. “Why doesn’t he fix it himself?” is like telling someone who complains about a collapsing bridge to go pour concrete. The argument sounds tough-minded only because it has quietly substituted an individual consumer for the institution being criticized.

The billionaire version does the same thing. The serious claim concerns concentrations of power, rent-seeking, taxation, political influence, or distribution. “But you have a car” does not rebut any of those mechanisms; it merely puts the critic on trial. It has the shape of a reply without doing the work of one. And because the maneuver ends a conversation quickly, it spreads easily.

There is an important qualification. I will criticize double standards in the next paragraph, and that is not the same as claiming hypocrisy refutes an unrelated proposition. Hypocrisy can be relevant to whether someone sincerely or consistently applies a professed standard; it does not, by itself, show that a factual criticism is false. If a person condemns sexualized entertainment on principle, we may reasonably ask whether they apply that standard to their favored performers. But even a genuine inconsistency would not settle the quality of Bad Bunny’s music or the truth of a claim about Puerto Rico’s grid.

The full premises, hidden assumptions, and critical questions for these three patterns are preserved in [Appendix A](#appendix-argument-schemes). They are useful for close analysis, but the essay does not need a long formal derivation in the middle of a living-room story.

<a id="children-and-double-standards"></a>

### “Protect the children,” selectively

The other predictable objection was that Bad Bunny’s music or performance was too vulgar for children. It is perfectly legitimate to criticize the content of a televised performance, including sexually suggestive material. But “think of the children” can also function as a moral-panic wrapper around a different discomfort. When the same conversation complains about Spanish lyrics, national loyalty, and presumed DEI contamination, the children argument no longer arrives in isolation.

The contrast with Turning Point USA’s advertised family-values alternative, headlined by Kid Rock, is instructive. Kid Rock’s broader catalogue and public persona are not an obvious template for children’s entertainment; press coverage before the show noted the contradiction ([San Francisco Chronicle](https://www.sfchronicle.com/super-bowl-lx/article/kid-rock-backlash-underage-21341430.php)). That comparison does not prove that every concern about explicit lyrics was insincere. It does raise a fair question about consistent standards. If vulgarity is the criterion, why does ideology so reliably alter which artists receive scrutiny?

Here is the broader pattern: criticisms that sound universal—patriotism, family values, keeping politics out of sports—can be applied in a highly partisan fashion. When someone else asserts an identity, it becomes identity politics. When your side asserts one, it becomes tradition. When someone else makes art political, that is propaganda. When your side organizes a competing political spectacle, it is wholesome entertainment. This is not subtle. It is the culture war performing its own denial.

<a id="living-room-to-feed"></a>

### From the living room to the feed

Some of the people watching with me were also looking at their phones as commentary circulated. That matters as an observation, though it cannot by itself establish a causal chain from any particular post to any particular remark. I was witnessing the offline end of a process I had already seen repeatedly online: a high-profile event, an immediate stock of outraged interpretations, and a group competing to discover—or recite—the next incriminating detail.

There are at least two possible routes to this outcome. In one, political organizations, influencers, paid networks, or other actors actively seed and promote a frame. In the other, no central organizer is necessary. People with similar identities watch similar content, confront similar incentives, and independently converge on similar rhetorical moves. A synchronized interpretive swarm can look orchestrated even when much of it is emergent. Both routes can also interact: a deliberately introduced frame becomes something ordinary people sincerely elaborate in their own words.

These arguments are *socially functional*. They reaffirm belonging, identify a villain, supply moral certainty, and end a difficult conversation without resolving it. What I saw was not merely the absence of independent thought; it was the presence of a ready-made substitute. The next case shows what happens when that substitution concerns life, death, and public authority rather than a halftime spectacle.

<a id="pretti-heading"></a>

## 2. Alex Pretti: When Classification Governs Evidence

The stakes change dramatically here. The question is no longer what a cultural image symbolizes, but how people decide whether a killing by the state was justified—and whether they allow observed evidence to challenge the categories they have already accepted.

<a id="pretti-record"></a>

### Beginning with the record

On January 24, 2026, federal immigration agents shot and killed Alex Pretti, a 37-year-old intensive-care nurse, in Minneapolis. Multiple bystander videos and independent visual analyses described Pretti holding a phone during the encounter, being restrained by agents, and an agent removing a firearm from his person immediately before the first shots. Reporting described approximately ten shots in a matter of seconds ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/videos-minneapolis-shooting-witnesses/); [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/investigations/2026/01/25/minneapolis-shooting-video-gun/)). The video analysis also has a crucial limit: it cannot establish what each shooting agent perceived at the instant they fired. That uncertainty deserves investigation, not permission to ignore what the video does show.

Initial federal statements describing the incident as a violent threat came under substantial scrutiny. Independent analyses found significant tensions between some official claims and the available footage ([FactCheck.org](https://www.factcheck.org/2026/01/video-analyses-at-odds-with-dhs-statements-on-minneapolis-shooting/); [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/861a0d8f3ee182f3b5909b3613900e2e)). Later dispute over access to investigative evidence made the institutions responsible for establishing the facts part of the story, too ([Hennepin County Attorney’s Office report, October 2026](https://www.hennepinattorney.org/-/media/cao/news/2026/October/hcao-report.pdf)).

I regard the shooting as an appalling instance of state violence. Others have called it justified force; still others reached for labels such as “terrorist” or “would-be assassin.” It is important to keep different types of claims separate. A medical examiner’s classification of a death as *homicide* is not a court’s determination that *murder* occurred. A political description of a person as a threat is not proof that lethal force was legally justified. And the fact that Pretti carried a firearm does not establish whether he posed an imminent threat at the moment of the shooting. The relevant legal, factual, and moral questions intersect, but they are not interchangeable.

That last distinction is central. The most consequential public argument was not only about an event sequence; it was about which *kind* of event people thought they were seeing. Was it an unjustified killing by law enforcement? A justified response to a perceived imminent threat? An encounter to be interpreted primarily through the category of terrorism? Once one of these descriptions is fixed in advance, facts tend to be promoted or demoted according to their usefulness to that description.

<a id="pretti-classification"></a>

### How a label changes the question

Suppose someone enters the discussion with the classification “terrorist.” That word does not merely record a neutral observation. It brings a story with it: the person is dangerous, the agents represent order, state force is presumptively protective, and criticism of that force may amount to siding with enemies. Evidence that might have been decisive under a use-of-force question—such as when a weapon was removed, or whether the person was pinned—can become secondary to the category. Instead of asking *Was shooting justified at that moment?*, the argument becomes *What are we supposed to do about people like him?*

The inverse danger also exists. A person who begins with a settled conclusion of deliberate murder can fail to distinguish what is visible from what remains unknown about perception, intent, and applicable legal standards. The fact that two sides can misuse categories does not mean the evidence is evenly balanced or every interpretation equally plausible. It means the analysis should show where the differences actually lie, instead of merely announcing that everyone has their own narrative.

There are several separate disputes hiding inside a single shouted argument. What happened physically? Which witnesses and records are reliable? What law governs force in this encounter? Which actions satisfy the relevant standard? And who has the authority to characterize the event in public? These questions require different kinds of evidence. A source can be wrong about one without being wrong about all of them. A political label cannot do the work of a timeline, and a timeline cannot, without legal and normative analysis, answer every question about culpability.

| Level | What is being disputed? | What would help? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Observation** | The sequence of actions, timing, visible objects, and shots. | Verified video, corroborated accounts, physical evidence, a consistent timeline. |
| **Interpretation** | Threat perception, causal sequence, and what the observations imply. | Independent reconstruction, competing explanations, explicit assumptions. |
| **Norm and law** | The standard for lawful and justified force, responsibility, and accountability. | Applicable law, credible investigation, transparent reasoning. |
| **Authority** | Who may certify the observations and whose account is dismissed in advance. | Independent review, traceable evidence, source-specific scrutiny. |

This is where my experience of talking to people about Pretti becomes more revealing than another argument over a headline. Someone can say, “Show me evidence,” while having already ruled out the very institutions and recording methods that could supply it. They are not merely holding a different conclusion. They may be operating with a different mechanism for deciding what a conclusion is allowed to rest on.

<a id="evidence-and-models"></a>

### The evidence does not interpret itself

“Evidence” sounds like a simple relationship: observation *E* supports hypothesis *H*. But an observation becomes evidence in relation to a question, a description of what was observed, background assumptions, and rival explanations. A video is a recording; what the video establishes depends on its authenticity, angle, sequence, and the proposition being tested. This does *not* mean facts are arbitrary or reality is whatever your preferred pundit says it is. It means we need to be explicit about the bridge from a fact to a conclusion.

Bayesian reasoning makes the distinction unusually clear. Bayes’ rule tells us how to update probabilities once a set of hypotheses, observations, priors, and likelihoods has been specified. It does not, by itself, supply the correct description of the event, force us to consider a neglected alternative, or tell us whether an official statement is a reliable measurement. Those are questions of model construction and evidence evaluation that come *before* the arithmetic. The full equation and an expanded diagnostic are in [Appendix B](#appendix-bayes).

Imagine two observers looking at the same video analysis. One considers it independently corroborated evidence about the timing of disarmament. The other treats the very fact that mainstream outlets agree as evidence of coordinated manipulation. They have not merely assigned different weights to the same input. They disagree about *which process generated the input* and *which sources are trustworthy*. If every new observation is reclassified as deception whenever it conflicts with a preferred account, ordinary updating becomes almost impossible.

Or consider a shift of hypothesis. If the claim under investigation is *Was lethal force justified at the moment shots were fired?*, the sequence surrounding disarmament is highly relevant. If the question silently changes to *Was Pretti generally hostile to federal enforcement?*, evidence about the moment of shooting can be pushed aside. But the second question does not answer the first. Changing the target is not a rebuttal; it is a change of subject with the same political costume.

Different prior assumptions can create reasonable disagreement, and source reliability should be questioned. The mistake is not having priors or mistrusting an institution. It is refusing to articulate the conditions under which the evidence would count against your position. A disciplined skeptic asks which particular record is unreliable and why; an epistemically captured partisan may declare an entire class of counterevidence impossible by definition.

<a id="trust-and-epistemic-authority"></a>

### When trust becomes a hidden variable

Pretti’s case also demonstrates the difference between skepticism and suspicion as an identity. Suspicion can be justified: officials can misstate events, journalists can miss context, and viral images can be manipulated. Indeed, fact-checkers documented an altered image related to the shooting, another reason to prefer independently verified video and corroborating evidence over a dramatic screenshot ([Reuters fact check](https://www.reuters.com/fact-check/verified-footage-showing-alex-prettis-death-edited-alter-object-hand-2026-01-27/)). But skepticism that cannot distinguish between one demonstrated error and the total unreliability of every unwelcome source is not very good skepticism.

What matters is the structure of trust. Does a person trust a source because it has verifiable methods, corrections, and independently checkable evidence? Or do they trust it because it can be counted on to validate the side they already identify with? Do they hold government statements to the same standard as news footage? Do they acknowledge when an initial claim is contradicted by later evidence? Do they distinguish an eyewitness account from its political interpretation? These questions expose the epistemic rules under which each person is operating.

I am not suggesting that disagreement automatically proves “epistemic capture.” That conclusion requires evidence of a pattern: sources disqualified in advance, changing standards, rival explanations excluded without consideration, and counterexamples transformed into proof that the conspiracy goes deeper. But the Pretti dispute illustrates how a controversy can shift from contesting facts to contesting who is even entitled to produce facts. Once that happens, a request for “more evidence” may become ceremonial. The requested evidence has nowhere to go.

The performance and the killing are not analogous in moral weight. Their shared lesson concerns the infrastructure of interpretation: categories guide attention; attention selects evidence; and some categories defend themselves against correction. The next step is to explain why those categories often come packaged as the same familiar arguments, ready for use before anyone has looked carefully at the event.

> **Part II · The mechanism — From Interpretation to Self-Sealing Discourse**
>
> The problem is not that people have frames. Nobody reasons without them. The problem begins when familiar frames become scripts that predetermine the answer and disqualify correction.

<a id="scripts-heading"></a>

## 3. Why the Same Arguments Keep Appearing

Across very different political events, public reactions tend to draw on a limited library of compressed explanations. I think of these as **causal scripts**: portable stories that identify the villain, explain the event, tell the audience what to feel, and specify which response marks them as a member of the group.

<a id="scripts-as-shortcuts"></a>

### A library of pre-made interpretations

Consider how often the same phrases appear in different settings: “they’re indoctrinating kids,” “this is anti-American,” “they want to replace us,” “you’re a hypocrite,” “just comply with the law,” “the media are covering it up.” Some are claims, some are commands, and some are not even meaningful arguments until their terms are specified. Their shared function is to compress a difficult question into an immediately recognizable moral arrangement: *us, them, victim, threat, outrage, closure*.

A causal script is not necessarily false. A person can reasonably object to indoctrination, hypocrisy, criminal violence, or inaccurate reporting. The problem arises when a script substitutes for investigation. “Protect children” tells us nothing about whether a particular performance was inappropriate. “Respect the law” does not tell us whether officers used lawful force. “The media are biased” does not tell us which parts of a report are wrong. These phrases can be true in some contexts and absurd in others; the script works politically because it suppresses the need to decide which context we are in.

The Bad Bunny examples showed this at the level of art and identity. Sugarcane activated the *anti-woke accusation* script; power lines activated the *you’re a hypocrite* script; Spanish and patriotism activated the *enemy of the nation* script. In the Pretti case, labels like “terrorist” or maxims such as “just comply” can move attention away from a precise use-of-force sequence toward a preexisting moral story about enemies and order. The same rhetorical machinery can be applied to almost anything. What changes is the object being shoved into it.

These scripts are efficient for three reasons. They are **cognitively cheap**: remembering an argument template is easier than investigating a new case. They are **socially legible**: the group knows immediately whose side you are on. And they are **emotionally gratifying**: the script offers certainty and moral superiority before the boring work of checking has begun. This helps explain why people can sound remarkably confident while saying something structurally identical to the last ten people who passed the same clip around.

This simply is Brain Rot and I am not interested in pretending it is a model of civic virtue. The important point is that a communications environment can make these responses available, rewarding, and habitual. Repetition eventually changes what feels like an obvious interpretation. A person no longer experiences the script as a script. It feels like common sense.

<a id="self-sealing-logic"></a>

### Bubbles omit; echo chambers discredit

Here the distinction made by philosopher [C. Thi Nguyen](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/episteme/article/echo-chambers-and-epistemic-bubbles/5D4AC3A808C538E17C50A7C09EC706F0) becomes useful. An *epistemic bubble* leaves out relevant voices, often without its members recognizing the omission. An *echo chamber*, in Nguyen’s account, does something more active: it undermines trust in outsiders and gives members reasons to dismiss outside testimony. A bubble may be corrected by introducing missing information. An echo chamber has already prepared its members to reject that intervention.

Endre Begby’s discussion of *evidential preemption* adds another component. A person can be taught in advance how to classify possible counterevidence: contrary reporting is staged, critical experts are corrupted, firsthand footage is selectively edited, defections are proof of betrayal. The person encounters the disconfirming fact and still feels vindicated, because the script anticipated the fact’s arrival. The apparent strength of the worldview is partly a trick of its design: every possible outcome has been assigned a loyalty-preserving interpretation ([Begby, draft paper](https://endrebegby.synthasite.com/resources/Evidential%20Preemption%2C%20web%20draft.pdf)).

That is the point at which a frame becomes **self-sealing**. It is not just a preference for one interpretation; it is a rule specifying that rival interpretations are illegitimate before they are evaluated. The phrase “do your own research” becomes absurd when every permissible source has already been decided by group identity. The person claims independence while outsourcing the conditions of belief to a closed network of trusted authorities.

Still, we should not diagnose echo chambers every time people refuse a weak argument. A good thinker should reject weak evidence. The diagnostic is whether reasons for rejection are specific, stable, and applicable to favored sources too. Does the person point to an actual flaw in the video, timeline, inference, or institution? Or does every inconvenient item receive the same generic verdict—fake, woke, deep state, enemy propaganda—regardless of its content?

<a id="participation-and-reward"></a>

### Why participation hardens the script

People do not merely receive interpretations from the top. They adapt, circulate, and defend them. One person supplies the outrage; another finds a clip; another makes a joke; another adds a story about an alleged cover-up. What began as a media frame becomes a community production. This is related to the participatory dynamics studied by [Kate Starbird](https://www.cip.uw.edu/2021/05/26/participatory-disinformation-kate-starbird/): misleading narratives can develop through feedback among influential accounts, political actors, and ordinary participants, including participants who sincerely believe what they are spreading.

Not every mistaken interpretation is *disinformation*. The useful distinction from Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan is between misinformation (false information shared without necessarily intending harm), disinformation (false information deliberately produced or shared to cause harm), and malinformation (genuine information deployed in a harmful way). These categories can be difficult to apply to ambiguous images or slanted commentary; selective framing may be misleading without creating a false factual claim. Precision matters here. If we call every interpretation we dislike “disinformation,” we have merely invented another team password ([Council of Europe, *Information Disorder*](https://edoc.coe.int/en/media/7495-information-disorder-toward-an-interdisciplinary-framework-for-research-and-policy-making.html)).

A stronger description is that a frame can be *participatory* and *self-reinforcing* without each participant intending to deceive. A clip gets posted with a suspicious caption; commenters supply more suspicious readings; influencers quote the comments as proof of widespread concern; others encounter the commentary rather than the original footage. An event generates a reaction, the reaction generates content, and soon the existence of all that content is mistaken for independent confirmation of the original story.

In my living room, nobody needed to receive a formal propaganda brief. They had a cultural vocabulary, an internet connection, and plenty of opportunities to show one another that they understood the preferred story. Their reactions were not random. But this remains an inference about an interpretive environment, not proof of a coordinated operation targeting that gathering. Later I will describe what would count as stronger evidence for that claim.

<a id="what-would-change-your-mind"></a>

### What, exactly, would change your mind?

This is the question that cuts through much of the performance: **what observation would actually change your view?** If Bad Bunny’s power-line imagery had not been associated with “El Apagón,” would that matter to your interpretation? If an independent reconstruction of the Pretti shooting contradicted the initial official description, what would it do to your assessment? If your preferred performer made the same allegedly inappropriate artistic choices, would you apply the same standard?

In a genuine disagreement, people may not have an immediate answer, but they can usually describe the kind of evidence, explanation, or changed condition that would move them. They can separate the questions they care about and acknowledge what they do not know. Someone may reasonably say, “I would need a second angle,” or “I trust the video but need an expert assessment of the legal standard.” That is a testable condition for revision.

The self-sealing answer is different. It offers no possible revision, or answers each proposed test with another layer of the same script: the second angle would be fake, the independent investigator would be bought, the witness would be an activist, the source would be part of a conspiracy. Once a position is insulated this way, additional information can increase conviction rather than improve accuracy. The person is no longer arguing only about a proposition. They are defending a mechanism designed to keep the proposition safe.

The social consequences extend beyond unpleasant conversations. If political groups lack shared standards for determining what happened, they may also lack shared standards for determining what government should do. Compromise can become treason, restraint becomes weakness, and institutional procedures appear legitimate only when they produce the preferred result. The system is pulled toward stalemate or escalation: people cannot negotiate over contested facts when they deny one another the authority to establish any facts at all.

We have described the psychological and conversational machinery. That still leaves a systemic question: why are the same scripts so abundant, memorable, and easy to circulate? To answer that, we need to look beyond individual beliefs toward the processes that select which communications become visible in the first place.

## 4. From Luhmann’s Selectors to Computational Propaganda

If you have ever wondered why so much political commentary sounds like a slight rearrangement of what you heard yesterday, selection is a good place to begin. We encounter neither an unfiltered world nor a random sample of available interpretations.

<a id="luhmann-selection"></a>

### Luhmann: communication is already selective

In *The Reality of the Mass Media*, Niklas Luhmann treats mass media not as a transparent window onto reality but as a system that must continually select from more possible events and communications than it can present. His account of news selection pays attention to criteria such as surprise, conflict, quantities, local relevance, norm violations, moral judgments, persons, and opinions. These are not necessarily a secret plan by journalists. They are mechanisms by which something becomes a media event while countless other things do not ([Luhmann, *The Reality of the Mass Media*](https://dissent.is/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2025/11/massenmedienrealitaetluhmann.pdf)).

There is a related but distinct idea in Luhmann’s general theory of communication: communication involves selections of information, utterance, and understanding. In ordinary terms, there is what is communicated, how it is communicated, and how it is taken up as communication. I mention the distinction because it is easy to jumble his levels together and call every choice a “selector.” For this essay, the point is more specific: at every stage, possibilities are reduced, and the reductions shape the reality that becomes available for subsequent communication ([Luhmann, “What Is Communication?”](https://www.unisalento.it/documents/20152/2157613/LUHMANN-What-is-Communication.pdf/)).

Now connect that insight back to the two cases. A politically charged halftime show is selected for conflict, identity, moral evaluation, and novelty. A fatal shooting generates attention through violence, public authority, moral stakes, and competing accounts. The next communication is often not the original event but someone’s response to it. Commentary on commentary becomes newsworthy because people are now arguing, and the existence of the argument becomes a fresh event. The system begins feeding on its own reactions.

None of this entails that every selection is dishonest. Any account of an event must omit something. The critical questions are *what selection criteria are operating, what they reliably exclude, and what kinds of interpretations they reward*. Once a system repeatedly selects conflict and moral threat, viewers can begin to experience those features as the underlying character of ordinary public life.

<a id="platform-selection"></a>

### From editorial selection to continuous ranking

The platform era adds a crucial infrastructure. Instead of a comparatively limited set of editorial outlets deciding which stories to publish and highlight, recommender systems continuously rank enormous volumes of candidate content. Creators see which posts receive attention and adapt. Users click, pause, reply, follow, share, and watch; those behaviors become further signals for distribution. Selection is not a one-time editorial act but an ongoing feedback process. Researchers have studied how recommendation and network structures can contribute to the diffusion of misinformation, though their effects vary across platforms, populations, and designs ([research on recommender-driven diffusion](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3616088); [research on misinformation cascades](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4725489/)).

On TikTok, discovery heavily depends on recommendation and viewer-response signals; a short political story can be formatted to maximize attention and repeated viewing. On X, feeds, replies, quote-posts, and network relationships create different pathways for reactive content to spread. Neither platform has a single, unchanging ranking criterion, and we should not pretend to know the precise weight of every signal. X has made versions of recommendation-system code public, which makes some pipeline elements more inspectable than the general mythology of “the algorithm” suggests ([X/Twitter recommendation code](https://github.com/twitter/the-algorithm)).

What matters for my argument is not an assertion that outrage always wins. It is that **content producers operate in an environment where attention and engagement are continuously measured, and their future output can adapt to those measurements**. A nuanced explanation of Puerto Rican recovery policy demands patience. “WOKE NFL HATES AMERICA” is recognizable, antagonistic, easy to repeat, and immediately usable in a group setting. An intricate dispute over probable cause and the legal standards for lethal force asks people to distinguish multiple questions. “Terrorist” arrives as a complete moral story in one word. There is a strong incentive to package political communication for instant uptake.

This is an adaptation of Luhmann, not a claim that he predicted TikTok or that recommender algorithms are identical to his account of functional social systems. His framework helps name the selection problem; contemporary platform research supplies the empirical mechanisms. When these are collapsed into one theory, the essay can sound impressive while becoming less precise. They work better together when we keep their contributions distinct.

<a id="influencers-and-roles"></a>

### Influencers are more than advertisements with faces

Elizabeth Dubois and Louise Stahl’s [*Influencers and Elections*](https://www.polcommtech.com/research/influencers-and-elections) report offers an important bridge between institutional politics and platform-native communication. An influencer may act as an advertiser, endorser, campaign volunteer, media outlet, journalist, lobbyist, or intermediary in data-driven political communication. These roles can overlap within a stable public persona. The audience encounters a creator who “always talks like this,” while the creator’s actual function may shift between commentary, promotion, reporting, and organized campaigning.

That blending matters because a trusted creator can introduce an interpretation into a community in the voice of someone familiar rather than in the official language of a campaign. Political messaging may not appear as messaging. A viewer hears a casual observation, a joke, or an indignant clip breakdown from somebody they follow. The rhetorical frame arrives already accompanied by a trust relationship. And once hundreds of creators apply slight variations to the same subject, the variation itself can look like independent corroboration.

Funding can increase the capacity to test and distribute these messages, sometimes under opaque sponsorship arrangements. Money buys production, staffing, paid placement, analytics, multiple accounts, creator relationships, and additional attempts to produce a post that travels. It does not guarantee persuasion. It creates more opportunities to pass through the system’s selection machinery. Research and reporting on influencer campaigning, transparency gaps, and political advertising make this a concrete institutional concern, not merely a metaphor ([University of Ottawa](https://www.uottawa.ca/en/news-all/new-report-examining-impact-influencers-elections); [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/dark-money-hit-record-high-19-billion-2024-federal-races)).

This is where adapting Luhmann becomes particularly useful. A communicator’s *role* shapes what counts as a successful performance: a reporter seeks credibility and a publishable story, an advertiser seeks reach or conversion, an activist seeks mobilization, a provocateur seeks reaction. These are my analytic categories, not a claim that Luhmann assigns each influencer a formal binary code. An individual can shift functions while maintaining one recognizable voice. The resulting communication can be difficult for audiences to classify correctly, especially when sponsorship or coordination is undisclosed.

<a id="the-amplification-pipeline"></a>

### A pipeline, not a master conspiracy

One way to connect these theories is to trace a possible communication pipeline. An actor identifies a goal and audience; producers choose an emotionally legible “case”; accounts publish variations; the platform allocates exposure based on its own ranking signals; influencers and audiences repeat or remix the most resonant versions; and news outlets or politicians may then cite the online reaction as a story in its own right. Finally, engagement data feed back into the next round of production. The process can be deliberately coordinated, partly coordinated, or largely spontaneous.

1. **Select the issue.** Pick a salient event, goal, and audience; assign it a familiar conflict or grievance.
2. **Package the frame.** Turn complexity into clips, labels, accusations, or a short causal story.
3. **Seed and distribute.** Publish through accounts, influencers, advertising, and ordinary networks.
4. **Reinforce through feedback.** Attention, copying, discussion, and ranking promote some variants over others.
5. **Convert attention into apparent authority.** “Everyone is talking about it” becomes a substitute for independently checking the original claim.

The fifth step is especially pernicious. A vague allegation first appears as a post. People react to it. A commentator reports that people are outraged. Another commentator uses the outrage to argue that a real scandal exists. Soon there is abundant evidence of *conversation* but little additional evidence of the original allegation. Information has been laundered through repetition. The audience is no longer encountering the event alone; it is encountering the social proof attached to the event.

Computational propaganda research investigates the combination of automation, data, organized actors, and social platforms used to influence public opinion and behavior ([Oxford Internet Institute](https://navigator.oii.ox.ac.uk/what-is-comprop/)). But not every online cascade is an operation, and not every amplified political opinion is propaganda. That is why I want to preserve two routes through this machinery: **coordination by actors** and **coordination by shared incentives**. The outputs can resemble one another even when their causes differ. The distinction should be investigated, not assumed away because the resulting takes all seem suspiciously similar.

<a id="causal-attribution"></a>

### From Engineered Narratives to Everyday Discourse

One of the more disturbing things I've noticed is how quickly narratives circulating through media ecosystems seem to materialize in ordinary conversation. This became particularly apparent to me during the war in Gaza. I remember reading investigative reporting about Israeli state-linked efforts to influence American public opinion, including campaigns involving anti-Muslim messaging and attempts to shape discourse surrounding the conflict ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/article/2024/jun/24/israel-fund-us-university-protest-gaza-antisemitism); [Middle East Monitor](https://www.middleeastmonitor.com/20240717-israeli-ministry-funding-multi-million-dollar-disinformation-campaign-spreading-islamophobia/)). Not long afterward, I began noticing a substantial increase in hostile, sometimes explicitly dehumanizing rhetoric toward Muslims among politically conservative people in my own community.

What struck me was not simply the hostility itself, which is hardly a new phenomenon, but the apparent convergence of rhetoric. People who had previously shown little interest in these subjects suddenly seemed to have remarkably similar things to say. Familiar accusations, moral justifications, and representations of an entire population as inherently threatening began appearing in otherwise ordinary conversations. It was fascinating and deeply disturbing to watch. It felt almost like observing a media narrative propagate through a social network in real time.

Now, I cannot demonstrate that the particular campaigns described in those reports directly produced the rhetoric I encountered. Establishing that connection would require tracing the messages through specific accounts, influencers, platforms, and interpersonal networks. The temporal relationship alone cannot identify a causal pathway, and major events surrounding the war undoubtedly contributed to changes in public discourse. But that limitation should not obscure what makes the observation significant.

We have documented reporting about organized attempts to shape public perceptions, established research describing how political messaging travels through digital networks, and firsthand observations of recognizable narratives appearing in everyday social interactions. These are not equivalent forms of evidence, nor do they establish a complete causal chain. But neither are they unrelated curiosities. Together, they provide compelling grounds to investigate whether deliberate messaging efforts are contributing to the interpretive frameworks that ordinary people adopt and reproduce.

More importantly, the influence of propaganda does not depend on everyone knowingly participating in a coordinated campaign. That is precisely what makes contemporary networked propaganda so effective. A message can originate with an interested political actor, circulate through influencers and algorithmic recommendation systems, acquire credibility through repetition and social endorsement, and eventually emerge from an ordinary person's mouth as though it were an independently formed conclusion. By that point, the original source may be completely invisible. The person repeating the narrative need not be dishonest, malicious, or even particularly aware of the political interests their argument might serve.

This is also why the distinction between **deliberate coordination and organic amplification** matters. A coordinated campaign can exploit existing prejudices without creating them. Algorithms can amplify narratives without being explicitly programmed to promote a particular political ideology. Ordinary users can reproduce propaganda without recognizing it as propaganda. These processes interact, making it entirely possible for a deliberately introduced frame to circulate through largely spontaneous social behavior. What begins as strategic messaging can become a self-sustaining pattern of interpretation.

The analytical challenge is therefore not simply to identify who originated a message, but to understand how particular narratives become so widely available that people begin reaching for them almost automatically. Evidence of repeated rhetorical templates, synchronized bursts of engagement, recurring source material, and cross-platform diffusion can help reconstruct that process. Establishing responsibility for a specific campaign requires additional evidence, particularly concerning funding, coordination, and identifiable transmission pathways. But the inability to reconstruct every link should not prevent us from recognizing the broader mechanisms at work.

My observation does not prove that a specific Israeli influence operation caused the hostility I witnessed. I am not claiming that it does. What it illustrates is something I find no less concerning: political narratives can move from organized messaging environments into everyday discourse, where they are reproduced as ordinary common sense. The boundary between propaganda and personal conviction becomes increasingly difficult to distinguish—not necessarily because people are being directly instructed what to believe, but because the information environments in which they form their beliefs are themselves subject to systematic manipulation.

**The most effective propaganda does not need to sound like propaganda. It only needs to become the language people naturally reach for when explaining the world.**

<a id="theoretical-boundaries"></a>

### What these theories do—and do not—explain

Luhmann helps explain why communication is selected rather than transparently transmitted. Platform research helps explain the infrastructure through which that selection is continuously revised. Dubois and Stahl clarify the changing roles of political influencers. Starbird and information-disorder research illuminate the participation of audiences in circulating and elaborating narratives. Nguyen and Begby help explain how some interpretive communities become resistant to correction. These are compatible perspectives, not interchangeable theories, and none by itself proves that a given person’s reaction was caused by a specific media actor.

The combined explanation is narrower, and therefore more useful, than “the media brainwashed everyone.” Particular environments can make some ways of interpreting events more visible, memorable, socially rewarding, and resistant to revision. Those conditions do not eliminate human agency. They help explain why the exercise of that agency so often arrives in prefab form.

<a id="conclusion-heading"></a>

## 5. What Happens When Evidence Stops Working?

I began with a halftime performance because the absurdity was easy to see. A sugarcane field becomes a coded attack on America. A power-line scene becomes evidence that an artist is a hypocrite for using electricity. A manufactured distinction between “our patriotic show” and “their political show” is repeated with a straight face. It is tempting to laugh at that kind of thing, and often I do. But the laughter stops carrying much explanatory weight when the same habits of classification appear in arguments about an actual killing by the state.

Alex Pretti’s death forces a more serious version of the problem. If an accusation such as “terrorist” can be installed before the available sequence of events is examined, subsequent facts will be pressured to fit the accusation. If a video is dismissed categorically because the wrong kind of source shared it, if legal standards are confused with moral labels, and if any independent review is discredited in advance, the public dispute no longer concerns only the shooting. It concerns the very rules that allow a disputed event to be investigated.

This is why I resist treating media literacy as the simple skill of identifying that “the other side” is biased. That is the most basic observation imaginable. Nearly anyone can say it, and millions of people say it every day. The harder task is to describe the actual mechanisms: which facts have been selected, how a claim has been framed, what causal story links evidence to conclusion, which sources deserve calibrated trust, and which incentives shaped the communication before it reached you.

The media structures I have discussed make that harder. They supply people with convenient scripts, reward confident performance, and recirculate reactions until a claim seems independently confirmed by the volume of its repetition. Some of that is organized manipulation; much of it is ordinary participation under conditions that make simplification and hostility pay. The result is not simply an uninformed public. It is an environment in which **performing certainty can be more socially valuable than earning accuracy**.

The distinction between skepticism and cynicism matters here. Healthy skepticism asks for provenance, alternative explanations, independent checks, and standards that apply consistently to allies and opponents. Cynicism says everybody lies, all institutions are corrupt, and therefore only my side can be trusted. Cynicism looks sophisticated because it has learned the language of media criticism. In practice, it is just another shortcut past the work.

For my own part, I think there are a few questions worth carrying into the next argument: What exactly was observed, and what am I inferring? Am I evaluating a claim or merely judging the person who said it? Which alternative explanation have I seriously considered? Would I apply this standard if the political identities were reversed? What would make me revise my conclusion? And if I suspect coordination or propaganda, what evidence do I actually have for the causal claim?

These are not magic defenses. Nobody can inspect every source, escape every social influence, or reason without a frame. But we can stop mistaking a familiar frame for proof. We can stop treating repetition as corroboration. We can distinguish the justified distrust of a particular claim from the reflexive dismissal of entire categories of evidence. Above all, we can retain the possibility of changing our minds without treating it as a humiliating surrender of identity.

The problem is not only that people believe nonsense. It is that too much of our public conversation now provides sophisticated social rewards for refusing to find out whether the nonsense is true. The first act of resistance is not adopting a better slogan. It is recovering the difference between *having a response* and *having an argument*.

## Appendix A. Formal Argumentation Schemes

The main text identifies three critique-nullifiers. What follows preserves the fuller Walton-style reconstructions from the original draft: premises, hidden assumptions, and critical questions. These are *analytical reconstructions* of recurring arguments, not assertions that every person who uses one of these responses consciously endorses every premise. In particular, an argument from hypocrisy may legitimately bear on credibility or consistency without automatically refuting the underlying claim.

### Full scheme: Argument from hypocrisy / inconsistent commitment

**Scheme 1: Argument from Hypocrisy / Inconsistent Commitment (Tu quoque)**

- **P1.** Speaker S asserts/advocates claim (C) (often a critique of system (X)).
- **P2.** S’s actions (A) are inconsistent with (C) (S participates in (X), benefits from (X), or fails to act in line with (C)).
- **C.** Therefore, (C) should be rejected / S’s critique is not credible / S has no standing to criticize.

- Hidden assumptions (common)

  - **A1.** If S does not fully comply with (C), then (C) is false or unjustified.
  - **A2.** Participation in (X) is voluntary and avoidable (so participation implies endorsement).
  - **A3.** Moral standing is a necessary condition for the truth/acceptability of (C).
  - **A4.** The only legitimate way to express commitment to (C) is through personal behavioral conformity (not speech, art, organization, voting, etc.).
  - **A5.** S has sufficient power/resources to avoid (X) or solve the criticized harm.
- Critical Questions

  1. **CQ1 (Relevance):** Does S’s inconsistency, if real, actually bear on the **truth** or **merits** of (C), or only on S’s character/credibility?
  2. **CQ2 (Degree):** How strong is the alleged inconsistency—full contradiction, partial compromise, or trivial participation?
  3. **CQ3 (Voluntariness):** Is participation in (X) meaningfully **optional**, or is it a constraint of living in society (e.g., using electricity, money, roads)?
  4. **CQ4 (Feasibility):** Could S reasonably comply with (C) given structural constraints, costs, risks, or limited alternatives?
  5. **CQ5 (Burden shift):** Is the argument improperly shifting the burden from evaluating (C) to judging S’s purity?
  6. **CQ6 (Independence):** Are there independent reasons/evidence supporting (C) that remain even if S is inconsistent?
  7. **CQ7 (Double standard):** Is this purity standard applied consistently to *all* advocates, or only to targets from one side?

**Puerto Rico pole example mapping:**

- (C): “Puerto Rico was neglected / grid failure is a serious injustice.”
- (A): “Bad Bunny performed in the U.S. using electricity ⇒ hypocrisy ⇒ critique invalid.”
  CQs 1, 3, 4, 6 are especially lethal: hypocrisy (even if true) doesn’t refute the claim; electricity use is non-optional; rebuilding grids is institutional; independent evidence exists.

### Full scheme: “If you care, you must personally fix it”

**Scheme 2: “If you care, you must personally fix it” (A distorted **Argument from Practical Commitment** / **Argument from Responsibility**, often functioning as a derail)**

- **P1.** S says they care about problem (P) (or criticizes harm (H)).
- **P2.** If someone truly cares about (P), they will take direct personal action (A) that significantly reduces/solves (P).
- **P3.** S has not taken (A) (or not enough (A)).
- **C.** Therefore S doesn’t really care / S’s criticism is illegitimate / we can dismiss the critique.

- Hidden Assumptions

  - **A1.** “Caring” entails an obligation to take **one specific kind** of action (usually direct remediation) rather than other meaningful actions (speech, organizing, donating, voting, raising awareness).
  - **A2.** The relevant action (A) is within S’s power and capacity.
  - **A3.** Individual action is sufficient (or the primary lever) to address (P).
  - **A4.** If the critic is not personally acting, then no one else is (ignores collective/institutional action).
  - **A5.** The critique is only valid if paired with an immediately actionable plan by the speaker.
  - **A6.** Attention/representation is “free” and cannot itself be part of remedy (denies agenda-setting effects).
- Critical Questions

  1. **CQ1 (Action pluralism):** Are there multiple reasonable ways to “do something” about (P) besides (A)? Why is (A) the required one?
  2. **CQ2 (Capacity):** Does S have the capability, authority, or resources to do (A) at the scale needed?
  3. **CQ3 (Collective action):** Is (P) primarily an institutional/collective problem requiring government/industry coordination rather than individual fixes?
  4. **CQ4 (Effectiveness):** Would (A) actually help meaningfully, or is it symbolic/ineffective compared to systemic levers?
  5. **CQ5 (Truth vs. messenger):** Even if S did nothing, would that make the underlying diagnosis of (P) false?
  6. **CQ6 (Burden placement):** Why is the burden placed on the critic rather than on the responsible institutions/actors with duty/authority?
  7. **CQ7 (Consistency):** Do you apply this same requirement to people who defend the status quo (e.g., “If you think the grid is fine, what are you doing to ensure reliability?”)?

**Puerto Rico grid mapping:**

- (P): “massive grid failure / inadequate response.”
- Required (A) is implicitly “fix the grid / rebuild infrastructure.”
  CQs 2 and 3 are decisive: grid repair isn’t an individual-level remedy; authority lies with government + utilities + contractors; “doing something” includes advocacy and accountability.

### Full scheme: “You have a car, so you cannot criticize billionaires”

**Scheme 3: “You have a car, so criticizing billionaires/taxes is hypocritical” (Blend of Scheme 1 + **False Equivalence / Scale Collapse**)**

- **P1.** S argues for policy (C) (e.g., higher taxes on billionaires, redistribution, anti-capture).
- **P2.** S possesses some wealth/consumption item (W) (car, smartphone, comfort).
- **C.** Therefore S’s policy critique (C) is hypocritical or invalid.

- Hidden assumptions

  - **A1.** Any possession (W) is morally equivalent (in kind) to billionaire wealth (collapse of magnitude).
  - **A2.** The critique is “no one should have anything,” rather than “extreme concentration has systemic effects.”
  - **A3.** The policy claim’s validity depends on personal austerity by the speaker.
- Critical Questions

  1. **CQ1 (Relevance):** Does S owning (W) address the causal claim about billionaire-scale wealth and political economy?
  2. **CQ2 (Magnitude):** Is the comparison proportionate, or does it ignore orders-of-magnitude differences that matter to the policy argument?
  3. **CQ3 (Type):** Is the critique about **absolute possession** or about **concentration/power/externalities**?
  4. **CQ4 (Mechanism):** Which mechanism of harm (capture, rent-seeking, inequality) is allegedly neutralized by S owning a car?

## Appendix B. The Bayesian and Meta-Evidential Model

The technical point of the Pretti discussion can be summarized with the odds form of Bayes’ rule:

$$
\frac{P(H_1\mid E)}{P(H_2\mid E)} = \frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_2)}\times \frac{P(E\mid H_1)}{P(E\mid H_2)}
$$

Once the rival hypotheses and the observation are defined, the rule describes how prior odds should change in light of the observation’s comparative likelihood. It does not supply the relevant hypothesis space, the interpretation of the observation, or the likelihoods themselves. Evidence that looks decisive under one model can become less decisive under another when the models disagree about source reliability, relevant causal mechanisms, or the claim actually being tested. This is not permission to choose any likelihood one likes; model assumptions must themselves be open to independent criticism.

| Decision made before the update | Question to ask | Application to the Pretti dispute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hypothesis space** | Which rival propositions are considered possible, and are they comparable? | Separate justified force, unjustified force, and claims about intent or political identity rather than treating them as mutually exclusive labels. |
| **Observation** | What, precisely, does *E* describe? | “There was a firearm,” “an agent removed a firearm,” and “the shooters knew the weapon was removed” are different claims. |
| **Measurement and trust** | How was the observation generated and verified? | Distinguish primary footage, authenticated reconstructions, eyewitness reports, official statements, and altered stills. |
| **Likelihoods and background** | How expected is this observation under each serious explanation? | Consider alternative threat-perception accounts and whether the observed sequence actually discriminates among them. |
| **Question and standard** | What is the inference intended to establish, and to what threshold? | Do not substitute a political terrorism label for a time-specific assessment of lawful force. |

The meta-evidential standards used to evaluate those inputs include **independence** (do different methods converge?), **robustness** (does the result survive reasonable reanalysis?), **specificity** (does the observation actually distinguish hypotheses?), **anti-cherry-picking** (are unfavorable examples omitted?), **error sensitivity** (would the method reveal a mistake?), and **adversarial scrutiny** (has the case survived serious attempts to refute it?).

A usable diagnostic is to specify the background assumptions rather than hiding them behind “the evidence is obvious.” Under description *D* of the data, model *M*, background beliefs *B*, standards *S*, and question *Q*, does *E* really favor *H* over the relevant alternatives? The purpose of this schema is not to turn every argument into a mathematical exercise. It is to locate the disagreements that a political label can conceal.

## Appendix C. Selectors, Roles, and Platform Routines

This table distinguishes three levels that were developed at greater length in the original draft. Luhmann’s mass-media selectors are not identical to a platform’s engagement signals, nor should the roles in Dubois and Stahl be treated as his formal functional-system codes. The comparison is an *analytical extension*, useful because it isolates different questions.

| Analytical level | Question | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Selection criteria** | What makes an event or communication worth presenting? | Conflict, novelty, norm violation, moral judgment, personalization, topicality. |
| **Production routines** | How is a candidate communication repeatedly manufactured? | Clipping, headlines, briefs, templated accusations, cross-posting, creator collaboration. |
| **Platform allocation** | What makes a post visible to a particular audience? | Ranking and recommendation systems, user interactions, follows, predicted attention; weights vary by system and change over time. |
| **Influencer role** | What political or communications function is the creator performing? | Journalist, endorser, advertiser, volunteer, media outlet, lobbyist, data intermediary. |
| **Feedback** | How does the response alter subsequent communication? | Analytics-driven iteration, imitation, remixing, cross-network endorsement, commentary on commentary. |

Another way to clarify the influencer question is to distinguish the *appearance* of a message from the *function* it serves. A video may look like an independent news update while operating as sponsored advocacy. An apparently spontaneous reaction may be an organic reaction, a paid deliverable, or something in between. These claims cannot be adjudicated from style alone. Evidence of funding, coordination, production records, disclosure, or network relationships is needed for strong attribution.

The original draft discussed “flooding the zone,” distributed amplification, and the “4D” family of disinformation strategies. They are valuable as possible tactics in specific operations, but no single operation should be inferred merely because a public debate feels noisy or cynical. The point is to identify *observable mechanisms*: how messages were produced, how they moved, how audiences participated, and what evidence—rather than suspicion—links the stages together.
