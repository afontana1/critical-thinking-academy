# Economies as Metabolic processes

Metabolism is the set of all chemical processes your body uses to stay alive and functioning. It includes how your body:

* converts food into energy
* builds and repairs tissues
* regulates hormones and body temperature
* removes waste products
* stores or uses nutrients

There are two major parts:

1. **Catabolism** — breaking things down to release energy
   Example: digesting carbohydrates into glucose your cells can use.

2. **Anabolism** — building things up using energy
   Example: building muscle proteins after exercise.

Your body’s cells constantly perform these reactions, even when you’re sleeping or resting.

A common term is **basal metabolic rate (BMR)**, which is the amount of energy your body needs just to keep essential functions running, like breathing and circulation.

Several factors affect metabolism, including:

* age
* sex
* genetics
* muscle mass
* activity level
* hormones and health conditions

People often talk about having a “fast” or “slow” metabolism, but differences between individuals are usually smaller than popular culture suggests. Habits like regular exercise, especially strength training, can increase daily energy use by increasing muscle mass.

Here’s a simplified way to think about it:

**Food → nutrients → cellular reactions → energy + building materials**

At the cellular level, much of metabolism centers around converting nutrients into a molecule called ATP, which acts like the body’s energy currency.

Biologically, metabolism is fundamentally a network of processes that:

* take in inputs,
* transform them through regulated pathways,
* distribute resources,
* store or release energy,
* and produce outputs and waste.

In systems terms, metabolism resembles:

* a distributed processing pipeline,
* a logistics network,
* and an adaptive control system all at once.

You can think of it as:

> **An organism’s resource-processing and energy-management architecture.**

The analogy to algorithms and computation is surprisingly deep.

### Inputs

Examples:

* oxygen
* glucose
* fats
* amino acids
* micronutrients

These are analogous to:

* raw data,
* fuel,
* or computational resources.

### Transformations

Cells apply highly structured biochemical “algorithms”:

* glycolysis
* the citric acid cycle
* protein synthesis
* fat oxidation

Each pathway:

* has ordered steps,
* conditional regulation,
* feedback loops,
* error correction,
* and resource constraints.

That’s very similar to:

* state machines,
* optimization systems,
* compiler pipelines,
* or distributed compute graphs.

### Delivery & Routing

The circulatory system acts like a transport layer:

* delivering substrates where needed,
* reallocating energy dynamically,
* prioritizing critical systems.

Hormones act somewhat like:

* signaling protocols,
* orchestration messages,
* or scheduling instructions.

### Storage & Caching

The body stores:

* glycogen,
* fat,
* minerals,
* even oxygen-binding capacity.

Comparable to:

* buffers,
* caches,
* reserve compute,
* or battery systems.

### Outputs

Outputs include:

* movement,
* heat,
* growth,
* cognition,
* repair,
* reproduction,
* waste products.

### Regulation

Perhaps most importantly, metabolism is adaptive:

* it responds to scarcity,
* stress,
* temperature,
* activity,
* circadian rhythms,
* disease states.

So it’s not just a static process pipeline; it’s a self-regulating, self-maintaining dynamic system.

This systems perspective is why metabolism becomes such an important concept outside biology too:

* economics (“metabolism” of cities),
* ecology,
* cognition,
* organizations,
* AI systems,
* and cybernetics.

In all these contexts, “metabolism” implies:

> continuous transformation and flow that sustains the system’s existence.

# Process Economics

I think this heavily overlaps with W. Brian Arthurs work on modeling economies as a process. Look into his work, especially Economics in Nouns and Verbs. Explain to me his research, his insight about modeling economies with verbs/algorithms, and how this relates to the process view we have been looking at

Yes — Arthur is almost exactly in the same conceptual neighborhood as Mobus, but with a different emphasis.

Mobus says: **an economy is metabolic**.
Arthur says: **an economy is verb-like, algorithmic, and always becoming**.

Arthur’s core claim in **“Economics in Nouns and Verbs”** is that standard economics mostly models **nouns**: prices, quantities, GDP, interest rates, unemployment, assets, consumption, investment, inflation. These are measurable “things” or “levels.” But real economies are full of **verbs**: agents buy, sell, forecast, imitate, invent, adapt, learn, speculate, build, replace, exploit, reorganize. Arthur argues that formal economics often converts these verbs back into nouns, which makes it miss process, formation, novelty, adjustment, and nonequilibrium dynamics. ([sites.santafe.edu][1])

His proposed remedy is **algorithmic expression**. Equations are good for quantities and stable relations; algorithms are good for sequences of action: “if this happens, agents respond this way; that response changes the environment; the changed environment triggers new responses.” In other words, algorithms let economic theory include procedures, conditional behavior, history, adaptation, and evolving structure. Arthur says complexity economics sees the economy as “in motion,” “perpetually computing itself,” and “perpetually constructing itself anew.” ([Department of Economics][2])

The key move is this:

> The economy is not a finished machine whose equilibrium we solve for.
> It is a running process whose structure is continually produced by the actions inside it.

That is why Arthur’s economics is close to the metabolism metaphor. Metabolism is not a thing inside the cell; it is the cell’s ongoing network of transformations. Likewise, an economy is not merely its stock of capital, GDP, money supply, or firms. It is the ongoing pattern of producing, consuming, innovating, routing, storing, signaling, replacing, and adapting.

A simple mapping:

| Metabolic view                                | Arthur’s process view                                                        |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| nutrients flow through pathways               | resources flow through firms, markets, institutions                          |
| enzymes transform substrates                  | technologies and organizations transform inputs                              |
| feedback regulates activity                   | prices, expectations, rules, and institutions regulate action                |
| pathways adapt to conditions                  | agents change strategies as outcomes change                                  |
| new structures emerge from prior structures   | technologies and institutions generate further technologies and institutions |
| the organism is maintained through throughput | the economy is maintained through ongoing activity                           |

Arthur’s “verbs” are the economy’s metabolic reactions.

For example, standard economics might say: “investment increased,” “productivity rose,” or “a new technology shifted the production function.” Arthur wants to model the actual verb-chain: someone invents a technology; firms adopt it; older techniques are displaced; prices and incentives change; new opportunities appear; other technologies become possible; institutions adjust; and the economy’s structure changes again. He explicitly describes this as an algorithmic formation process that can trigger never-ending cascades of further change. ([Department of Economics][2])

This is why equilibrium becomes secondary. In an equilibrium model, the economy is often treated as if agents already know the relevant possibilities, optimize within them, and settle into a stable configuration. Arthur’s economy is different: possibilities themselves are being created. Agents do not merely choose among given options; they help generate the option-space. That is much more like biology, where evolution and metabolism do not just operate within a fixed structure — they continually reshape the organism and its niche.

The overlap with Mobus is strongest here:

> An economy is a self-maintaining, adaptive transformation system.

Mobus emphasizes the **energetic/material metabolism**: real economies transform energy and matter to maintain society.
Arthur emphasizes the **procedural/cognitive metabolism**: agents act, learn, invent, and recursively change the system they inhabit.

Together, they give a richer picture:

**Economy as metabolism** = material-energy throughput.
**Economy as verbs** = action-process structure.
**Economy as algorithm** = conditional, adaptive, historical unfolding.
**Economy as complex system** = the recursive interaction of all three.

So your connection is right: Arthur’s “nouns and verbs” framework is essentially a methodological argument for modeling the economy metabolically — not as a static collection of measurable objects, but as an ongoing, adaptive, generative process.

# Modeling the Economy as a Metabolic Process

So suppose we adopt the algorithmic/process view combined with the metabolic metaphor. Process modeling , process calculus , and process architecture all have concepts and languages for describing processes. One such formalizations is a Petri net. How could we use these structures, in conjunction with Mobus’ formal definition of a system, combined with the insights from Economics in Nouns and Verbs, to model an economy as a metabolic system? 

Yes. A Petri net is almost tailor-made for this synthesis.

Arthur’s point is that economies should model **verbs**: producing, consuming, exchanging, investing, learning, inventing, adapting. Petri nets give you a formal way to represent those verbs as executable processes, not just as static variables. Arthur argues that algebraic economics captures “quantifiable nouns” but leaves out “process, formation, adjustment, creation and nonequilibrium”; he proposes algorithmic expression as the way to include these. ([sites.santafe.edu][1])

A metabolic-economic Petri net would look like this:

| Petri-net element     | Economic/metabolic interpretation                                           |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Places**            | stocks, reservoirs, inventories, capacities, knowledge pools                |
| **Tokens**            | energy, materials, money, labor-hours, information, goods                   |
| **Transitions**       | verbs/processes: extract, refine, transport, hire, produce, consume, repair |
| **Arcs**              | input-output dependencies                                                   |
| **Weights**           | stoichiometry / production recipes                                          |
| **Inhibitor arcs**    | scarcity, regulation, bottlenecks, prohibitions                             |
| **Timed transitions** | production delays, shipping time, learning curves                           |
| **Colored tokens**    | heterogeneous goods, agents, skills, technologies                           |
| **Hierarchical nets** | firms, sectors, households, states, ecosystems                              |

So instead of writing:

> output = f(capital, labor)

you model the actual verb-structure:

> energy + materials + labor + knowledge → production transition → goods + waste + income + experience

That is Arthur’s “verbs” made formal.

Mobus’s systems frame then gives the architecture around the net. A system is a bounded organization of interacting components, embedded in an environment, with inputs, outputs, internal transformations, feedback, and control. His systems-science framing emphasizes bounded networks of relations among parts forming a holistic unit, interacting with larger systems. ([isecoeco.org][2])

So the modeling workflow becomes:

1. **Define the system boundary**
   Example: a household, firm, city, national economy, or global industrial system.

2. **Identify environmental inputs**
   Solar energy, fossil fuels, minerals, food, labor, information, ecological services.

3. **Represent internal metabolism**
   Extraction, conversion, distribution, consumption, repair, waste processing.

4. **Represent control and feedback**
   Prices, laws, inventory signals, credit conditions, expectations, ecological limits.

5. **Run the system dynamically**
   Tokens move, transitions fire, bottlenecks appear, stocks deplete, new pathways emerge.

A very simple economic-metabolic Petri net might be:

```text
[Energy] + [Raw Materials] + [Labor] + [Know-how]
        ↓
   (Production)
        ↓
[Goods] + [Waste] + [Income] + [Experience]
```

Then income feeds consumption; experience feeds learning; learning modifies production; waste feeds ecological damage; ecological damage constrains future input availability. That turns the economy into a recursive adaptive metabolism, not a static allocation problem.

The powerful part is that Petri nets can show **nonequilibrium** behavior: congestion, starvation, cascading failures, lock-in, oscillations, and phase transitions. That matches Arthur’s view of the economy as action-based, evolving, and not fully knowable in advance. ([arXiv][3])

So the synthesis is:

> **Mobus gives the system ontology.**
> **Arthur gives the verb/process epistemology.**
> **Petri nets give the executable formalism.**

The resulting model treats an economy as a living metabolic architecture: tokens circulate, processes transform them, feedback regulates them, and new structures emerge from repeated activity.

# A Better Description of Economies

It seems like this view gives us a more flexible way of describing economic systems and the components. 

For example, a “market” is now conceived of as some type of institution that binds or connects agents within the broader economic system, to achieve certain goals. There can be many types of markets, with different features, governed differently, that can achieve different ends and serve different functional roles. You can think of “market” in the abstract, general sense of the term, and go down the type hierarchy into more specific instantiations. The market serves a role within the broader metabolic system, covering different types of system needs.

So instead of conflating economies with markets, or conflating economics itself as the study of markets, we take a broader systems view. 

I think you can do this even more broadly; abstract economic structures serving the metabolic functioning of the system. 

Yes — that is exactly the conceptual payoff.

In this view, **markets are not the economy**. They are one class of **coordination organ** inside the economy’s broader metabolism.

An economy includes many functional structures:

| Structure          | Metabolic/system role                                     |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Markets            | decentralized coordination, discovery, allocation         |
| Firms              | organized production, specialization, internal planning   |
| Households         | reproduction of labor, care, consumption, socialization   |
| States             | rule-setting, stabilization, redistribution, public goods |
| Money/credit       | accounting, signaling, temporal coordination              |
| Infrastructure     | transport, communication, energy circulation              |
| Law                | constraint architecture, trust, conflict resolution       |
| Education          | skill formation, cultural memory, adaptive capacity       |
| Science/technology | novelty production, process improvement                   |
| Commons            | shared resource governance                                |
| Insurance/welfare  | risk pooling, system resilience                           |

So “market” becomes a **typed institutional process**, not the master concept.

A market might be:

> A rule-governed interaction space that connects agents for exchange, price formation, allocation, and information discovery.

But then you can specialize it:

```text
Market
├── spot market
├── futures market
├── labor market
├── housing market
├── auction market
├── platform market
├── black market
├── prediction market
├── public procurement market
└── regulated utility market
```

Each has different:

* participants,
* rules,
* permissions,
* tokens,
* feedback mechanisms,
* failure modes,
* moral/political constraints,
* and metabolic function.

A labor market does not perform the same system role as a carbon market, a wheat market, or a prediction market. They are all “markets,” but their **process architecture** differs.

That lets economics become broader than “the study of markets.” It becomes:

> the study of how societies organize metabolic processes: provisioning, production, distribution, maintenance, adaptation, and regeneration.

From that angle, “economic structure” means any institution or process that helps the system answer questions like:

* What inputs are needed?
* Who transforms them?
* How are outputs distributed?
* How are shortages detected?
* How is waste handled?
* How are risks absorbed?
* How does the system learn?
* How does it reproduce itself over time?

This also avoids market fundamentalism and anti-market reductionism. Markets are neither magic nor inherently bad. They are **tools with functional domains**. Some metabolic tasks are well served by markets. Others are better served by planning, reciprocity, commons governance, public provision, household care, or professional norms.

So the broader frame is:

> An economy is a system of interlocking process-structures that sustain a society’s material, energetic, informational, and social reproduction.

Markets are one such structure. Firms are another. States are another. Families, schools, hospitals, logistics networks, banks, standards bodies, and research institutions are all part of the broader metabolic architecture.

This is a much richer ontology than:

> economy = market exchange

It lets you ask a more powerful design question:

> What kind of coordination structure best serves this metabolic function, under these constraints, at this scale?

That is where systems science becomes genuinely useful for economics.
