## I. The Conceptual Problem: What Does a Grade Represent?

Before evaluating the usefulness of grades or grade point averages (GPA) as measures of knowledge, we must first establish what these quantities are supposed to represent. This is a fundamental question in measurement: what is the underlying attribute of interest, how is that attribute conceptually defined, and what relationship does the observed measurement have to it? Without specifying the object of measurement, it becomes difficult to determine what constitutes an accurate measurement, how to interpret variation between observations, or what conclusions those observations can reasonably support.

Consider a student who receives an A in intermediate microeconomics. What exactly does this grade tell us? Does it mean the student possesses a high level of knowledge of microeconomic theory? Does it indicate mastery of the course's learning objectives? Does it establish proficiency in applying economic models to unfamiliar problems? Or does it simply indicate that the student performed sufficiently well on a particular collection of assignments and examinations, as evaluated under the instructor's grading criteria? These interpretations are not equivalent. A student may perform exceptionally well on an examination without possessing a comprehensive understanding of the subject. Conversely, a student may have considerable knowledge of the material while performing poorly under particular assessment conditions. The observed grade and the underlying knowledge it is presumed to represent are therefore conceptually distinct. This distinction becomes clearer when we separate several related, but different, attributes: **knowledge, understanding, competence, achievement, and observed academic performance**.

Knowledge refers broadly to the information, concepts, principles, and relationships an individual possesses within some domain. Understanding involves grasping the relationships between those concepts, including the ability to explain their significance, recognize their implications, and apply them beyond familiar examples. Competence refers to the capacity to perform tasks requiring some combination of knowledge, understanding, and relevant skills. Achievement concerns the extent to which an individual has attained specified learning objectives. Observed academic performance, meanwhile, refers to what an individual actually demonstrates through assignments, examinations, projects, or other assessment activities.

These concepts overlap, but none is necessarily reducible to another. A person may possess substantial conceptual understanding while lacking proficiency in a particular technical procedure. Another may be highly competent at executing familiar procedures without understanding the principles underlying them. Both individuals may exhibit very different patterns of performance depending on the tasks used to evaluate them. The distinction matters because a grade is not a direct observation of these underlying attributes. It is an evaluation of demonstrated performance under a particular set of institutional and instructional conditions. A grade may provide evidence about knowledge or competence, but the relationship between the observation and those attributes must be established rather than assumed.

In measurement theory, an attribute such as knowledge is commonly treated as a **latent construct**: something that cannot be directly observed in its entirety but whose presence or degree may be inferred through observable indicators. An instructor cannot directly inspect a student's complete state of knowledge. Instead, the instructor presents tasks intended to elicit responses that reveal aspects of that knowledge. Those responses are subsequently evaluated and translated into grades. This introduces a distinction between the *theoretical construct* and its *operationalization*. The theoretical construct is the attribute we intend to investigate, such as an individual's understanding of microeconomic theory. The operationalization consists of the procedures used to generate observations: examinations, assignments, assessment rubrics, scoring rules, and grading thresholds. The resulting grade is an output of those procedures. To illustrate, we can represent the observed grade as the outcome of a general measurement process:

$$
G_i=f(K_i,A_i,E_i,I_i,S_i)+\varepsilon_i
$$

where:

- $G_i$ represents the observed grade assigned to individual $i$.
- $K_i$ represents the individual's underlying subject-matter knowledge.
- $A_i$ represents the assessment design and its alignment with the knowledge and abilities being evaluated.
- $E_i$ represents effort, preparation, and test-taking behavior.
- $I_i$ represents instructor-specific and institutional grading practices.
- $S_i$ represents socioeconomic, personal, and situational circumstances affecting performance.
- $\varepsilon_i$ represents residual influences not otherwise captured by the model.

This expression is conceptual rather than an empirically estimated equation. Its purpose is to illustrate that the observed grade is generated through a process involving multiple factors, only one of which is the underlying knowledge we may wish to assess. The immediate implication is that the relationship between knowledge and grades need not be one-to-one. Individuals possessing different levels or configurations of knowledge may receive identical grades, while individuals with similar knowledge may receive different grades. The observation alone does not necessarily identify the underlying state that produced it. Furthermore, knowledge itself is not necessarily a single, homogeneous attribute. Someone's knowledge of economics may encompass mathematical proficiency, theoretical understanding, empirical reasoning, institutional knowledge, and the ability to apply economic concepts to unfamiliar problems. These dimensions need not develop uniformly within an individual. Consequently, even the statement that one person "knows more economics" than another may be underspecified unless we establish which dimensions matter and how they are to be compared.

The problem is therefore not merely that grades are imperfect observations. It is that the underlying attribute people often believe grades represent may itself be insufficiently defined. A course grade can summarize performance according to a specified assessment scheme without establishing a universal ordering of individuals along some single dimension of knowledge. This also requires distinguishing the **administrative meaning** of a grade from its **substantive interpretation**. Administratively, an A indicates that a student satisfied the requirements for that grade under a particular evaluation system. That is a meaningful and ordinarily verifiable statement. However, when an observer interprets the same A as evidence that the student possesses exceptional subject-matter understanding, the observer is making an additional inference about the relationship between the assessment process and the underlying knowledge construct.

The fact that a grade has a clearly defined administrative meaning does not guarantee that it possesses an equally clear interpretation as a measure of intellectual competence. This distinction is consistent with the broader literature on educational measurement. Messick (1989) emphasized that validity concerns the interpretations and uses of assessment results, rather than merely the numerical scores produced by an instrument. Similarly, Brookhart et al. (2016), in their synthesis of a century of grading research, examined the multiple academic and nonacademic meanings embedded in grades. Bowers (2011) likewise characterized teacher-assigned grades as multidimensional indicators that reflect more than performance on conventional measures of academic achievement.

None of this implies that grades are meaningless, or that they bear no relationship to knowledge. Indeed, grades may provide useful evidence of learning, achievement, persistence, and other characteristics relevant to educational success. The objection is not to the existence of such relationships, but to the assumption that they are sufficiently direct, stable, or comprehensive to justify treating a grade as a definitive representation of the underlying attribute. A high grade may increase our confidence that an individual understands the material. However, how much it should increase that confidence depends on what was assessed, how performance was evaluated, and how strongly the resulting observation is associated with the kind of understanding we wish to infer.

The question, then, is not simply whether an individual received an A or possesses a 4.0 GPA. It is **what information that observation provides about the individual's knowledge, and what assumptions are necessary to justify that interpretation**. This establishes the foundational issue for the analysis that follows. Before using grades to compare individuals or draw conclusions about their competence, we must examine how the observations are generated, whether the instruments meaningfully represent the intended attributes, and whether the resulting measures are sufficiently consistent and comparable to support the inferences we wish to make. A grade is an observation generated by a measurement process; **it should not be mistaken for the underlying property we are attempting to measure.**

## II. The Measurement Problem: Instrumentation, Calibration, and Error

Having distinguished underlying knowledge from the grades intended to represent it, we can now examine how those grades are acquired. Even if we accept that subject-matter knowledge is a meaningful and measurable attribute, the question remains whether the instruments used to assess it produce observations that can be reliably interpreted and compared. This distinction becomes particularly apparent when we contrast academic grading with the measurement of a comparatively well-defined physical quantity, such as an individual's height. The purpose of this comparison is not to suggest that physical measurements are perfectly objective or free from error. Rather, it is to identify the properties that make a measurement interpretable: a clearly specified quantity, standardized instruments, calibrated units, reproducible procedures, and a reasonably well-characterized relationship between the observed value and the underlying attribute.

Consider an individual whose height is measured at 185 centimeters. This measurement conveys information independently of the person who performed it or the particular instrument used, provided the measurement followed appropriate procedures. The result can be independently verified, expressed in different units, or compared with other height measurements without fundamentally changing its meaning. The instruments themselves are relatively standardized. A measuring tape, a stadiometer, and other properly calibrated devices are intended to measure the same physical quantity. Differences between their measurements can often be attributed to identifiable sources of error, such as instrument calibration, posture, positioning, or procedural inconsistencies. We can represent this process through a simplified measurement model:

$$
X_{ij}=H_i+b_j+\varepsilon_{ij}
$$

where $X_{ij}$ is the observed height of individual $i$ using instrument $j$, $H_i$ is the individual's actual height under a specified measurement protocol, $b_j$ represents systematic instrument bias, and $\varepsilon_{ij}$ represents residual measurement error. Under appropriate assumptions, including a stable underlying quantity during measurement and suitably centered residual errors, repeated observations can estimate the underlying height with increasing precision. Moreover, the instrument-specific bias can often be evaluated using external reference standards. If a measuring device systematically overstates height by two centimeters, its readings can be corrected accordingly. This model is deliberately simplified, but it illustrates an important property of physical measurement: **the distinction between the underlying quantity, the measurement instrument, and the error introduced by that instrument is comparatively well-defined.** The same cannot be assumed of academic grades. An instructor assessing knowledge of intermediate microeconomics must first decide which aspects of the discipline to examine. The instructor might emphasize constrained optimization, graphical analysis, comparative statics, formal proofs, conceptual explanations, or applications of economic theory. Even when instructors ostensibly evaluate the same subject, their selection of tasks may differ substantially.

The instrument is therefore not merely an alternative device for observing an otherwise clearly specified quantity. Its design partly determines which dimensions of knowledge become observable and which remain unexamined. This is the first major departure from the physical measurement analogy. Whereas two calibrated devices for measuring height are intended to produce observations of the same physical dimension, two academic examinations may evaluate different combinations of abilities even when administered in courses bearing the same title. An examination emphasizing mathematical technique may strongly reward computational proficiency while providing comparatively little information about conceptual understanding. Another examination may reverse those priorities. The resulting grades cannot automatically be interpreted as interchangeable measurements of the same underlying attribute. A more appropriate representation of academic assessment therefore requires explicitly accounting for the instrument and the conditions under which it operates. Let the observed performance score be:

$$
Y_{ijr}=m(\mathbf K_i,T_j,C_{ir})+u_{ijr}
$$

where:

- $Y_{ijr}$ represents the performance of student $i$ on assessment instrument $j$ during administration $r$.
- $\mathbf K_i$ represents the student's potentially multidimensional knowledge state at the time of assessment.
- $T_j$ represents the instrument's task selection, content, format, and emphasis.
- $C_{ir}$ represents the circumstances under which the student completes the assessment.
- $m(\cdot)$ represents the relationship between knowledge, assessment design, and demonstrated performance.
- $u_{ijr}$ captures residual variation not otherwise represented.

Observed performance is then translated into a grade through an additional scoring function:

$$
G_{ijr}=q_{jr}(Y_{ijr};R_j)
$$

where $R_j$ represents the grading rubric, weighting scheme, thresholds, and other evaluation rules, while $q_{jr}$ allows for variation in scoring across evaluators or administrations. These expressions are conceptual models rather than estimated empirical relationships. They nevertheless clarify an important point: **the grade is the output of at least two distinct processes—the elicitation of performance and the evaluation of that performance.** The first determines what the student demonstrates. The second determines how that demonstration is translated into an institutional grade. Variation at either stage can affect the final observation without corresponding to an equivalent change in underlying knowledge.

### Instrument Heterogeneity and the Absence of Common Calibration

In physical measurement, calibration establishes a relationship between an instrument's output and an independently defined reference quantity. A ruler marked in inches and another marked in centimeters can produce measurements that are converted without losing their underlying meaning:

$$
1\text{ inch}=2.54\text{ centimeters}
$$

The conversion is possible because the instruments refer to the same underlying dimension and the units have a defined relationship. Academic grading systems possess standardized reporting conventions, but these do not imply an equivalent calibration of their underlying assessment processes. An A assigned by one instructor is not necessarily equivalent to an A assigned by another. The two instructors may use different examinations, scoring criteria, expectations, and thresholds. Their instruments may also measure different dimensions of knowledge. Consequently, there is no universal transformation that maps the grade assigned under one assessment system into an equivalent measure of knowledge under another. This is not simply because some instructors grade more generously than others. Differences in grading severity might, under appropriate conditions, be estimated and corrected. The more fundamental problem is that the instruments may not be evaluating the same attributes in the same proportions. Suppose two assessments produce grades according to different functions:

$$
G_{iA}=f_A(\mathbf K_i)
$$

$$
G_{iB}=f_B(\mathbf K_i)
$$

If $f_A$ primarily rewards mathematical proficiency while $f_B$ primarily rewards conceptual understanding, the relationship between their outputs depends on the student's particular competence profile. A common conversion would require additional assumptions establishing how the different dimensions relate to a shared target construct. Without those assumptions, a higher score on one instrument cannot necessarily be translated into an equivalent score on the other. In other words, the difficulty is not merely that the instruments use different scales. They may use fundamentally different measurement functions.

Empirical research on grading practices supports this concern. Lipnevich, Guskey, Murano, and Smith (2020), in *What Do Grades Mean? Variation in Grading Criteria in American College and University Courses*, examined 250 course syllabi from American colleges and universities. They identified variation in grading criteria across disciplines, including differences in the emphasis placed on assessed products, academic processes, and examinations. The significance of this finding is that differences in grade generation are not merely hypothetical. Academic institutions routinely use heterogeneous procedures to produce observations that are subsequently expressed through common grading categories. Thus, the apparent uniformity of an A–F grading scale can conceal substantial variation in what has actually been measured.

### Measurement Error, Systematic Variation, and Reliability

A second issue concerns the characterization of measurement error. In classical measurement theory, an observed score is often represented as:

$$
X_i=\theta_i+\varepsilon_i
$$

where $\theta_i$ is a true score, defined within a particular measurement framework, and $\varepsilon_i$ is measurement error. The usefulness of this model depends on assumptions about the relationship between the underlying score and the observed variation. If residual errors are appropriately centered and sufficiently independent across repeated measurements, averaging observations can reduce their influence. However, academic grading frequently involves sources of variation that do not behave like independent random disturbances. An instructor may consistently reward particular kinds of responses. Another may consistently penalize them. Students may perform differently depending on the format of an examination, their familiarity with a particular class of problems, or the amount of time available. Such variation may be systematic, correlated with student characteristics, or dependent on the interaction between the individual and the assessment instrument. A useful framework is to consider the observed numerical assessment score as containing several components:

$$
Y_{ijr}
=
\mu+\theta_i+a_j+c_r+(\theta a)_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ijr}
$$

where:

- $\mu$ represents the overall expected score.
- $\theta_i$ represents an individual-specific component of performance within the defined assessment domain.
- $a_j$ represents the effect of the assessment instrument.
- $c_r$ represents the effect of the administration conditions.
- $(\theta a)_{ij}$ represents the interaction between a particular student and the instrument.
- $\varepsilon_{ijr}$ represents residual variation.

This is an illustrative additive decomposition on a numerical assessment scale, not a universal model of categorical letter grades. The interaction term is especially important. Some students may perform particularly well on quantitative examinations but poorly on essay-based or conceptual assessments. Others may exhibit the opposite pattern. Thus, the effect of changing the instrument need not be the same for every student. Under an appropriately specified random-effects model, with mutually uncorrelated components, the observed score variance can be decomposed as:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y)
=
\sigma_\theta^2+
\sigma_a^2+
\sigma_c^2+
\sigma_{\theta a}^2+
\sigma_\varepsilon^2
$$

This decomposition illustrates how observed score differences can arise from several sources beyond stable individual differences. The relative importance of these components is an empirical question. It cannot be established merely by observing the final grade. Under a simpler classical model, a reliability coefficient can be written as:

$$
\rho=
\frac{\sigma_\theta^2}
{\sigma_\theta^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2}
$$

where $\sigma_\theta^2$ represents variance in the true-score component and $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ represents error variance. This expression applies under restrictive assumptions about the relevant score and error components. More elaborate measurement models account for differences among tasks, raters, administrations, and their interactions. The essential distinction is that reliability concerns the consistency of measurement, not necessarily the appropriateness of what is measured. An examination can produce highly consistent results while repeatedly evaluating an incomplete representation of the intended knowledge domain. For example, a student might consistently perform well on examinations emphasizing familiar computational procedures while lacking the ability to explain the conceptual foundations of those procedures. In that case, the instrument may be reliable with respect to the competencies it repeatedly evaluates, while providing limited evidence about broader disciplinary understanding. **Reliability is therefore necessary for many strong interpretations of assessment results, but it is not sufficient to establish their validity.**

### The Evaluator as a Source of Measurement Variation

The role of the instructor introduces another potential source of variation. Even when the assessment task and student response remain unchanged, different evaluators may assign different scores. This is the problem of inter-rater reliability. A simplified model is:

$$
G_{ij}=P_i+\alpha_j+\varepsilon_{ij}
$$

where $P_i$ represents performance on a common numerical assessment scale, $\alpha_j$ represents the scoring tendency of evaluator $j$, and $\varepsilon_{ij}$ represents residual variation. The model is most readily interpreted for numerical scores before conversion to letter grades. Under this formulation, differences between assigned scores may arise partly from differences between evaluators rather than differences in demonstrated student performance. An early illustration appears in Starch and Elliott's (1912) study, *Reliability of the Grading of High-School Work in English*. The researchers asked 142 teachers to evaluate the same English paper and found that assigned scores ranged from 50% to 98%.

The historical study does not establish that such variation is typical of all contemporary grading systems. Nevertheless, it demonstrates a fundamental measurement problem: identical student work can produce substantially different recorded outcomes when evaluated by different individuals. Modern grading practices can mitigate this problem through explicit rubrics, blind evaluation, common scoring standards, multiple raters, and evaluator calibration. Their effectiveness, however, depends on how consistently those practices are implemented. Furthermore, even perfectly consistent scoring of a common task would not resolve the separate question of whether that task adequately represents the knowledge being assessed.

### Reproducibility and the Problem of Independent Verification

A further distinction concerns the ability to replicate and independently verify the measurement. If an individual's height is measured at 185 centimeters, a second measurement under comparable conditions should ordinarily yield a similar result within a reasonably narrow range of error. The original result can also be checked using a different, properly calibrated instrument. With academic assessments, the meaning of replication is considerably more complicated. Suppose a student receives an A on one financial engineering examination and a C on another. Several explanations are possible. The student's knowledge may have changed. The examinations may have sampled different material. The testing conditions may have differed. One examination may have emphasized mathematical computation, while the other emphasized conceptual understanding or applications. These possibilities cannot be distinguished merely by observing the two grades.

There are consequently two distinct problems: **whether the same measurement procedure produces stable results, and whether different procedures intended to evaluate the same subject actually measure equivalent attributes.** The first concerns test–retest reliability. The second concerns the comparability and validity of the instruments themselves. A large difference between repeated assessments need not automatically indicate measurement error, since genuine learning or forgetting may occur between administrations. Similarly, variation between different examinations may reveal meaningful differences in the competencies being assessed. However, this reinforces the need to examine the data-generating process. Without information about the instruments and testing conditions, we cannot confidently interpret changes in grades as changes in knowledge. Nor does merely increasing the number of observations resolve every problem. Repeating a narrow examination may yield an increasingly precise estimate of performance on that type of examination without revealing dimensions of competence that the instrument does not measure.

### Assessment Conditions and the Reactivity of Measurement

Academic assessments also differ from many routine physical measurements because the circumstances of measurement can influence the behavior through which the underlying attribute becomes observable. Consider the effect of time pressure on an examination. A student may understand the relevant concepts but require more time to formulate a response. Another may be exceptionally proficient at recognizing familiar problem types and rapidly executing procedures. A timed examination could systematically favor the second student, even if the first possesses stronger conceptual understanding. Anxiety, fatigue, preparation, language demands, and familiarity with examination formats can similarly influence demonstrated performance. These factors are not inherently irrelevant. If rapid problem-solving under pressure is itself a desired competence, time constraints may be an appropriate component of the assessment. But if the intended construct is durable conceptual understanding, those same conditions may introduce variation that complicates the inference from observed performance to knowledge.

The relevant distinction is between the attribute an instrument is intended to measure and the additional conditions that determine whether an individual can demonstrate it. This concern becomes especially important because academic assessments are consequential social activities. Students know that their performance may affect scholarships, admissions, employment opportunities, and professional advancement. Those consequences can influence how they prepare for and respond to assessment. By contrast, although height measurement can also carry social meaning and is not entirely free from procedural influences, the immediate psychological and behavioral response to routine measurement ordinarily plays a much smaller role in determining the observed physical quantity. The broader incentive effects of grading will be examined separately in the discussion of Goodhart's law. For present purposes, the point is that the conditions of measurement cannot be presumed independent of the performance being observed.

### Implications for Interpreting Grades

Several different obstacles to interpretation therefore need to be kept separate. Academic instruments differ in the competencies they elicit and the weights assigned to them; grading systems have no universal calibration that guarantees equivalence across instructors; scores may contain random, systematic, and student-by-task variation; and testing conditions can influence what students demonstrate. Neither a stable repeated grade nor a changed grade conclusively establishes the state of the broader knowledge construct, because test–retest consistency and construct coverage pose different questions. These limitations do not make educational measurement impossible. Standardized tasks, shared rubrics, evaluator calibration, and diverse independent observations can improve the evidence. But the improvements must be demonstrated rather than inferred from the use of the same A–F reporting scale. **Numerical uniformity is not measurement equivalence**, and comparison requires attention to the instruments and conditions that generated the observations. The next problem concerns what happens when those already heterogeneous outputs are reduced to a single score, then averaged across courses.

## III. The Representation Problem: Multidimensional Knowledge Reduced to a Scalar

Even perfectly calibrated, consistently scored assessments would leave a further question unresolved: can the knowledge we want to evaluate be represented adequately by a single numerical outcome? Competence in a field such as economics involves distinct capabilities, and the score on a course assessment reflects the particular selection and weighting of those capabilities. The process is not merely observation but also compression: a multidimensional knowledge profile becomes a numerical score, then a grade category, and eventually one element of a GPA. The resulting aggregate can faithfully describe performance under its chosen rules without describing the student's broader competence with comparable fidelity. This section therefore examines **which distinctions are preserved by scalar summaries, which are lost, and when the lost information matters for interpreting an individual's understanding**. The problem exists even in the absence of grading error and becomes more consequential when different courses' grades are averaged.

### Knowledge as a Multidimensional Attribute

Consider the meaning of possessing knowledge of economics. Economic competence encompasses several related but distinguishable abilities. An individual may understand theoretical relationships, manipulate mathematical models, interpret empirical evidence, recognize institutional constraints, or apply economic reasoning to unfamiliar problems. These dimensions are not necessarily interchangeable, nor do they develop uniformly. A student may demonstrate exceptional proficiency in mathematical optimization while possessing limited intuition about the economic significance of the models being manipulated. Another student may understand the conceptual foundations of economic theory but have weaker computational skills. A third may possess strong empirical reasoning and institutional knowledge but relatively limited experience with formal economic modeling. It is not obvious that these individuals can be unambiguously ranked according to a single, universally defined quantity called economic knowledge. We can formalize this issue by representing an individual's knowledge state as a vector:

$$
\mathbf K_i=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{i,\text{conceptual}}\\
K_{i,\text{mathematical}}\\
K_{i,\text{empirical}}\\
K_{i,\text{institutional}}\\
K_{i,\text{applied}}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Each component represents a dimension of knowledge or competence associated with the discipline. This is a conceptual representation rather than a claim that the dimensions can necessarily be measured on a common cardinal scale. The purpose is to distinguish the underlying configuration of abilities from any single statistic constructed to summarize it. A comparison between individuals now becomes more complicated. If Student A possesses greater mathematical proficiency than Student B but weaker conceptual understanding, which student possesses more economics knowledge? The answer depends on the definition of the relevant competence and the relative importance assigned to its dimensions.

If the objective is to evaluate capacity for highly quantitative economic research, mathematical proficiency may warrant considerable weight. If the objective is to evaluate the ability to interpret economic mechanisms and formulate policy arguments, conceptual and institutional knowledge may be more important. Neither weighting scheme is inherently universal. Each reflects a particular conception of what matters for the task. Thus, before combining different competence dimensions into a single number, we must establish a rule governing how those dimensions are to be compared and aggregated.

### The Assessment as an Implicit Weighting Function

An academic examination does not measure every dimension of subject knowledge equally. Its task selection, format, difficulty, and grading criteria determine which abilities contribute to the observed score. To illustrate, suppose that an assessment produces a numerical score according to a weighted combination of several dimensions of competence:

$$
S_{ic}=\mathbf a_c^\top\mathbf K_i+u_{ic}
$$

where:

- $S_{ic}$ represents student $i$'s numerical score in course $c$.
- $\mathbf K_i$ represents the student's multidimensional knowledge state.
- $\mathbf a_c$ represents the weights assigned to different dimensions by the assessment.
- $u_{ic}$ represents additional performance influences and residual error.

For illustrative purposes, suppose that the components of knowledge have been placed on a common normalized scale and that the weights are nonnegative and sum to one:

$$
a_{cm}\geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_{m=1}^{d}a_{cm}=1
$$

These assumptions make the example mathematically transparent; they do not establish that actual knowledge possesses such a natural scale. The score is then a projection of the multidimensional knowledge state onto the particular weighting scheme used by the assessment. The implications are substantial. Consider two hypothetical economics students whose proficiency across two dimensions is represented below. The numbers are illustrative normalized values, not empirical measurements of their knowledge.

| Competence dimension | Student A | Student B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mathematical proficiency | 0.95 | 0.65 |
| Conceptual understanding | 0.40 | 0.85 |

Suppose the examination places 80% of its weight on mathematical proficiency and 20% on conceptual understanding. The resulting scores would be:

$$
S_A=0.8(0.95)+0.2(0.40)=0.84
$$

$$
S_B=0.8(0.65)+0.2(0.85)=0.69
$$

Under this assessment, Student A substantially outperforms Student B. Now suppose another instructor evaluates the same students using an examination that assigns 20% of its weight to mathematical proficiency and 80% to conceptual understanding. The results become:

$$
S_A=0.2(0.95)+0.8(0.40)=0.51
$$

$$
S_B=0.2(0.65)+0.8(0.85)=0.81
$$

The ordering reverses. Nothing about the students' underlying competence profiles has changed. Only the assessment's weighting function has changed. The same individuals can therefore receive different performance rankings depending on which dimensions the instrument emphasizes. This illustrates a central limitation of interpreting grades as general measures of knowledge: **the observed ranking may reflect the assessment designer's weighting of competencies rather than an independently established ordering of overall subject-matter understanding.** The result is not necessarily an error in the examination. Both assessments could be accurately administered and consistently scored. Each could legitimately evaluate a different combination of abilities. The problem arises when an observer treats either resulting score as a comprehensive, context-independent measure of the student's knowledge of economics.

### The Financial Engineering Example: Technical Performance Versus Conceptual Understanding

The distinction between a multidimensional competence profile and a scalar examination score is illustrated by the financial engineering example introduced earlier. In a graduate financial engineering course, a student asked whether hedging would appear on the examination. The question was striking because hedging is a conceptual foundation of financial engineering. Yet the student ultimately received a higher grade than another individual who possessed a stronger understanding of the relevant conceptual foundations, partly because the former was proficient at solving a difficult integral. The significance of the example is not that mathematical proficiency is irrelevant to financial engineering. Mathematical techniques are essential to many areas of the discipline. Rather, the example demonstrates how an examination may assign substantial weight to a technical procedure while providing comparatively little information about whether the student understands the economic rationale for the procedure or the principles that motivate the broader model.

A student may know how to perform the necessary calculations without understanding why the calculations are undertaken, what assumptions justify them, or how their results should be interpreted. Conversely, another student may possess stronger conceptual understanding while being less proficient in the particular computational techniques emphasized by the examination. If the assessment primarily rewards the latter techniques, the resulting grade may accurately reflect computational performance while failing to discriminate adequately between different states of conceptual understanding. The observed grade is therefore conditional on the implicit or explicit weighting of different capabilities. This example also illustrates why the concept of general subject-matter competence requires more than a collection of technically difficult problems. A technically demanding assessment can still provide incomplete coverage of the construct it is intended to measure.

### The Many-to-One Mapping from Competence to Grades

An additional implication of scalar representation is that different underlying competence profiles can generate the same observed score. Suppose two students possess the following normalized competence profiles:

$$
\mathbf K_A=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.9\\
0.5
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf K_B=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.5\\
0.9
\end{bmatrix}
$$

If an examination weights both dimensions equally, then:

$$
S_A=0.5(0.9)+0.5(0.5)=0.7
$$

$$
S_B=0.5(0.5)+0.5(0.9)=0.7
$$

Both students receive the same numerical score, despite possessing different configurations of competence. This is a many-to-one mapping: multiple underlying states produce the same observation. Formally, if the assessment is represented by a function:

$$
S=f(\mathbf K)
$$

then there may exist distinct competence profiles such that:

$$
\mathbf K_A\neq\mathbf K_B
$$

while:

$$
f(\mathbf K_A)=f(\mathbf K_B)
$$

The observed score does not uniquely identify the underlying competence profile. This is not simply ordinary measurement noise. Even in the absence of residual error, the mapping can discard information because it reduces multiple dimensions to one. For a linear weighted assessment, differences between knowledge states that leave the weighted sum unchanged cannot be distinguished by that score. If the assessment is designed to capture only one combination of competencies, other dimensions or combinations may remain unobserved. This creates an identification problem. Knowing the observed score is insufficient to reconstruct the student's underlying profile. The problem becomes more pronounced when a numerical score is subsequently converted into a letter grade.

### From Continuous Scores to Categorical Grades

Course grades commonly involve another transformation: converting a numerical assessment score into a finite set of categories. Let the letter grade be determined by:

$$
G_{ic}=Q_c(S_{ic})
$$

where $Q_c$ represents the grading rule used in course $c$. For a simplified grading scheme:

$$
Q_c(S)=
\begin{cases}
A & S\geq 0.90\\
B & 0.80\leq S<0.90\\
C & 0.70\leq S<0.80\\
D & 0.60\leq S<0.70\\
F & S<0.60
\end{cases}
$$

These cutoffs are illustrative; actual grading rules vary. Under this scheme, students scoring 90% and 99% both receive an A, despite potentially meaningful differences in their demonstrated performance. Meanwhile, students scoring 89% and 90% receive different letter grades despite being separated by only one percentage point. The categorical transformation therefore discards some distinctions between scores while introducing discrete boundaries between adjacent categories. The letter grade provides a coarser representation of assessed performance than the underlying numerical score. This does not necessarily make categorical grading inappropriate. Categories can be useful for communicating whether performance satisfies defined standards. However, interpreting the resulting letter as though it represented a precise quantity of knowledge requires additional justification. In particular, the conversion does not establish that the distance between an A and a B corresponds to the same change in underlying competence as the distance between a C and a D. The categories may be ordered, but their relationship to an interval-scale representation of knowledge is not automatically established.

### The Ordinal and Interval-Scale Problem

When letter grades are converted into grade points, educational institutions typically assign numerical values such as:

| Letter grade | Grade points |
|---|---:|
| A | 4.0 |
| B | 3.0 |
| C | 2.0 |
| D | 1.0 |
| F | 0.0 |

This conversion creates a numerical scale suitable for performing administrative calculations. Yet the numerical differences between grade points do not necessarily correspond to equal differences in subject-matter knowledge. A one-point difference between an A and a B need not represent the same difference in underlying competence as a one-point difference between a C and a D. Nor does a 4.0 necessarily represent twice as much knowledge as a 2.0. The distinction concerns the interpretation of the scale. Ordinal information establishes an ordering. Interval-scale interpretation additionally requires meaningful equality of differences along the attribute being measured. Ratio-scale interpretation requires further properties, including a meaningful zero.

GPA has a numerical structure defined by institutional conventions, but this does not independently establish that it is an interval or ratio measure of knowledge. Importantly, this does not mean that arithmetic operations on grade points are mathematically invalid. GPA is a perfectly well-defined numerical index once the conversion and weighting rules are specified. The problem concerns what substantive interpretation can be attached to the resulting arithmetic. A difference of 0.5 grade points is an exact difference on the adopted administrative scale. It does not necessarily represent a standardized difference of 0.5 units in knowledge. Thus, **the numerical precision with which GPA is calculated should not be confused with the precision or interpretability of the underlying attribute it is presumed to represent.**

### GPA as a Second Stage of Aggregation

The representation problem becomes more complicated when grades from multiple courses are combined into GPA. A standard credit-weighted GPA can be represented as:

$$
\operatorname{GPA}_i=
\frac{\sum_{c=1}^{C_i}w_cg_{ic}}
{\sum_{c=1}^{C_i}w_c}
$$

where:

- $C_i$ is the number of courses included for student $i$.
- $g_{ic}$ is the numerical grade-point value assigned in course $c$.
- $w_c$ represents the corresponding course weight, ordinarily credit hours.

The resulting statistic combines multiple course-level evaluations into one scalar. But each course grade may already have been generated by a distinct assessment function:

$$
g_{ic}=q_c\left(f_c(\mathbf K_{ic},T_c,C_{ic},R_c)\right)
$$

Here, $\mathbf K_{ic}$ represents the relevant knowledge state at the time of course $c$, $f_c$ represents the performance-generation process, and $q_c$ represents the conversion from assessed performance into grade points under the course's grading rules. This formulation highlights the compounded nature of the aggregation. The individual course grade reduces a multidimensional performance profile into a numerical or categorical outcome. GPA then combines these outcomes across courses that may assess different competencies using different methods. The aggregation operates on the outputs of heterogeneous measurement processes.

For example, a grade in mathematical economics may predominantly reflect formal optimization and mathematical reasoning, while a grade in economic history may depend more heavily on historical interpretation, institutional understanding, and written argumentation. Both grades contribute to GPA according to administrative weights that need not reflect the importance of those competencies for a particular purpose. Thus, GPA does not simply average repeated measurements of one well-defined quantity. It may combine evaluations of distinct attributes according to a weighting system that has not been established as a meaningful measure of general subject competence.

### Identical GPAs Can Conceal Different Academic Profiles

Consider two students who have completed two equally weighted courses.

| Course | Student A | Student B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mathematical Economics | A (4.0) | B (3.0) |
| Economic Theory | C (2.0) | B (3.0) |
| **GPA** | **3.0** | **3.0** |

Both students possess identical GPAs. However, their academic profiles are different. Student A has demonstrated stronger performance in mathematical economics but weaker performance in the second course. Student B has demonstrated more balanced performance across the two. If the objective is to select a student for a mathematical modeling task, Student A's transcript may be more relevant. If the objective requires balanced competence across the two areas, Student B may be preferable. Those conclusions would still depend on the underlying assessments, but the example illustrates why the average alone is insufficient. The GPA does not preserve the distribution of course outcomes. This is a general property of arithmetic averages. Distinct distributions can possess identical means. An average can summarize a collection of observations without retaining the information necessary to reconstruct the collection. The same difficulty appears throughout statistical analysis. A summary statistic can be highly informative for one purpose while concealing variation essential to another. In this case, the appropriate interpretation depends on whether the differences between academic profiles matter for the decision being made.

### Formalizing Information Loss

The loss of information through aggregation can be expressed more generally. Let the student's complete recorded academic profile be:

$$
Z_i=\{(c,w_c,g_{ic})\}_{c=1}^{C_i}
$$

This includes the identity of the courses, their weights, and the associated numerical grade points. GPA is then a deterministic function of that profile:

$$
M_i=F(Z_i)
$$

where $M_i$ denotes the student's GPA. Because many different transcripts can produce the same GPA, the transformation is generally not invertible. The information contained in the original transcript cannot, in general, be recovered from the aggregate. In information-theoretic terms, if $\mathbf K$ denotes a random vector representing underlying competence, $Z$ denotes the recorded academic profile, and $M=F(Z)$ denotes GPA, then the data-processing inequality gives:

$$
I(\mathbf K;M)\leq I(\mathbf K;Z)
$$

Here, $I(\cdot\,;\cdot)$ denotes mutual information. Under this formulation, a deterministic summary of the transcript cannot contain more information about underlying competence than the full transcript from which it was calculated. Equality would require the aggregate to preserve all information in the transcript relevant to the specified knowledge construct. This distinction is particularly important because an aggregate can be useful without being sufficient. For GPA to retain all the information in the academic profile relevant to knowledge, a strong sufficiency condition would need to hold:

$$
P(\mathbf K\mid Z)=P(\mathbf K\mid M)
$$

In other words, once GPA is known, observing the full transcript would provide no additional information about the underlying competence profile. There is little reason to assume this condition holds for general subject-matter knowledge. Course identities, relative strengths, patterns of performance, and the specific domains assessed may all convey information that GPA discards. The conclusion is not that GPA contains no information. Rather, GPA is generally a coarser representation than the transcript and may be insufficient for inferences requiring details about particular competencies.

### Aggregation and the Problem of Compensability

Another consequence of averaging is that it imposes a compensatory structure. Under a weighted arithmetic mean, a higher score in one component can offset a lower score in another. This may be appropriate when the objective is to summarize overall graded performance. But many forms of professional and disciplinary competence are not fully compensatory. Consider financial engineering. A student might demonstrate exceptional computational proficiency while lacking a foundational understanding of hedging, risk-neutral valuation, or the assumptions underlying a model. Whether excellent performance in one area should compensate for substantial deficiencies in another depends on the competence being evaluated. For some tasks, strong performance in one dimension cannot substitute for a minimum level of competence in another. We can represent such a requirement by distinguishing an aggregate performance criterion from a threshold requirement. A compensatory rule might rank students according to:

$$
U_i=\sum_{m=1}^{d}a_mK_{im}
$$

where $U_i$ is an illustrative competence index. A noncompensatory requirement might instead impose:

$$
K_{im}\geq \tau_m
\qquad
\text{for every essential dimension }m
$$

where $\tau_m$ represents the minimum required proficiency in a particular competency. These rules serve different purposes. A high aggregate score does not guarantee that all essential competence thresholds have been satisfied. Because GPA is ordinarily constructed through an averaging procedure, it does not inherently communicate whether an individual possesses minimum proficiency in each dimension relevant to a particular decision. The issue is especially consequential when a deficiency in one foundational area undermines the usefulness of otherwise strong performance.

### Why Aggregation Can Still Improve Measurement

A careful critique must acknowledge an important counterargument: aggregation can improve the reliability of a measurement. If several assessments provide partially independent observations of the same underlying attribute, averaging them can reduce the influence of idiosyncratic errors. Suppose repeated assessments follow a simplified classical model:

$$
Y_{ij}=\theta_i+\varepsilon_{ij}
$$

where the errors are independent, have mean zero, and share variance $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$. The average of $n$ such observations is:

$$
\overline{Y}_i=\theta_i+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{ij}
$$

Conditional on a stable true-score component, the measurement-error variance becomes:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\overline{\varepsilon}_i)
=
\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{n}
$$

Under these assumptions, the average becomes more precise as the number of assessments increases. This is one reason a GPA compiled from many courses may provide a more stable indicator of academic performance than the result of a single examination. However, the conclusion depends on the structure of the measurements. If the assessments evaluate different constructs, share systematic biases, or use incompatible standards, averaging may not estimate a single underlying attribute with the same interpretation. Even if the aggregate becomes more stable, we must distinguish stability from validity.

For example, a GPA may reliably summarize a student's ability to fulfill academic requirements across several semesters. That reliability does not establish that the GPA measures conceptual economics knowledge with comparable precision. Nor does the improvement in reliability imply that the average preserves as much information as the complete set of observations. Both propositions can be true simultaneously: **Aggregating multiple measurements can reduce random error, while aggregating their results can discard information relevant to understanding the individual being evaluated.** This distinction avoids the mistaken conclusion that aggregation must always make measurement worse. The more defensible claim is that the benefits of aggregation are conditional on the similarity of the constructs, the error structure, and the purpose for which the aggregate is being used.

### Implications for Comparing Individual Competence

The preceding analysis has direct implications for evaluating individuals. A course grade compresses different aspects of performance into a single outcome. GPA then aggregates these outcomes across different courses. At each stage, the observer receives a simpler representation of a more complex underlying state. This simplification can be administratively useful, but it introduces uncertainty about the distribution of competencies that generated the observed number. Two individuals with identical GPAs need not possess equivalent knowledge profiles. Two individuals with different GPAs need not be unambiguously ranked according to every relevant dimension of competence.

And a higher GPA does not necessarily establish greater proficiency in the particular skills relevant to a specific task. To infer more, we require additional information about the courses completed, the assessments administered, the competencies emphasized, and the individual's demonstrated strengths and weaknesses. This is particularly important when comparing academic performance across disciplines or institutions, where the underlying dimensions and their weights may differ substantially. A scalar GPA remains a legitimate summary of academic performance under an established administrative convention. The error occurs when that summary is treated as though it were a complete representation of the student's underlying knowledge. The distinction can be summarized through the sequence:

$$
\text{Multidimensional knowledge}
\longrightarrow
\text{Assessed performance}
\longrightarrow
\text{Course grade}
\longrightarrow
\text{GPA}
$$

Each transformation may reduce the information available about the original knowledge state. The resulting aggregate can remain useful, predictive, and mathematically well-defined. Nevertheless, its meaning is determined by the information preserved through these transformations, not by the apparent precision or familiarity of the final number. This leads to the central conclusion of the representation problem: **A scalar summary of multidimensional competence cannot, without additional assumptions, be treated as a complete or uniquely interpretable measure of the underlying knowledge it is intended to represent.**

## IV. The Comparability Problem: Grades Across Institutions and Populations

The compression of different competencies into a grade becomes especially consequential when grades are compared across educational environments. An individual with a 3.9 GPA at one university may have completed a curriculum very different in content, rigor, and assessment methodology from that of another student with a 3.5 elsewhere. Even within one institution, departments and instructors may differ in their standards and in the kinds of performance they reward. These comparisons are often made as though a common numerical reporting scale guarantees a common meaning. It does not. To interpret the GPA difference as a difference in knowledge, we need evidence that the assessments evaluate sufficiently similar attributes under comparable standards, and that we can distinguish differences in student competence from differences in grading regimes and the populations subject to them. **The issue is not whether the GPAs can be compared arithmetically, but whether the arithmetic comparison has the proposed substantive interpretation.**

### Measurement Invariance and the Meaning of Comparable Grades

A fundamental requirement of meaningful measurement comparison is that the relationship between an observed measurement and its underlying attribute should remain sufficiently stable across the contexts being compared. In educational measurement, this concern is closely related to *measurement invariance*: whether an assessment relates to the intended latent construct in comparable ways across groups or settings. Suppose student $i$ possesses a multidimensional knowledge state $\mathbf K_i$ and is evaluated under institutional assessment regime $j$. A general representation is:

$$
G_{ij}=f_j(\mathbf K_i,\mathbf C_i,\mathbf E_i)
$$

where:

- $G_{ij}$ represents the grade produced under regime $j$.
- $\mathbf K_i$ represents the student's underlying competence profile.
- $\mathbf C_i$ represents relevant assessment and situational conditions.
- $\mathbf E_i$ represents effort, preparation, and performance-related behavior.
- $f_j$ represents the institution-specific or instructor-specific measurement function.

The subscript on $f_j$ is essential. It allows the relationship between knowledge and observed grades to differ across assessment regimes. If two institutions evaluate different dimensions of competence, assign different weights to those dimensions, or establish different thresholds for high performance, the same underlying knowledge state may produce different grades. Conversely, the same grade may correspond to different knowledge states across institutions. A strong form of measurement invariance, assuming a shared one-dimensional target construct $\theta$, would require that the conditional distribution of grades remain equivalent across assessment regimes:

$$
P(G\geq g\mid\theta,J=A)
=
P(G\geq g\mid\theta,J=B)
$$

for each relevant threshold $g$ and value of $\theta$, under appropriately comparable assessment conditions. Here, $J$ identifies the grading regime. This expression states that individuals possessing the same level of the target attribute should have the same probability of obtaining a grade at or above a specified threshold, regardless of which regime evaluates them. The condition is deliberately strong. Complete equality is not necessary for every useful comparison, and real assessments may only achieve partial invariance. Nevertheless, the expression clarifies what is at stake.

If the probability of receiving an A differs substantially across institutions even after accounting for the underlying competence being evaluated, the A does not possess an equivalent conditional interpretation across those institutions. This does not establish that one assessment system is necessarily better. It establishes that their outputs cannot automatically be treated as interchangeable. The situation becomes more complicated when the target construct is multidimensional. If one institution primarily evaluates mathematical technique while another emphasizes conceptual interpretation, there may not be a single common competence scale on which the grades can be meaningfully equated. Consequently, the comparability problem is not limited to differences in grading severity. It can arise from differences in the very attributes that institutions choose to evaluate.

### Institutional and Disciplinary Heterogeneity

Academic institutions differ in their curricula, instructional objectives, and assessment practices. These differences are particularly apparent across disciplines. Mathematics courses may emphasize proofs, formal reasoning, and problem-solving accuracy. Courses in history or literature may place greater weight on written argumentation, interpretation, and the synthesis of evidence. Laboratory courses may include experimental performance, technical procedures, and research reports. Such differences are not inherently problematic. They reflect the varied intellectual objectives of different fields. The problem arises when their resulting grades are treated as measurements on a common substantive scale without accounting for what those grades represent. Even within a single discipline, substantial variation may exist.

Consider economics. One university may organize its economics curriculum around formal mathematical modeling, econometrics, optimization, and quantitative problem-solving. Another may emphasize conceptual economic reasoning, institutional analysis, policy applications, and written examinations. Both programs may award a bachelor's degree in economics. Both may use conventional A–F grading systems. Yet the competencies required to obtain high grades may differ considerably. A student receiving an A in a mathematically demanding microeconomic theory course has demonstrated something different from a student receiving an A in a course emphasizing qualitative interpretation of economic concepts. Neither accomplishment is necessarily less legitimate. They simply provide different kinds of evidence. The relevant question is whether those differences have been accounted for when comparing the students.

Empirical research supports the existence of heterogeneous grading practices. Lipnevich, Guskey, Murano, and Smith (2020), in *What Do Grades Mean? Variation in Grading Criteria in American College and University Courses*, examined 250 course syllabi from American colleges and universities across education, mathematics, science, psychology, and English. Their study identified significant differences in grading criteria and examination practices across disciplines. Courses in education and English relied more heavily on process-oriented criteria, while other fields exhibited different emphases in their assessment structures. The researchers also identified interactions between institution type and academic discipline. These findings are important because they demonstrate that heterogeneity in grading is not merely a theoretical possibility. Instructors and departments employ different criteria for generating observations that are subsequently summarized through a common grading system.

Achen and Courant (2009), in *What Are Grades Made Of?*, examined grading patterns at the University of Michigan between 1992 and 2008. Their analysis emphasized persistent differences in average grades across academic disciplines, including the familiar pattern in which mathematics and natural sciences often assign lower average grades than many humanities disciplines. The persistence of these differences raises a fundamental question: to what extent do observed grade differences reflect differences in student competence, curricular demands, grading standards, or the populations selecting into particular fields? A comparison of average grades alone cannot resolve that question. Nevertheless, it demonstrates why the assumption of common meaning across disciplines is difficult to justify without additional evidence.

### The Problem of Cross-Institutional GPA Comparison

Consider two hypothetical students who have completed economics degrees at different universities.

| Dimension | Student A | Student B |
|---|---|---|
| GPA | 3.9 | 3.5 |
| Curriculum | Primarily qualitative and policy-oriented | Highly quantitative and theoretical |
| Assessment emphasis | Essays, conceptual explanations, coursework | Mathematical models, proofs, technical examinations |
| Grading standards | Institution-specific | Institution-specific |
| Mathematical competence | Not independently established | Demonstrated through demanding coursework |
| Conceptual understanding | Not independently established | Not independently established |

A superficial comparison ranks Student A above Student B because 3.9 exceeds 3.5. But what does that numerical ordering establish? It establishes that Student A obtained a higher average grade-point value under the grading rules applied to their academic record. It does not establish that Student A possesses greater competence in mathematical economics, economic theory, empirical analysis, or any other particular dimension of disciplinary knowledge. Indeed, Student B may possess stronger mathematical proficiency because their curriculum demanded substantially more mathematical reasoning. Alternatively, Student A may possess stronger conceptual understanding because their program emphasized interpretation and economic intuition. Both possibilities are compatible with the observed GPAs. The difficulty is that the comparison combines variation in student characteristics with variation in the assessment functions. Formally, suppose the two institutions produce grades through:

$$
G_{iA}=f_A(\mathbf K_i)
$$

$$
G_{iB}=f_B(\mathbf K_i)
$$

Even if the same student could be evaluated by both institutions, there is no general reason to expect:

$$
f_A(\mathbf K_i)=f_B(\mathbf K_i)
$$

Furthermore, differences between the functions need not be reducible to a constant adjustment. A grading system may favor particular competence profiles relative to others. Thus, translating scores across institutions can require more than correcting average differences in grading severity. One might attempt to construct a transformation:

$$
G_B=h_{A\rightarrow B}(G_A)
$$

where $h_{A\rightarrow B}$ maps grades from regime A onto the scale used by regime B. But a universally valid conversion requires a stable relationship between the underlying measurement processes. If the institutions assess different combinations of abilities, a transformation based solely on the observed grade may not exist. Two students with identical grades under regime A could receive different grades under regime B because their competence profiles differ in dimensions that regime A did not adequately distinguish. This is a fundamental obstacle to treating institutionally generated grades as universally comparable measurements of knowledge.

### Calibration, Equating, and the Limits of Statistical Adjustment

It would be too strong to conclude that educational measurements can never be compared across institutions or assessment regimes. Psychometrics has developed methods specifically intended to improve comparability between different tests. These include common-item designs, statistical equating, score linking, item response theory, and procedures for evaluating measurement invariance. When two examinations are designed to assess the same construct under sufficiently comparable specifications, statistical equating may establish a correspondence between their score scales. For example, if two forms of a mathematics examination differ slightly in difficulty but assess the same content domain, carefully designed equating procedures may allow their scores to be interpreted on a common scale. However, these methods require evidence about the relationship between the instruments. They do not produce meaningful equivalence merely because two assessments report numerical scores. There is also a distinction between *equating* and the broader process of *linking*.

Equating seeks to establish sufficiently strong comparability that scores from alternate forms of an assessment can be treated as interchangeable for the intended purposes. Linking may establish more limited relationships between distinct assessments, without justifying full interchangeability. This distinction is discussed by the National Research Council in *Uncommon Measures: Equivalence and Linkage Among Educational Tests* and in the educational measurement literature on score equating. The difficulty becomes especially pronounced when the assessment content differs. Statistical adjustments may align distributions, but aligning distributions does not establish that the instruments measure the same underlying construct. For example, converting each student's GPA into a percentile within their institution does not solve the substantive comparability problem. A student at the 90th percentile of one program and a student at the 90th percentile of another occupy similar relative positions within their respective grade distributions. But this does not establish that they possess equivalent absolute competence. Their reference populations, curricula, and grading standards may differ. Formally:

$$
F_A(G_{iA})=F_B(G_{kB})
$$

where $F_A$ and $F_B$ are the respective cumulative grade distributions, establishes an equality of relative grade positions. It does not establish:

$$
\mathbf K_i=\mathbf K_k
$$

The distinction is between relative standing within a population and equivalence on an underlying competence scale. The former can often be calculated from grades alone. The latter requires stronger measurement assumptions and additional evidence.

### Criterion-Referenced and Norm-Referenced Grading

The comparability problem also depends on how grades are assigned. Two broad approaches illustrate the distinction. Under **criterion-referenced grading**, a student's performance is evaluated against specified standards. For example, a student might receive an A by demonstrating mastery of a defined set of learning objectives or obtaining a score above an established threshold. Under **norm-referenced grading**, a student's grade depends at least partly on their performance relative to a reference group. A course might assign a fixed proportion of A grades to the highest-performing students, regardless of the absolute level of performance achieved. The distinction has important consequences for interpretation.

Suppose a student's absolute examination performance remains unchanged. Under a fixed criterion-referenced system, the grade would ordinarily remain unchanged, assuming the standard and relevant assessment conditions remain stable. Under a norm-referenced system, the grade could change if the performance of the comparison population changes. Let $S_i$ denote the student's examination score and $F_c$ the score distribution of the relevant course population. A simplified norm-referenced grading rule may be written as:

$$
G_{ic}=Q_c\left(F_c(S_i)\right)
$$

where $F_c(S_i)$ represents the student's position within the course score distribution and $Q_c$ maps that relative position to a grade. Under this rule, the same absolute performance may receive different grades in different cohorts. This means that the observed grade partly describes the student's position relative to a selected population rather than only the student's absolute competence. The two grading approaches are not mutually exclusive; many courses combine elements of both. However, their differences illustrate why a grade category does not necessarily possess a uniform interpretation across settings.

### Grade Inflation and Changes in Grading Standards

Comparability problems are not limited to differences between institutions at a single point in time. They also arise when grading practices change. An A awarded under one historical grading regime may not carry the same performance implications as an A awarded under another. Rojstaczer and Healy (2012), in *Where A Is Ordinary: The Evolution of American College and University Grading, 1940–2009*, examined historical grading patterns at American colleges and universities. Their research documented a substantial increase in the frequency of A grades over the period studied. In their contemporary institutional dataset, A grades accounted for approximately 43% of letter grades, compared with an estimated 15% in 1960. The authors also documented differences by institution type and academic field. These findings demonstrate that the distribution of grade categories has changed considerably across historical assessment environments. But the interpretation of these changes requires care. An increase in the frequency of A grades does not, by itself, establish that grading standards have become less demanding. Several mechanisms could generate the observed change:

- Students may arrive with stronger preparation.
- Instructional practices may improve.
- Curricula and assessment methods may change.
- Institutional grading standards may become more generous.
- Student course selection may change.
- Withdrawal policies may alter which performances appear in completed-course grade distributions.
- Student populations may change in composition.

Observed grade inflation, understood as an increase in assigned grades, is therefore not independently sufficient to identify a decline in standards. However, this uncertainty reinforces the broader measurement argument. **If the meaning of a grade depends on the population, curriculum, and standards under which it was generated, changes in those conditions complicate comparisons across time.** The numerical scale may remain constant while the relationship between the scale and underlying performance changes. This is particularly relevant to GPA because it is frequently used to compare individuals who attended different institutions, completed different curricula, or graduated during different periods. The absence of historical or institutional calibration creates uncertainty about whether observed numerical differences represent comparable differences in achievement.

### The Problem of Selection Into Institutions and Disciplines

Another important source of noncomparability arises from the fact that students are not randomly assigned to educational environments. Individuals select into universities, academic disciplines, courses, concentrations, and instructors based on numerous characteristics. These may include:

- Prior academic preparation.
- Mathematical ability.
- Financial circumstances.
- Academic interests and career objectives.
- Expected course difficulty.
- Institutional admission requirements.
- Expectations about grading standards.
- Access to particular programs.

Consequently, observed grade distributions reflect both the measurement regimes and the characteristics of the students who encounter them. This introduces a selection problem. Suppose we observe that one institution awards higher average GPAs than another. The difference could reflect more generous grading, but it could also reflect differences in the students' incoming preparation or competence. To formalize this, let $J$ denote the institution, $G$ the observed grade, $\mathbf K$ the relevant knowledge state, and $X$ other characteristics affecting grade generation. The observed grade distribution in institution $j$ can be expressed as:

$$
p(G\mid J=j)
=
\int
p(G\mid \mathbf K=\mathbf k,X=x,J=j)
\,dF_j(\mathbf k,x)
$$

The first term inside the integral represents the institution-specific measurement relationship: the distribution of grades conditional on the student's characteristics. The second term represents the joint distribution of those characteristics among students in the institution. The observed distribution therefore depends on both the grading process and the population being graded. This creates an identification problem. A difference in average grades between institutions does not tell us how much of that difference arises from assessment standards and how much arises from differences in student composition. In a simplified mean framework, define:

$$
\mu_j(\mathbf k,x)
=
E[G\mid\mathbf K=\mathbf k,X=x,J=j]
$$

Then:

$$
E[G\mid J=j]
=
\int\mu_j(\mathbf k,x)\,dF_j(\mathbf k,x)
$$

The difference in average grades between two institutions is:

$$
\Delta
=
\int\mu_A\,dF_A
-
\int\mu_B\,dF_B
$$

By adding and subtracting the same counterfactual quantity, this can be decomposed algebraically as:

$$
\Delta
=
\underbrace{
\int(\mu_A-\mu_B)\,dF_B
}_{\text{Measurement-regime component}}
+
\underbrace{
\int\mu_A\,d(F_A-F_B)
}_{\text{Population-composition component}}
$$

This decomposition illustrates how observed differences may reflect both differences in conditional grading functions and differences in student populations. However, the decomposition is not automatically identified from ordinary institutional data. The relevant knowledge states are partly latent, and the counterfactual grading functions may not be observed for comparable populations. Its interpretation also depends on the chosen reference distribution. The important point is conceptual: **differences in observed grades combine properties of the students with properties of the systems evaluating them.** Without additional information, these components cannot generally be disentangled. This is especially relevant when comparing students from academic programs with different admission standards and curricular demands. A highly quantitative economics program may attract students with greater mathematical preparation while also imposing more demanding quantitative assessments. The resulting grades reflect the interaction of student selection and assessment standards. An external observer examining GPA alone cannot reliably separate these influences.

### Socioeconomic Conditions and the Opportunity to Demonstrate Knowledge

Socioeconomic circumstances introduce another layer of complexity. Students differ in their access to educational resources, prior preparation, and the conditions under which they study and complete assessments. Some students may have substantial time for academic work, access to tutoring, stable housing, and limited obligations outside school. Others may face financial pressures, employment responsibilities, family obligations, or inadequate educational preparation. These conditions can affect both the acquisition of knowledge and the ability to demonstrate knowledge during assessment. Importantly, these are distinct mechanisms. A difference in access to educational resources may produce a genuine difference in acquired knowledge. A difference in testing conditions or outside obligations may produce differences in demonstrated performance even among students with comparable underlying knowledge. And differences in how instructors evaluate otherwise comparable work may generate an additional source of variation. These mechanisms should not be conflated. To illustrate the distinction, consider three broad pathways:

$$
SES\longrightarrow
\text{Educational opportunities}
\longrightarrow
K
\longrightarrow
G
$$

$$
SES\longrightarrow
\text{Assessment conditions}
\longrightarrow
G
$$

$$
SES\longrightarrow
\text{Evaluator expectations}
\longrightarrow
G
$$

Here, $SES$ represents socioeconomic status, $K$ represents acquired knowledge, and $G$ represents the observed grade. The first pathway describes how unequal educational opportunities can affect the actual acquisition of knowledge. The second describes how circumstances may affect the ability to demonstrate knowledge. The third describes how evaluator perceptions may influence the grade assigned to a given performance. These are schematic causal pathways, not a claim that every relationship is present or equally important in every institution. The distinction is important because different explanations imply different conclusions. If socioeconomic conditions affect knowledge acquisition, the grade may accurately reflect a difference in current achievement while remaining a poor measure of innate ability or future potential. If socioeconomic circumstances affect observed performance independently of knowledge, the grade may be a noisier indicator of the target competence. If evaluator expectations affect scoring conditional on demonstrated performance, the measurement process itself may introduce systematic distortion. The observed grade alone does not reveal which mechanism is responsible.

### Empirical Evidence of Evaluator Effects and Socioeconomic Bias

Research on grading provides evidence that evaluator judgments can be influenced by characteristics other than demonstrated academic proficiency. Doyle, Easterbrook, and Harris (2023), in *Roles of Socioeconomic Status, Ethnicity and Teacher Beliefs in Academic Grading*, conducted a preregistered experiment involving 416 teachers and trainee teachers in the United Kingdom. Participants evaluated identical student work that was randomly associated with students from different socioeconomic and ethnic backgrounds. The researchers found that work attributed to lower-socioeconomic-status students received less favorable evaluations, despite the work itself being identical. The reported overall grading difference associated with socioeconomic status was approximately 4.4%. The study did not find evidence of an overall ethnic-bias effect in the particular experimental conditions examined. This research illustrates how social information associated with a student can affect evaluation independently of the demonstrated work.

A separate study by Ferman and Fontes (2022), *Assessing Knowledge or Classroom Behavior? Evidence of Teachers' Grading Bias*, examined data from Brazilian schools. The researchers compared teacher-assigned and blindly assigned scores on high-stakes academic examinations covering the same material. They found that teachers systematically awarded more favorable scores to students exhibiting better classroom behavior and penalized students exhibiting worse behavior. These differences were not fully explained by the academic proficiency reflected in the comparison assessments. The findings are especially relevant because they distinguish the direct evaluation of academic performance from judgments associated with student behavior. Classroom behavior may itself be educationally or socially important. However, incorporating it into a score purportedly measuring academic proficiency changes the interpretation of the resulting observation.

A broader review by Zanga and De Gioannis (2023), covering 37 studies, examined evidence of discrimination in grading across multiple student characteristics. The review found evidence of grading discrimination in numerous settings, while acknowledging exceptions and methodological limitations. These findings should not be generalized indiscriminately to every instructor, institution, or student population. In particular, much of the evidence concerns primary or secondary education rather than university GPA directly. Nevertheless, they demonstrate that evaluator-dependent influences are empirically plausible and, in some settings, observable even when the underlying work or assessed content is held constant. They therefore challenge the assumption that every recorded grade reflects only the student's demonstrated academic proficiency.

### Distinguishing Measurement Bias from Unequal Educational Outcomes

It is important not to interpret socioeconomic differences in grades as evidence that the grades themselves are necessarily biased. Suppose students from two socioeconomic groups receive different average grades. That observation alone does not identify the mechanism responsible for the difference. The groups may differ in prior educational opportunities, preparation, school quality, outside obligations, or other characteristics affecting acquired knowledge and performance. Alternatively, the difference may partly reflect differential evaluation of comparable work. These explanations are substantively distinct. Formally, an unconditional disparity:

$$
E[G\mid SES=A]
\neq
E[G\mid SES=B]
$$

does not establish that evaluators treat otherwise equivalent performances differently. Evidence of differential evaluation requires a comparison that appropriately holds the relevant demonstrated performance and assessment conditions constant. For example, under a specified common task $Y$, grading bias might be investigated by asking whether:

$$
E[G\mid Y=y,SES=A]
\neq
E[G\mid Y=y,SES=B]
$$

This conditional comparison is informative only if $Y$ sufficiently captures the performance that should legitimately determine the grade and relevant confounding has been addressed. Experiments that randomly assign socioeconomic information to identical work provide particularly direct evidence about the effect of that information on evaluator judgments. Observational studies generally require additional assumptions. This distinction strengthens the broader argument because it prevents the critique of GPA from resting on an overly broad claim that every difference in grades is a measurement artifact. The more defensible conclusion is that **grades are influenced by multiple mechanisms, and observing their numerical values does not by itself identify which mechanisms generated the differences.**

### Why Normalizing GPAs Does Not Fully Resolve Comparability

One possible response to institutional grading differences is to normalize GPA relative to an institution's grade distribution. For example, instead of comparing raw GPAs, one might compare standardized scores:

$$
Z_{ij}
=
\frac{G_{ij}-\mu_j}{\sigma_j}
$$

where $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ denote the mean and standard deviation of GPA within institution $j$. This transformation describes how far an observed GPA lies from the institution's mean, measured in institution-specific standard deviations. Such normalization can help identify a student's relative standing within a particular reference population. However, it does not establish equivalence of knowledge across institutions. A standardized GPA remains dependent on the characteristics of the reference population and the institution's grading practices. If two institutions differ in curricular demands or student selection, the same standardized score does not necessarily correspond to the same underlying competence. Moreover, normalization cannot recover distinctions between dimensions of knowledge that were never assessed or were compressed by the original grading procedure. Thus, statistical normalization may improve certain forms of relative comparison without resolving the deeper problems of construct equivalence and measurement invariance. This is analogous to the distinction between ranking individuals within a group and measuring an absolute attribute on a common scale. A student's percentile or standardized GPA can be precisely calculated while its interpretation as subject-matter competence remains uncertain.

### What Would Be Required for More Meaningful Comparisons?

The preceding difficulties do not imply that cross-institutional comparisons are impossible. They indicate that meaningful comparison requires evidence beyond the observed GPA. Several forms of additional information could improve interpretation. First, curricula and learning objectives can be examined to establish whether the programs evaluate sufficiently similar competencies. Second, assessment instruments can be compared with respect to content coverage, difficulty, grading criteria, and the dimensions of knowledge they emphasize. Third, common examinations or carefully designed linking procedures can provide information about the relationship between different assessment systems. Fourth, relevant student characteristics and selection mechanisms can be considered when comparing institutional grade distributions. Finally, subject-specific performance evidence can be used to supplement GPA, especially when evaluating competencies that differ substantially across programs.

The objective is not necessarily to construct a universal conversion formula. In many settings, the more appropriate solution is to acknowledge that institutional grades provide different kinds of information and to interpret them in relation to the specific decision being made. For example, evaluating candidates for a quantitative economics research position may require direct evidence of mathematical modeling, statistical reasoning, and econometric proficiency. GPA can remain part of the evaluation, but the substantive information relevant to the position may be better represented by the courses completed and the individual's demonstrated technical abilities. Similarly, evaluating conceptual economic understanding may require assessments emphasizing model interpretation, economic intuition, and the application of theory to unfamiliar circumstances. The importance of additional evidence increases when the decision requires distinctions that GPA is poorly designed to identify.

### Implications for Individual-Level Comparisons

The combined effects of institutional heterogeneity, selection, socioeconomic circumstances, and grading standards substantially complicate the interpretation of differences between individual GPAs. Suppose Student A has a 3.9 GPA and Student B has a 3.5. If both students completed the same curriculum, were evaluated under similar standards, and faced comparable assessment conditions, the difference may provide useful evidence about their relative academic performance. If they attended different institutions, completed different courses, and were assessed using substantially different procedures, the same numerical difference becomes more difficult to interpret. The uncertainty arises not only from measurement error but also from differences in what the grades represent. We cannot generally conclude:

$$
G_A>G_B
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
K_A>K_B
$$

without additional assumptions linking the grading systems to a shared competence construct. Even when the underlying grade functions are positively related to knowledge, different measurement regimes may produce overlapping distributions of grades for students with different levels or profiles of competence. Consequently, the probability that the higher-GPA student possesses greater relevant knowledge depends on the context. A meaningful comparison should therefore incorporate the institution, curriculum, assessment regime, and other relevant evidence rather than treating the observed GPA as an independently sufficient statistic.

### Conclusion: Comparability Must Be Established, Not Assumed

Institutional grades reflect both the characteristics of the students being evaluated and the conditions under which they are evaluated. Differences in curricula, scoring practices, historical standards, and student selection can all affect the observed distributions; socioeconomic circumstances may influence knowledge acquisition, performance conditions, or evaluation of comparable work. A common grade-point scale does not distinguish among these mechanisms. Consequently, comparing two GPAs as indicators of knowledge requires more than comparing their numerical values. Percentile transformations or institution-specific standardization may clarify relative standing, but they do not by themselves establish equivalence of subject competence. Carefully designed common assessments and linking procedures can sometimes improve comparability, yet these require evidence of a shared construct and adequate calibration. **Comparability must be established for the proposed inference rather than presumed from identical grade labels.** This is a problem even under fixed assessment regimes; the additional complication is that those regimes and the behavior of their participants can change in response to incentives.

## V. The Incentive Problem: Goodhart's Law and Strategic Optimization

The limitations considered so far can arise even when participants simply follow established assessment procedures. GPA introduces a further difficulty because it is not a passive observation: students, instructors, and institutions respond to the rewards and consequences attached to grades. Scholarships, admissions, employment, course evaluations, enrollment, and institutional performance expectations can all influence how academic outcomes are pursued and produced. A student can raise their GPA through genuine learning, but also through course selection, narrow preparation for predictable questions, or effort concentrated on heavily weighted assignments. An institution can raise grades by improving instruction or by changing thresholds, assessment difficulty, and curricular composition. Those routes may generate similar numerical changes while implying different changes in knowledge, and they need not involve dishonesty. **When an indicator becomes an objective of strategic behavior, the relationship between the indicator and what it was meant to reveal can itself change.** Goodhart's law and Campbell's law provide a framework for understanding this endogenous measurement problem.

### Goodhart's Law: When an Indicator Becomes a Target

Goodhart's law originated in monetary economics, where Charles Goodhart examined the instability of statistical relationships when economic authorities attempted to use particular monetary indicators as policy targets. The broader principle is that a statistical relationship that is useful for monitoring a system may cease to behave in the same way once participants adapt their behavior to the policy that targets it. The commonly used formulation—that a measure can become less useful when it becomes a target—captures the intuition, although it is a generalization of Goodhart's original argument rather than a statement that every target necessarily loses all informational value. The underlying issue is the distinction between a metric's **observational relationship** with an objective and the relationship that emerges when agents are incentivized to optimize the metric itself. Suppose $K_i$ denotes an individual's underlying knowledge and $M_i$ denotes an observable performance indicator. Under an initial measurement regime, we might approximate their relationship as:

$$
M_i=\alpha+\beta K_i+\varepsilon_i
$$

where:

- $M_i$ represents the observed indicator.
- $K_i$ represents the underlying attribute of interest.
- $\alpha$ represents an intercept.
- $\beta$ represents the statistical relationship between knowledge and the indicator.
- $\varepsilon_i$ represents other influences on the observation.

This equation describes a relationship under a particular set of conditions. It does not establish that the same relationship will remain stable under every possible incentive structure. Now suppose the indicator becomes an important determinant of desirable outcomes. Students receive rewards for obtaining higher grades, and they respond by selecting behaviors that increase the expected value of the indicator. Some behaviors increase knowledge and therefore improve grades through the intended measurement relationship. Others increase grades through channels that do not produce equivalent changes in knowledge. The measurement process may then be more appropriately represented as:

$$
M_i=\alpha+\beta K_i+\gamma P_i+\varepsilon_i
$$

where $P_i$ represents behaviors that improve the measured outcome independently of the particular knowledge construct being evaluated. These behaviors might include strategic course selection, exploiting predictable assessment formats, or concentrating on activities that receive unusually high grading weights. The term $\gamma P_i$ does not necessarily represent cheating or misconduct. It represents performance-enhancing behavior whose contribution to the metric is not fully explained by improvement in the target attribute. If students increasingly optimize these behaviors, the distribution of $P_i$ changes. The statistical relationship between grades and knowledge may change with it. A grade that previously provided relatively strong evidence of knowledge may become less informative if a greater proportion of its variation is attributable to strategies that improve the score without comparable improvements in understanding. The crucial implication is that **the relationship between the proxy and the underlying objective is not necessarily invariant to the incentives created by using that proxy.** This is a dynamic problem. The observer cannot simply assume that a measurement relationship established under one behavioral environment will remain valid after the indicator becomes a high-stakes target.

### Campbell's Law and the Corruption of Social Indicators

A closely related argument appears in Donald Campbell's work on social indicators and program evaluation. In *Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change* (1979), Campbell argued that the use of quantitative indicators in consequential social decisions creates pressures that can distort both the indicators themselves and the processes they are intended to monitor. Campbell's argument is especially relevant to education because academic assessment is embedded in a system of institutional rewards, expectations, and incentives. Grades do not merely communicate information about student performance. They help determine access to scholarships, academic programs, professional opportunities, and institutional recognition. As the consequences associated with grades increase, students and institutions have greater incentives to influence the observed outcomes. This introduces two related forms of distortion. The first concerns the indicator itself. Participants may change their behavior in ways that improve recorded performance without equivalently improving the underlying educational objective. The second concerns the educational process. Instruction, curriculum design, and student effort may increasingly be organized around producing favorable indicators rather than achieving the broader purposes for which those indicators were originally introduced.

For example, an instructor facing strong pressure to produce high examination scores may narrow instruction to the particular kinds of problems likely to appear on the examination. Students may become more proficient at those problems, and the resulting grades may accurately reflect that proficiency. But if the instructional emphasis displaces broader conceptual understanding, the improvement in the indicator may overstate the improvement in the educational objective. Campbell's law is therefore particularly important because it identifies the possibility that measurement incentives can alter not only the interpretation of an outcome, but also the underlying process that produces it. The distinction is subtle but consequential. A grading system may begin as an attempt to evaluate learning. Once grades become sufficiently important, learning activities can become organized around satisfying the grading system. The instrument then becomes part of the causal process it was originally intended to observe.

### Formalizing the Student's Optimization Problem

The incentive problem can be developed through a simple model of student behavior. Suppose a student has a limited amount of time and effort that can be allocated between two activities:

1. Developing knowledge and durable understanding of the subject.
2. Improving expected grades through strategies that do not generate equivalent gains in the target knowledge.

Let $L_i$ represent effort devoted to learning and $P_i$ represent effort devoted to grade-oriented strategies. For simplicity, suppose the student's underlying knowledge evolves according to:

$$
K_i=K_{i0}+aL_i
$$

where $K_{i0}$ is initial knowledge and $a>0$ represents the effectiveness of learning effort. Let the student's numerical performance indicator be:

$$
M_i=\beta K_i+\gamma P_i+b_j+\varepsilon_i
$$

where:

- $\beta>0$ represents the sensitivity of the indicator to knowledge.
- $\gamma\geq0$ represents the effectiveness of grade-oriented strategies.
- $b_j$ represents the influence of the grading regime.
- $\varepsilon_i$ represents residual variation.

This is an illustrative model of an underlying performance index, not a claim that the bounded GPA scale is literally linear. Suppose the student values both the rewards associated with the indicator and the intrinsic benefits of knowledge. A simplified utility function is:

$$
U_i=\lambda M_i+\eta K_i-C(L_i,P_i)
$$

where:

- $\lambda$ represents the value placed on obtaining a favorable measured outcome.
- $\eta$ represents the value placed directly on knowledge.
- $C(L_i,P_i)$ represents the cost of effort.

This formulation makes clear that improving knowledge and improving the metric are not necessarily identical objectives. Substituting the equations for knowledge and measured performance yields:

$$
U_i=
\lambda\left[
\beta(K_{i0}+aL_i)+\gamma P_i+b_j+\varepsilon_i
\right]
+
\eta(K_{i0}+aL_i)
-
C(L_i,P_i)
$$

The attractiveness of each activity depends on how effectively it improves the metric, how much knowledge it produces, and the costs associated with pursuing it. Now suppose the student faces a fixed effort budget:

$$
L_i+P_i=E_i
$$

where $E_i$ is the total effort available. Ignoring stochastic variation and costs that are constant under this fixed-budget assumption, the marginal payoff from allocating an additional unit of effort to learning is:

$$
\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial L_i}
=
(\lambda\beta+\eta)a
$$

The corresponding marginal payoff from allocating effort to grade-oriented strategies is:

$$
\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial P_i}
=
\lambda\gamma
$$

Under these deliberately simplified linear assumptions, the student favors grade-oriented effort when:

$$
\lambda\gamma>(\lambda\beta+\eta)a
$$

The interpretation is straightforward. If grade-oriented strategies improve the rewarded indicator more efficiently than genuine learning does, and the reward associated with the indicator is sufficiently important, the student has an incentive to allocate effort away from the development of underlying knowledge. To see how the stakes matter, rearrange the inequality:

$$
\lambda(\gamma-\beta a)>\eta a
$$

If $\gamma>\beta a$, increasing the importance of the grade can eventually make grade-oriented behavior more attractive than learning, even for someone who values knowledge independently. The critical reward level in this simplified model is:

$$
\lambda^*=\frac{\eta a}{\gamma-\beta a}
$$

For $\lambda>\lambda^*$, the marginal incentive favors the grade-oriented activity. This result depends on the model's assumptions, including the fixed effort budget and linear returns. It does not establish that all students respond this way, or that every increase in grading stakes reduces learning. Rather, it identifies the conditions under which the incentive structure can produce a divergence between optimizing the metric and improving the underlying objective. In a more realistic setting, returns to effort are likely diminishing, students differ in preferences, and some study strategies improve both grades and understanding. The optimal allocation may therefore be interior rather than concentrated entirely in one activity. Nevertheless, the central implication survives: **the allocation of effort depends partly on what the grading system rewards, and that allocation can change the relationship between observed grades and acquired knowledge.**

### Productive Optimization Versus Metric Gaming

An important qualification is that strategic responses to grading incentives are not inherently undesirable. A student who studies harder because a high grade is important may acquire substantially more knowledge than they otherwise would have. In such a case, the incentive is aligned with the educational objective. Similarly, students who complete additional practice problems, seek feedback, improve their time management, or devote more attention to difficult material may increase both grades and competence. The relevant distinction is not whether an action is motivated by grades. It is whether the action improves the attribute the grade is supposed to represent. Consider several possible strategies:

| Student behavior | Expected effect on GPA | Relationship to underlying knowledge |
|---|---|---|
| Developing conceptual understanding | Often positive | Directly improves relevant knowledge |
| Practicing applications and unfamiliar problems | Often positive | May improve transfer and competence |
| Memorizing answers to predictable questions | Potentially positive | May produce narrow, limited learning |
| Allocating effort toward highly weighted assignments | Potentially positive | Depends on the content and weighting |
| Selecting a more generously graded course | Potentially positive | Does not itself establish greater knowledge |
| Avoiding difficult but educationally valuable coursework | Potentially positive | May reduce exposure to relevant knowledge |
| Exploiting grading loopholes | Potentially positive | May have little relationship to learning |

These examples illustrate why grade optimization cannot be classified uniformly as productive or unproductive. The effect depends on the alignment between the activity being rewarded and the underlying educational objective. A well-designed assessment system can make productive learning strategies the most effective means of achieving high grades. A poorly aligned system may reward narrow procedural proficiency or strategic behavior that generates higher scores without corresponding improvements in the broader competence being evaluated. Thus, Goodhart's law does not establish that grades inevitably lose their meaning when students care about them. It establishes a vulnerability: the informational relationship between grades and knowledge depends partly on how participants respond to the incentives created by the grading system.

### Course Selection as a Form of Strategic Optimization

One particularly important mechanism is the selection of courses. Students do not generally receive a randomly assigned set of courses and instructors. Within institutional constraints, they choose electives, concentrations, course sequences, and sometimes instructors. These choices may reflect intellectual interests, career objectives, academic preparation, or scheduling constraints. They may also reflect expected grades. If a student is attempting to maximize GPA, the anticipated difficulty and grading standards of available courses become relevant to the decision. Suppose student $i$ can select from courses $c\in\mathcal C_i$. A simplified course-selection problem is:

$$
c_i^*
=
\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal C_i}
\left[
\lambda E(G_{ic}\mid\mathcal I_i)
+\eta E(\Delta K_{ic}\mid\mathcal I_i)
-C_{ic}
\right]
$$

where:

- $E(G_{ic}\mid\mathcal I_i)$ represents the expected grade, conditional on the student's available information.
- $E(\Delta K_{ic}\mid\mathcal I_i)$ represents the expected learning benefit.
- $C_{ic}$ represents the anticipated cost of completing the course.
- $\lambda$ and $\eta$ represent the importance of grades and learning.
- $\mathcal I_i$ denotes the information available when the choice is made.

The course maximizing expected grade value need not be the course maximizing expected learning. If the grade component receives sufficiently high weight, a student may rationally avoid challenging courses despite their potential educational value. The student is not necessarily behaving irrationally or dishonestly. They are responding to an environment in which GPA influences important future opportunities. This produces a compositional effect on GPA. A student may improve their expected average grade by changing the distribution of courses they complete, even if their underlying competence within any given domain does not improve. The effect is particularly relevant when different departments and instructors exhibit persistent differences in grading standards.

### Empirical Evidence: Grades Influence Course Selection

The economics of education provides direct evidence that grading incentives can affect students' curricular choices. Sabot and Wakeman-Linn (1991), in *Grade Inflation and Course Choice*, examined grading patterns across nine colleges and universities and analyzed the relationship between grading policies and enrollment decisions at Williams College. They documented substantial differences in grading practices across departments and investigated how these differences influenced course selection. Their analysis highlighted a tension between institutional educational objectives and the incentives students face when deciding which courses to take.

For example, an institution may wish to encourage enrollment in mathematically demanding disciplines, while a student concerned about GPA may perceive those courses as imposing greater risks to the academic record. This creates a potential divergence between the educational value of coursework and its attractiveness under the grading system. Further evidence comes from Bar, Kadiyali, and Zussman (2009), in *Grade Information and Grade Inflation: The Cornell Experiment*. Cornell University began publishing course median grades online in 1998. The policy was intended to provide more information about grading practices and help contextualize academic performance. The researchers found that providing this information induced students to select more leniently graded courses. They also found that the tendency to select leniently graded courses was weaker among higher-ability students, and that the resulting changes in course selection contributed to increasing average grades.

The study is particularly instructive because the intervention primarily changed the information available to students about the grading environment. Making grading standards more visible altered student behavior, which in turn influenced observed grade outcomes. This illustrates that the measurement system is not independent of the people being measured. Students adapt their choices to the rules and information available to them, and those choices affect the distribution of the recorded metric. A related analysis by Hernández-Julián and Looney (2016), *Measuring Inflation in Grades: An Application of Price Indexing to Undergraduate Grades*, examined data from a large public university between 1982 and 2001. The researchers estimated that approximately one-quarter of the observed increase in average grades was attributable to changes in students' course choices, including a shift toward departments that historically assigned higher grades.

Another approximate quarter was associated with changes in observable student characteristics, including academic preparation. Less than half was attributed to the remaining unexplained component, which the authors characterized as grade inflation in their decomposition. This is important for two reasons. First, an increase in average grades can arise partly from changes in the composition of courses taken, rather than improvements in performance within a fixed collection of courses. Second, even a well-documented increase in average GPA does not identify a unique underlying cause. The observed metric reflects the combined influence of student characteristics, course selection, grading regimes, and other factors. The research does not establish that every movement toward more generously graded courses represents gaming. Students may have legitimate academic reasons for making such choices. It does establish that course-selection behavior matters for interpreting grade distributions, and that the observed average is endogenous to the composition of academic choices.

### High-Stakes Thresholds and Discontinuous Incentives

Letter grades introduce another interesting incentive problem because their consequences are often discontinuous. Suppose an A requires a numerical score of at least 90%, while a B corresponds to scores immediately below that threshold. A student scoring 89% and another scoring 90% may possess very similar levels of demonstrated competence, yet the resulting letter grades differ. If scholarship eligibility, admissions decisions, or GPA thresholds depend on those categories, the difference in external rewards may be substantial. Let a numerical score be $S_i$, and let the letter grade be:

$$
G_i=Q(S_i)
$$

The reward associated with the grade is then:

$$
V_i=V(Q(S_i))
$$

If the reward for an A exceeds that for a B, the payoff function may contain a discontinuity at the grade boundary. A student near the boundary therefore has a strong incentive to allocate effort toward crossing the threshold. Some of that effort may produce genuine learning. But other responses may involve negotiating for additional points, concentrating narrowly on grade-relevant tasks, or selecting activities most likely to affect the final classification. The concern is not that thresholds are inherently inappropriate. Clearly specified standards often require boundaries. Rather, the classification can create behavioral incentives that depend on proximity to a numerical cutoff rather than on the educational significance of the corresponding improvement. This can lead to a situation in which relatively small differences in measured performance have large consequences for incentives and decision-making. The effect also reinforces the distinction between an underlying continuous competence profile and the categorical evaluations used to represent it.

### Instructor Incentives and the Grading Process

Strategic responses to grades are not limited to students. Instructors operate within institutional environments that may reward or penalize particular teaching and assessment outcomes. Student evaluations of teaching, course enrollment, retention, departmental expectations, and administrative performance measures can all affect the incentives surrounding course design and grading practices. Consider the relationship between grading leniency and student evaluations. If students systematically prefer courses in which they anticipate favorable grades, instructors may face incentives to adjust their evaluation standards. A simplified instructor objective might be represented as:

$$
U_j
=
\rho E_j+\zeta L_j-C_j(R_j,T_j)
$$

where:

- $U_j$ represents the instructor's objective function.
- $E_j$ represents the teaching evaluations or institutional rewards received.
- $L_j$ represents student learning.
- $R_j$ represents the grading rules.
- $T_j$ represents the assessment and instructional design.
- $C_j$ represents associated costs.
- $\rho$ and $\zeta$ represent the weights attached to evaluations and learning.

This is an illustrative representation, not a claim that instructors consciously maximize a single numerical utility function. If teaching evaluations respond positively to expected grades, an instructor may face an incentive to make grading more generous. Ewing (2012), in *Estimating the Impact of Relative Expected Grade on Student Evaluations of Teachers*, investigated this relationship using University of Washington data. The study found evidence consistent with incentives for instructors to grade leniently in response to the relationship between students' expected grades and teaching evaluations, while also demonstrating the importance of accounting for unobserved instructor and departmental characteristics.

Related research by Stroebe (2016, 2020) has argued that institutional reliance on student evaluations may create incentives for grading leniency and reduced academic demands. These findings should be interpreted carefully. A positive relationship between student grades and teaching evaluations does not automatically demonstrate that instructors manipulate grades. Students may receive high grades because they were taught effectively, and effective teaching may also produce favorable evaluations. There may also be unobserved differences in course content, instructor quality, and student composition. Nevertheless, when evaluation outcomes carry institutional consequences, the possibility of strategic responses becomes relevant to interpreting the resulting grades. The central point is not that instructors necessarily behave improperly. It is that the measurement function itself can respond to incentives.

### Institutional Incentives and the Endogeneity of Grading Standards

The same logic extends to colleges and universities. Institutions may face pressures relating to student retention, graduation rates, recruitment, rankings, and the employment or graduate-school outcomes of their students. Some institutional responses can improve education. Better instruction, academic support, tutoring, advising, and curriculum design may enable students to learn more effectively and earn higher grades. Other responses can increase recorded academic outcomes without producing equivalent improvements in knowledge. For example, changing grade thresholds or altering the weighting of assignments can increase average GPA without requiring a corresponding change in student competence. Let the grading function used by an institution at time $t$ be:

$$
G_{it}=f_t(\mathbf K_{it},T_t,R_t,C_{it})
$$

If the institution changes its assessment design $T_t$ or grading rules $R_t$, observed grades may change even if the underlying distribution of competence remains constant. To isolate this possibility, consider a counterfactual in which student knowledge is held fixed:

$$
\mathbf K_{i,t+1}=\mathbf K_{it}
$$

A change in institutional rules can still generate:

$$
f_{t+1}(\mathbf K_i,T_{t+1},R_{t+1},C_i)
\neq
f_t(\mathbf K_i,T_t,R_t,C_i)
$$

The same competence state may therefore produce a different recorded grade under a new evaluation regime. This is a direct challenge to interpreting changes in GPA distributions as changes in academic knowledge. An increase in grades may reflect improved learning, more generous standards, changes in curriculum, changes in student composition, or some combination of these mechanisms. The numerical observation does not identify which mechanism occurred. Moreover, these incentives can create feedback across the educational system. Students may gravitate toward courses that offer favorable grade outcomes. Departments may experience enrollment changes as a consequence. Instructors may respond to enrollment and evaluation pressures. Institutions may modify policies in response to broader performance indicators. As the participants adapt, the process generating GPA can evolve. Consequently, the mapping between grades and knowledge may not remain stable even within the same institution over time.

### The Measurement Function as an Endogenous Object

The preceding examples suggest a more general formulation. In the earlier sections, the grading function was treated as a mapping from knowledge, assessment conditions, and institutional rules to an observed grade. However, when individuals strategically respond to the grading system, their behavior becomes a function of the rules and incentives. Let $\pi$ represent the incentive regime governing the consequences of grades. A student's strategy can be represented as:

$$
A_i^*=A_i^*(\pi,R_j,\mathcal I_i)
$$

where:

- $A_i^*$ is the student's chosen behavior.
- $\pi$ represents the reward structure.
- $R_j$ represents the grading rules.
- $\mathcal I_i$ represents the information available to the student.

The resulting grade is:

$$
G_{ij}
=
f_j\left(
\mathbf K_i,
A_i^*(\pi,R_j,\mathcal I_i),
C_i,
R_j
\right)
$$

The observed grade now depends not only on underlying knowledge and the assessment instrument, but also on the behavioral response induced by the measurement regime. If the incentive structure changes, the strategy may change. Even holding knowledge and formal grading rules constant, the conditional distribution of observed grades may differ:

$$
P(G\mid\mathbf K,R,\pi_1)
\neq
P(G\mid\mathbf K,R,\pi_2)
$$

This inequality is a possibility, not a universal necessity. It arises when the incentives alter performance-related behavior not already captured by the conditioning variables. The implication is that the observed indicator cannot always be interpreted independently of the institutional environment in which it becomes consequential. If instructors and institutions also modify their rules in response to incentives, the grading function itself may change:

$$
f_j=f_j(\pi)
$$

In that case, both the behavior of the measured individuals and the measurement procedure are endogenous to the incentive system. This produces a dynamic feedback process: **Grading rules and rewards → student and institutional behavior → observed grade distributions → decisions based on grades → renewed incentives and adaptations.** The importance of this feedback process is that statistical relationships estimated before an incentive change may not remain valid afterward. A GPA that was strongly associated with particular competencies under one regime may become less informative if the new regime rewards behaviors that improve grades through different channels.

### Why a Stable Historical Relationship May Not Remain Stable

Suppose that GPA historically predicts subject-matter knowledge reasonably well. This relationship might be established through a regression of independently assessed knowledge on GPA:

$$
K_i=\alpha+\beta G_i+u_i
$$

The estimated coefficient $\beta$ summarizes an empirical relationship in a particular population under a particular educational and incentive regime. But if students and institutions change their behavior, the conditional relationship may change. More explicitly, write:

$$
E[K_i\mid G_i,\pi]
=
m_\pi(G_i)
$$

where $m_\pi$ is the conditional expectation of knowledge given GPA under incentive regime $\pi$. There is no general reason to assume:

$$
m_{\pi_1}(G)=m_{\pi_2}(G)
$$

for every GPA value across different incentive regimes. A GPA of 3.8 might correspond to a different distribution of underlying knowledge after substantial changes in course-selection behavior, assessment design, or institutional grading practices. The same problem applies to comparisons across populations exposed to different incentives. For example, students in programs where GPA strongly influences access to competitive opportunities may employ different academic strategies from students in environments where numerical grades carry fewer consequences. Even if the formal grading scale remains the same, the behaviors generating the observed grades may differ. This means that the predictive or diagnostic value of GPA should not be treated as automatically transportable across institutional environments or historical periods. Its validity must be evaluated in relation to the conditions under which the observations are generated. This is an important extension of the comparability problem discussed in Section IV. There, the measurement regimes differed across institutions and populations. Here, we recognize that the regimes and the behavior of participants can evolve in response to the use of the metric itself.

### Evidence That Changing Grade Incentives Changes Behavior

The converse of the grade-optimization problem is also informative: what happens when the stakes associated with letter grades are reduced? Butcher, McEwan, and Weerapana (2024), in *Making the (Letter) Grade: The Incentive Effects of Mandatory Pass/Fail Courses*, examined a policy introduced at Wellesley College in 2014. The institution required first-year students in their first semester to take courses on a pass/fail basis, although instructors continued recording underlying letter grades for research and administrative purposes.

The researchers used the policy change to investigate the effect of grading incentives on course selection and performance. They found that students shifted toward lower-grading STEM courses during their first semester. However, this did not translate into increased engagement with STEM courses in later semesters. They also found that underlying letter-grade performance declined by approximately 0.13 grade points, or 23% of a standard deviation. After considering alternative explanations, the authors concluded that the findings were consistent with a reduction in student effort. These results are important because they demonstrate that changes in grading incentives can affect both academic choices and observed performance. However, the interpretation is not simply that reducing grade pressure improves education or that increasing it necessarily distorts learning.

The policy appears to have encouraged some students to explore courses they might otherwise have avoided, while also reducing effort as inferred from their performance. These effects can move in different directions. Grades may provide productive incentives for study, yet simultaneously discourage educationally valuable risk-taking and induce strategic course selection. Thus, the effects of grading incentives cannot be evaluated along a single dimension. A system may encourage effort while also promoting narrow optimization. Likewise, reducing grading pressure may improve some aspects of educational exploration while weakening incentives for others. The relevant question is not whether grades motivate behavior—they clearly can—but whether the resulting behavior is aligned with the educational objectives we want the system to promote.

### The Distinction Between Measurement Error and Incentive-Induced Distortion

It is useful to distinguish the incentive problem from the measurement problems developed in earlier sections. Measurement error concerns the discrepancy between an intended measurement and its observed outcome. Construct underrepresentation concerns whether the instrument adequately covers the relevant dimensions of the target attribute. Aggregation concerns which information is lost when multiple observations are reduced to a scalar. Strategic optimization introduces a different issue: **the behavior generating the observation changes because of the consequences attached to the indicator.** These problems are related but analytically distinct. Consider a student who memorizes a set of predictable problem-solving procedures and subsequently receives an A. The examination may have been administered correctly. The scoring rubric may have been applied consistently. The numerical grade may accurately reflect the student's performance.

Yet if the student would struggle to apply the underlying principles to unfamiliar problems, the A may be a limited indicator of generalizable understanding. Now suppose many students discover that memorizing such procedures is the most efficient way to obtain high grades. The assessment begins to reward increasingly specialized preparation, and student behavior adapts accordingly. At this point, the issue is no longer only that the examination measures a narrow domain. The prevalence of behavior optimized for that narrow domain may also increase, changing the relationship between grades and broader competence. Thus, a measurement instrument can become less useful as an indicator of an underlying objective even while continuing to measure its immediate scoring criteria accurately. The phenomenon can be summarized as follows:

| Problem | What changes or fails? | GPA illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Random measurement error | The observation varies imperfectly around its target | Idiosyncratic examination performance |
| Systematic measurement distortion | Scoring depends on factors outside the intended construct | Instructor-specific grading effects |
| Representation failure | The assessment omits or compresses relevant dimensions | Computation assessed without conceptual foundations |
| Strategic optimization | Participants change behavior to improve the indicator | Course selection or narrow exam preparation |
| Institutional adaptation | The rules producing the indicator change | Grading thresholds or assessment policies change |

Importantly, the last two problems can arise even if the first two are substantially reduced. A precisely recorded grade can still become a less informative proxy if the incentive structure causes the participants to optimize aspects of performance that are only imperfectly related to the educational objective.

### The Possibility of Improved Alignment

The argument should not be taken to imply that grade-based incentives are inherently counterproductive. In principle, a well-designed grading system can align the incentives of students and instructors with genuine learning. If high grades require conceptual understanding, successful transfer to unfamiliar problems, robust technical competence, and retention over time, optimizing for those grades may encourage precisely the behaviors the institution intends. The distinction can be expressed in terms of the relationship between changes in the indicator and changes in the underlying knowledge state. For a particular action $a$, consider:

$$
\Delta M(a)
=
M(a)-M(a_0)
$$

and:

$$
\Delta K(a)
=
K(a)-K(a_0)
$$

where $a_0$ represents a baseline behavior. An action that increases both quantities contributes to the intended alignment between metric and objective. An action for which:

$$
\Delta M(a)>0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\Delta K(a)\approx0
$$

raises the indicator without a corresponding improvement in the target knowledge. And an action that improves the indicator while reducing some important dimension of competence would represent a more serious misalignment. This suggests that the problem is not the existence of grading incentives, but the degree to which the incentives reward improvements in the relevant underlying attributes. The closer the assessment is aligned with the intended learning objectives, the less opportunity there may be to improve grades through strategies that do not meaningfully improve competence. Nevertheless, complete alignment is difficult when the underlying construct is multidimensional, assessment time is limited, and students can adapt to known examination formats. Even sophisticated assessments can become vulnerable to narrow preparation when their content and scoring procedures are predictable. A robust educational evaluation system must therefore consider not only whether an examination measures knowledge under existing conditions, but also how its use may influence the behavior of students and institutions over time.

### Implications for the Interpretation of GPA

The foregoing analysis introduces an additional source of uncertainty into GPA-based inference. GPA reflects academic performance under a set of grading rules, but the observed outcomes are partly shaped by the incentives associated with those rules. A high GPA may result from extensive learning, strong preparation, and sustained academic effort. It may also be influenced by strategic course selection, adaptation to assessment formats, the allocation of effort toward high-weight activities, or institutional grading practices. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. An individual's academic record may reflect several of them simultaneously. The observed GPA does not independently identify their respective contributions. This complicates the inference from the metric to the underlying knowledge state.

Even if two students possess similar academic abilities, they may respond differently to the incentives surrounding grading. One may take difficult courses, accept a greater risk of lower grades, and develop substantial competence in demanding areas. Another may select courses and assessment environments that maximize the probability of high grades. The resulting GPA ordering may partly reflect different strategies rather than a corresponding ordering of disciplinary understanding. This does not establish that the second student is less knowledgeable, nor that the first student's choices are necessarily better. It establishes that the GPA comparison is conditional on the choices and evaluation regimes that produced it. Furthermore, the strategic behavior of individuals can affect aggregate institutional outcomes.

If students increasingly select leniently graded courses, average GPA can rise even without equivalent improvements in knowledge. If instructors or institutions adjust grading standards, the same distribution of underlying competence may generate a different distribution of recorded grades. The observed indicator is therefore jointly determined by academic abilities, assessment procedures, incentives, and the adaptive behavior of participants. This creates an important limitation on both cross-sectional and longitudinal interpretations of GPA. A numerical difference between individuals, or a historical increase in average grades, cannot automatically be attributed to an equivalent difference or improvement in knowledge. Additional evidence is needed to distinguish genuine educational outcomes from the effects of strategic adaptation.

### Conclusion: GPA Is an Endogenous Indicator

Goodhart's law and Campbell's law help explain why grading is not merely a passive measurement of academic performance. Grades carry consequences, and students, instructors, and institutions respond to those consequences. Some responses—more effective instruction, sustained preparation, and substantive learning—improve the educational objective itself; others can raise recorded grades through narrower preparation, course selection, or changes to grading practices without an equivalent gain in the intended knowledge. These possibilities may coexist, and the observed GPA does not independently identify their contributions. The resulting indicator is therefore potentially endogenous to the incentive system. A grade can be scored accurately and remain reliable under its own rules while becoming a less informative proxy for broader competence as participants learn which behaviors raise the metric. This does not imply that all high grades reflect gaming or that grade incentives are necessarily counterproductive. **It means that validity must be evaluated in the behavioral and institutional environment where the measure is used**, including how that environment changes over time. The same tension appears in other fields where a convenient headline indicator becomes a stand-in for a more complex system.

## VI. The General Problem of Proxy Metrics: Lessons From Other Domains

The difficulties surrounding GPA are instances of a general problem in quantitative evaluation: a statistic can be accurate for its intended purpose yet incomplete for a broader judgment. Economists use national income and product accounts to measure economic activity, but GDP alone does not describe the distribution of welfare. Clinicians obtain precise physiological measurements without treating any one of them as a complete account of health. Reliability engineers distinguish average lifetime from the probability that a component survives a specified operating interval. These examples help separate two criticisms often conflated in discussions of GPA. An instrument may measure its designated quantity imperfectly; alternatively, it may measure that quantity very well while omitting dimensions essential to the question being asked. **The general error is to treat a partial representation as a sufficient account of the underlying system.** Improving calibration or consistency cannot, by itself, repair an inference that reaches beyond the information the statistic contains.

### A Metric as a Projection of a Multidimensional System

Many phenomena of interest are better represented as collections of interrelated attributes than as single quantities. Consider a system whose state is described by a vector:

$$
\mathbf X=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\
x_2\\
\vdots\\
x_n
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The components may represent different characteristics of an economy, a person's health, the reliability of an engineered system, or an individual's disciplinary competence. A summary indicator is constructed by applying some measurement or aggregation function:

$$
M=f(\mathbf X)
$$

where $M$ is the resulting scalar measure. This transformation reduces the dimensionality of the information used to describe the system. In many cases, different configurations of the underlying attributes can generate the same indicator. Formally, there may exist distinct system states such that:

$$
\mathbf X_A\neq\mathbf X_B
$$

while:

$$
f(\mathbf X_A)=f(\mathbf X_B)
$$

The mapping is then many-to-one. This has an immediate implication: observing the indicator does not uniquely identify the underlying system state. Two economies may possess identical GDP per capita while differing substantially in income distribution, household financial security, and environmental conditions. Two patients may exhibit identical blood pressure readings while possessing very different broader health profiles. Two components may have the same expected lifetime while exhibiting different probabilities of failure over a specified interval. Likewise, two students may possess identical GPAs while having different combinations of conceptual understanding, mathematical proficiency, empirical reasoning, and applied knowledge. The important issue is not simply that the summary omits details. It is that some of the omitted details may be essential to the question the observer is attempting to answer.

#### Representation and decision relevance

Suppose the objective is to evaluate some property of the system, represented by:

$$
U(\mathbf X)
$$

where $U$ is an evaluative or decision-relevant function. The summary indicator $M=f(\mathbf X)$ will adequately preserve the relevant information only under additional conditions. For example, a particularly strong condition would be that the evaluative function can be expressed entirely in terms of the indicator:

$$
U(\mathbf X)=h(f(\mathbf X))
$$

for some function $h$. Under this condition, knowing the indicator would be sufficient to determine the particular evaluation $U$. But there is no general reason to assume that such a relationship exists. If two system states have the same metric but different values of the evaluative function, then the metric is insufficient for that purpose:

$$
f(\mathbf X_A)=f(\mathbf X_B)
$$

while:

$$
U(\mathbf X_A)\neq U(\mathbf X_B)
$$

The indicator may remain useful for other purposes. Its inadequacy is specific to the broader evaluation being attempted. Similarly, even when one system has a higher metric value than another, the ordering need not be preserved by the decision-relevant function:

$$
M_A>M_B
\;\not\Rightarrow\;
U(\mathbf X_A)>U(\mathbf X_B)
$$

This distinction is central to interpreting GPA. A higher GPA establishes a higher value on the institutionally defined academic performance index. It does not automatically establish a higher value on every reasonable measure of subject-matter competence. The inference requires additional assumptions about the relationship between the reported statistic and the attributes relevant to the evaluation.

### Gross Domestic Product and the Measurement of Economic Conditions

One of the clearest examples of indicator overinterpretation occurs in macroeconomics. Gross domestic product is among the most important and extensively developed measures in economic statistics. It is produced within the standardized framework of the National Income and Product Accounts (NIPAs), which provide detailed information about economic production, income, consumption, saving, and investment. GDP is designed to measure the value of final goods and services produced within an economy over a specified period. Under the expenditure approach, it can be represented as:

$$
GDP=C+I+G+NX
$$

where:

- $C$ represents personal consumption expenditures.
- $I$ represents gross private domestic investment.
- $G$ represents government consumption expenditures and gross investment.
- $NX$ represents net exports of goods and services.

This is an accounting identity, not a model of economic welfare. The conceptual distinction matters. GDP is a measure of production, assembled from extensive underlying economic data. Its purpose is comparatively well-defined, and the measurement process is supported by established national accounting conventions. The difficulty arises when observers interpret the resulting statistic as though it were a comprehensive representation of general economic conditions or the well-being of the population. Economic production and economic welfare are related, but they are not equivalent. An economy can experience increasing output while particular groups experience stagnating real incomes, deteriorating affordability, or worsening financial insecurity.

GDP may increase because production rises in sectors whose gains are concentrated among relatively few individuals. It may also increase alongside environmental degradation or changes in other conditions that are relevant to social welfare but not directly represented by the measure. These possibilities do not establish that GDP is an inaccurate measure of production. They establish that production is not equivalent to every aspect of economic well-being. Dale Jorgenson (2018), in *Production and Welfare: Progress in Economic Measurement*, emphasizes precisely this distinction. GDP was developed to measure production, yet the absence of a single comprehensive welfare measure has encouraged its use as a proxy for broader economic welfare. The Bureau of Economic Analysis similarly distinguishes GDP and GDP per capita from broader measures of economic well-being. The implication is straightforward: **a well-defined measurement can be used to answer a question it was never designed to answer.**

#### The information concealed by an aggregate

Consider two hypothetical economies with the same population and identical total production. Their average output per person may therefore be identical. But suppose economic gains are distributed differently. In Economy A, income growth is relatively broad-based. In Economy B, the same aggregate increase is concentrated among high-income households. The headline aggregate may be identical even though the implications for the typical household differ. The difference can be formalized by distinguishing aggregate output from the distribution of household resources. Let household income be represented by a vector:

$$
\mathbf Y=
\begin{bmatrix}
y_1\\
y_2\\
\vdots\\
y_N
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The mean is:

$$
\overline y=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_i
$$

But distinct distributions can share the same mean. For example:

$$
\mathbf Y_A=
\begin{bmatrix}
50\\
50
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf Y_B=
\begin{bmatrix}
10\\
90
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Both have:

$$
\overline y_A=\overline y_B=50
$$

Yet their distributions are substantially different. These are illustrative income distributions, not a claim that household income is identical to GDP. They demonstrate the general statistical problem underlying the interpretation of economic aggregates. The average does not preserve information about dispersion, inequality, or the experiences of particular members of the population. To evaluate the broader economic condition, the observer must decide which aspects of the economy matter and consult measures that describe those aspects.

#### The importance of a portfolio of economic indicators

The NIPAs themselves contain far more information than headline GDP growth. Even within national accounting, analysts can examine consumption, investment, saving, income, sectoral contributions, and other components of economic activity. Additional datasets describe employment, unemployment, earnings, price changes, wealth distribution, household debt, and other dimensions of economic conditions. Which indicators are relevant depends on the question. If the objective is to measure the growth of domestic production, real GDP is highly informative. If the objective is to evaluate the purchasing power of the typical household, real household income and relevant price measures may be more directly informative. If the objective is to assess economic security, information about employment, financial obligations, household balance sheets, and the distribution of resources becomes important.

If the objective is to evaluate the sustainability of economic activity, other indicators may be necessary. There is no contradiction in using different measures for different questions. The mistake is to assume that one measure adequately represents all of them. This provides an important analogy to GPA. An institutionally defined GPA may be informative about accumulated academic performance. But determining whether an individual understands economic theory, possesses mathematical proficiency, or can conduct empirical research requires evidence about those specific competencies. Just as headline GDP cannot substitute for an entire analysis of economic conditions, GPA cannot automatically substitute for an evaluation of the relevant dimensions of knowledge.

### Health Indicators and the Multidimensional Nature of Health

A similar problem arises when evaluating individual health. Clinical measurements often describe particular physiological attributes with considerable precision. Blood pressure, blood glucose, laboratory measurements, and other indicators can provide important information about specific bodily functions. However, no single physiological indicator constitutes a comprehensive measure of an individual's health. Consider blood pressure. A blood pressure measurement describes a particular aspect of cardiovascular physiology under specified conditions. It can provide clinically relevant information, but it does not independently determine the state of every organ system, the individual's functional capacity, or the presence or absence of unrelated health conditions. Two individuals with identical blood pressure readings may differ substantially in other dimensions of health. We can represent a hypothetical health state as:

$$
\mathbf H_i=
\begin{bmatrix}
H_{i,\text{cardiovascular}}\\
H_{i,\text{metabolic}}\\
H_{i,\text{respiratory}}\\
H_{i,\text{functional}}\\
H_{i,\text{other}}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

A particular clinical measurement observes some function of this broader state:

$$
M_i=g(\mathbf H_i,C_i)
$$

where $C_i$ represents the conditions under which the measurement is obtained. The observed value may be highly informative about selected components of the health state while providing limited information about others. The relevant distinction is between measuring a physiological variable and evaluating the broader condition of the individual. A blood pressure reading can be accurate without constituting a sufficient description of health. This is not a criticism of blood pressure measurement. It is a criticism of assigning that measurement a broader interpretation than its evidential relationship supports.

#### Why multiple measurements matter

Clinical assessment commonly combines measurements, symptoms, medical history, and observations over time. Different sources of evidence address different aspects of the problem. This can be understood as an attempt to reduce uncertainty about the underlying state by observing multiple functions of it:

$$
M_1=g_1(\mathbf H)
$$

$$
M_2=g_2(\mathbf H)
$$

$$
\vdots
$$

$$
M_p=g_p(\mathbf H)
$$

If these measurements capture distinct aspects of the underlying state, their combination may provide more information than any individual observation. However, the advantage depends on the measurements selected and the relationships among them. Several highly correlated indicators may provide less additional information than a smaller collection of measurements covering genuinely different domains. Likewise, repeated observations of one physiological variable cannot necessarily reveal conditions unrelated to that variable. The same principle applies to education. Administering many examinations that emphasize the same narrow class of computational procedures may produce a reliable estimate of computational performance. It does not necessarily provide a comprehensive assessment of conceptual understanding, empirical reasoning, and the ability to transfer knowledge to unfamiliar problems. The objective should therefore be to select measurements that adequately cover the attributes relevant to the evaluation, rather than simply maximizing the number of observations.

### Health-System Performance as a Multidimensional Evaluation Problem

The distinction becomes even clearer at the level of healthcare systems. Evaluating the performance of a healthcare system requires more than measuring one outcome, such as life expectancy, expenditure per person, or the number of patients treated. A system may perform well in one dimension and poorly in another. For example, a healthcare system may provide high-quality care to patients who successfully obtain treatment while presenting substantial barriers to access. Another may achieve broad access while experiencing difficulties with timeliness, efficiency, or the consistency of care. A single indicator may conceal these differences. The World Health Organization's 2022 *Health System Performance Assessment: A Framework for Policy Analysis* addresses this problem by examining multiple functions and objectives of healthcare systems. The framework connects four major system functions—governance, financing, resource generation, and service delivery—with five performance goals: health improvement, people-centredness, financial protection, efficiency, and equity. This approach recognizes that system performance is not adequately captured by one headline statistic. A simplified representation might be:

$$
\mathbf H_{\text{system}}=
\begin{bmatrix}
H_{\text{health improvement}}\\
H_{\text{people-centredness}}\\
H_{\text{financial protection}}\\
H_{\text{efficiency}}\\
H_{\text{equity}}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The components do not necessarily share a natural common unit. Evaluating the system therefore requires determining how each dimension contributes to the objectives of interest. An aggregate health-system performance score could be constructed, but that aggregation would introduce assumptions about how different objectives are weighted and whether strong performance in one area can compensate for deficiencies in another. For example, exceptionally high efficiency does not necessarily justify inadequate access to essential care. The same issue arises when aggregating academic performance. High grades in some courses may compensate arithmetically for weak grades in others, but whether that compensation is substantively appropriate depends on the competencies required for the decision. A high GPA may conceal serious deficiencies in a foundational area of knowledge. Thus, the problem is not simply numerical aggregation. It is the substantive justification for allowing different dimensions to compensate for one another.

### Mean Time to Failure: Why a Mean Cannot Represent a Failure Distribution

Reliability engineering provides an especially clear mathematical illustration of the limitations of summary statistics. One commonly used measure is mean time to failure (MTTF). For a nonrepairable component, MTTF represents the expected time until failure. Let $T$ denote the nonnegative random variable describing the component's lifetime. Then:

$$
MTTF=E[T]
$$

For a continuous lifetime distribution with density $f(t)$:

$$
E[T]=\int_0^\infty t f(t)\,dt
$$

The mean provides information about the expected lifetime of the component. But it does not uniquely identify the distribution of failure times. Different lifetime distributions can possess the same expectation while exhibiting very different probabilities of failure at particular times.

#### Two components with identical MTTF

Consider two hypothetical types of components. Component A always fails at exactly 1,000 operating hours:

$$
P(T_A=1000)=1
$$

Component B has a 50% probability of failing at 100 hours and a 50% probability of failing at 1,900 hours:

$$
P(T_B=100)=0.5
$$

$$
P(T_B=1900)=0.5
$$

Both components have identical mean times to failure. For Component A:

$$
E[T_A]=1000
$$

For Component B:

$$
E[T_B]
=
0.5(100)+0.5(1900)
=
1000
$$

Thus:

$$
MTTF_A=MTTF_B=1000
$$

An observer relying exclusively on the mean would find the components indistinguishable on this statistic. Yet their failure behavior is substantially different.

| Property | Component A | Component B |
|---|---|---|
| MTTF | 1,000 hours | 1,000 hours |
| Failure pattern | Always fails at 1,000 hours | Half fail at 100 hours, half at 1,900 |
| Survives beyond 500 hours | 100% | 50% |
| Survives beyond 1,500 hours | 0% | 50% |
| Variability of lifetime | None in this hypothetical model | Substantial |

The mean conceals differences that could be critical to the engineering decision. If the component must reliably operate for 500 hours, Component A is preferable in this example. If the objective concerns survival beyond 1,500 hours, Component B has a higher probability of remaining operational. Neither comparison contradicts the equality of their mean lifetimes. The two components simply possess different lifetime distributions, and different decisions depend on different parts of those distributions.

#### The reliability function

Reliability engineering addresses this problem by examining the survival function:

$$
R(t)=P(T>t)
$$

where $R(t)$ represents the probability that a component remains operational beyond time $t$. For Component A:

$$
R_A(t)=
\begin{cases}
1 & t<1000\\
0 & t\geq1000
\end{cases}
$$

For Component B:

$$
R_B(t)=
\begin{cases}
1 & t<100\\
0.5 & 100\leq t<1900\\
0 & t\geq1900
\end{cases}
$$

At 500 hours:

$$
R_A(500)=1
$$

$$
R_B(500)=0.5
$$

At 1,500 hours:

$$
R_A(1500)=0
$$

$$
R_B(1500)=0.5
$$

The survival probabilities differ despite identical expected lifetimes. This demonstrates that MTTF is not sufficient to determine reliability at a particular operating duration. Indeed, for a nonnegative lifetime variable with finite expectation, MTTF can be expressed as the integral of the survival function:

$$
E[T]=\int_0^\infty R(t)\,dt
$$

The mean is therefore one summary of the entire survival function. Different survival functions can possess the same integral. The NIST/SEMATECH *Engineering Statistics Handbook* explicitly distinguishes reliability functions, lifetime distributions, failure rates, and expected lifetimes because these quantities answer different questions about system performance.

#### Variance and the shape of the distribution

The difference can also be illustrated through lifetime variance. For Component A:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(T_A)=0
$$

For Component B:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(T_B)
=
0.5(100-1000)^2+
0.5(1900-1000)^2
$$

Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(T_B)=810000
$$

The units are squared hours. Both components have the same mean, but their variances differ dramatically. Even the combination of mean and variance would not generally determine the entire failure-time distribution. More detailed information about distributional shape, tail behavior, and operating conditions may remain necessary. The essential point is that **the appropriate measure depends on the reliability question being asked**. If we want to estimate expected lifetime, MTTF is directly relevant. If we need to determine the probability that a system survives a particular mission duration, the reliability function at that duration is more directly relevant. If we are evaluating a repairable system, other measures such as repair time and availability may matter. For a simple repairable system under suitable steady-state assumptions, availability can be represented as:

$$
A=
\frac{MTBF}{MTBF+MTTR}
$$

where MTBF denotes mean time between failures and MTTR denotes mean time to repair. This formula concerns a different type of system and objective than the MTTF example, but it illustrates how additional dimensions become necessary when the evaluation shifts from component lifetime to operational availability. No single statistic is universally sufficient for every reliability decision.

### What Reliability Engineering Reveals About GPA

The analogy to academic grading is direct. MTTF is not meaningless because it fails to determine every aspect of reliability. It remains an informative statistic about expected lifetime. Likewise, GPA is not meaningless because it fails to determine every aspect of knowledge. It remains an informative summary of recorded academic performance. The problem emerges when an observer assumes that the summary statistic adequately characterizes the broader system. Consider two students with identical GPAs. One student may have consistently earned moderately high grades across courses. Another may have earned exceptionally high grades in some subjects and substantially lower grades in others. Their means may be identical, but their distributions of performance differ. Whether those differences matter depends on the purpose of the assessment. A research position requiring advanced mathematical competence may favor the student who demonstrated exceptional performance in quantitative courses.

A role requiring balanced competence across several domains may favor a different academic profile. The average does not answer the decision question by itself. The same reasoning applies to students with different GPAs. An individual with a higher average may nevertheless demonstrate weaker competence in a particular essential subject. Just as the component with the higher expected lifetime need not be the component with the greater probability of surviving a specified mission duration, the student with the higher GPA need not be the student with the stronger competence in every relevant domain. The appropriate statistic depends on which aspect of performance we are trying to evaluate. This is the general principle shared by reliability engineering and academic assessment: **a summary statistic must be interpreted relative to the property and decision for which it is informative, rather than treated as a universal ranking of the underlying system.**

### Measurement Quality and Representational Sufficiency Are Different Problems

The preceding examples also clarify an important distinction between two forms of criticism. One criticism concerns the quality of the measurement itself. An instrument may be inconsistently calibrated, subject to evaluator bias, or unable to reproduce its results under comparable conditions. These are problems of instrumentation, reliability, and measurement error. A separate criticism concerns whether the measurement contains enough information to support a broader inference. GDP may be measured with considerable methodological rigor while remaining inadequate as a complete welfare indicator. Blood pressure may be measured accurately while providing only partial information about individual health. MTTF may be calculated exactly from a known failure-time distribution while failing to determine reliability at a specified operating duration.

Similarly, a course grade or GPA may be calculated consistently while remaining insufficient to establish an individual's complete competence profile. These examples help distinguish two questions: **First, is the statistic an accurate measure of what it is explicitly designed to measure?** **Second, is what it measures sufficient for the broader conclusion we wish to draw?** An affirmative answer to the first does not establish an affirmative answer to the second. This is especially important for GPA because defenders of the measure may respond to criticism by emphasizing standardized grading scales, consistency, or statistical correlations with subsequent academic outcomes. Such evidence can be valuable, but it does not necessarily resolve the representation problem. The deeper question is whether GPA preserves the information required to evaluate the particular attribute of interest. A perfectly measured proxy can remain an incomplete proxy.

### Formalizing the Information Lost Through Summary Statistics

The relationship between a multidimensional system and its summary indicator can also be considered in information-theoretic terms. Suppose $\mathbf X$ represents the detailed observable state of a system, $M=f(\mathbf X)$ represents a deterministic summary, and $Y$ represents an outcome or attribute relevant to a decision. Because $M$ is constructed from $\mathbf X$, the data-processing inequality implies:

$$
I(Y;M)\leq I(Y;\mathbf X)
$$

where $I(\cdot\,;\cdot)$ denotes mutual information. Under the assumed joint distribution, a deterministic summary cannot contain more information about $Y$ than the complete observations from which it was constructed. However, equality is possible. A summary statistic can preserve all information relevant to a particular target even while discarding other details. The important question is therefore not whether aggregation always loses information in some abstract sense, but whether it loses information relevant to the specific inference or decision. For a summary to be sufficient for predicting $Y$ from $\mathbf X$, a strong condition is:

$$
P(Y\mid\mathbf X)=P(Y\mid M)
$$

Under this condition, once the summary indicator is known, observing the remaining details of $\mathbf X$ provides no additional information about the target outcome. This condition may be plausible for some narrowly defined questions. For example, if a decision genuinely depends only on expected component lifetime, MTTF may be an appropriate summary. But it is generally not sufficient for a question about the probability of surviving a particular operating interval. Similarly, GPA may be useful for predicting some kinds of academic outcomes. Yet there is no general reason to assume that it preserves all information from a student's transcript, examinations, projects, and other demonstrated work that is relevant to assessing subject-matter knowledge. The full information set may allow substantially more precise conclusions than the aggregate alone. This distinction provides a rigorous formulation of the intuitive problem: **a metric's informational sufficiency is relative to the question being asked.** A statistic need not be comprehensive to be useful. But using it as though it were comprehensive requires evidence that it preserves the relevant information.

### Why a Portfolio of Metrics Can Provide a Better Assessment

One response to the limitations of individual indicators is to examine several measurements that capture different aspects of the underlying system. In macroeconomic analysis, this may involve examining production, income, employment, inflation, investment, and distributional indicators. In healthcare, it may involve considering physiological measurements, clinical outcomes, symptoms, functional capabilities, and longitudinal evidence. In reliability engineering, it may involve examining expected lifetime, survival probabilities, failure rates, repair times, and the consequences of failure. In education, it may involve examining conceptual understanding, technical competence, empirical reasoning, independent work, and performance on unfamiliar problems. We can formalize a portfolio of measurements as:

$$
\mathbf M=
\begin{bmatrix}
M_1\\
M_2\\
\vdots\\
M_p
\end{bmatrix}
$$

where each measurement is generated by a potentially different function of the underlying state:

$$
M_j=g_j(\mathbf X)+\varepsilon_j
$$

The objective is to obtain observations that provide complementary information about the system. However, simply increasing the number of metrics does not necessarily improve the quality of the assessment. If the measurements are highly redundant, share systematic biases, or repeatedly examine the same narrow dimension, the additional information may be limited. For example, several examinations emphasizing nearly identical mathematical procedures may provide more reliable evidence about computational proficiency without substantially improving our understanding of the student's conceptual competence. The selection of metrics should therefore be guided by the attributes relevant to the evaluation.

#### The problem of aggregation reappears

A portfolio of metrics does not automatically eliminate the need to make judgments about their relative importance. If several indicators are ultimately combined into one score, the aggregation procedure introduces a new weighting function. For example:

$$
Q=\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_jM_j
$$

where the weights $w_j$ represent the relative contributions of the indicators. This aggregation may be useful, but it requires justification. The weights determine which dimensions dominate the resulting evaluation and which deficiencies can be compensated by strong performance elsewhere. In some decisions, compensation may be appropriate. In others, minimum competency requirements may be necessary. A student with exceptional mathematical ability but no foundational understanding of a critical economic concept may not satisfy the requirements of a particular task, regardless of their overall performance index. A reliability system with excellent average lifetime but an unacceptable probability of catastrophic early failure may likewise fail to satisfy an operational requirement. The appropriate decision rule depends on the purpose of the evaluation. Consequently, the objective should not necessarily be to replace GPA with a different universal scalar. It may be more informative to preserve the multidimensional assessment profile and apply decision-specific criteria.

### The Difference Between Evaluating a System and Ranking It

Another problem arises when indicators are used to rank systems or individuals. A ranking imposes an ordering. But multidimensional systems may not admit a unique ordering unless we establish how different attributes should be weighted. Suppose two students possess competence profiles $\mathbf K_A$ and $\mathbf K_B$. If Student A performs better in some dimensions while Student B performs better in others, neither necessarily dominates the other across the entire competence space. An unambiguous componentwise ordering exists when:

$$
K_{Am}\geq K_{Bm}
\qquad
\forall m
$$

with strict inequality for at least one dimension. In such a case, Student A is at least as proficient on every measured dimension and more proficient on at least one. But when the profiles cross—one student being stronger in some areas and weaker in others—an overall ranking depends on the chosen evaluative criteria. The same is true of healthcare systems, economic conditions, and engineered systems. One system may achieve higher average performance while another offers greater reliability under adverse conditions. One economy may have higher aggregate production while another has better outcomes on a particular distributional indicator. Without specifying what matters for the comparison, a universal ranking may be poorly defined. GPA creates an apparently unambiguous ordering because it assigns every student a single numerical value. But the clarity of the numerical ordering does not establish that the ordering is substantively appropriate for every interpretation of knowledge. The ranking is generated by the aggregation convention. Its relevance to an external decision remains a separate question.

### A General Taxonomy of Indicator Overinterpretation

The cross-domain examples reveal several recurring forms of error.

| Indicator | Legitimate measurement target | Potentially unjustified broader inference |
|---|---|---|
| GDP | Economic production | Overall economic welfare or prosperity for every group |
| Blood pressure | A specific physiological condition | Comprehensive individual health |
| Health-system performance indicator | A particular system outcome or function | Overall system quality |
| MTTF | Expected time until failure | Reliability under every operating condition |
| GPA | Aggregate recorded academic performance | General knowledge, intelligence, or disciplinary mastery |

Although these indicators differ substantially in their measurement quality, the same inferential problem can arise in each case. The observer moves from a statement about what the measure actually represents to a stronger statement about a broader construct. That transition may be reasonable when supported by evidence. But it cannot be justified merely by pointing to the numerical measurement. The distinction can be summarized through three levels:

1. **Measurement:** What quantity does the indicator record?
2. **Representation:** What aspects of the broader system does that quantity preserve?
3. **Inference:** What conclusions about the broader system are justified by the observation?

A failure at any stage can undermine the final interpretation. And these failures are not equivalent. An instrument can measure its intended quantity poorly. Alternatively, it can measure that quantity accurately while representing only a small part of the broader phenomenon. Or it can provide a useful representation while being used to support conclusions that exceed its evidential scope. These distinctions are particularly important in the context of GPA because problems may occur at all three levels.

### The Additional Problem of Optimization

The cross-domain comparison also helps connect the representation problem to Goodhart's law. An indicator can be incomplete even when no one attempts to manipulate it. MTTF does not fully describe reliability even when component lifetimes are recorded without error. GDP does not comprehensively measure welfare even when national accounts are compiled according to established standards. The limitation exists because the indicator captures only a particular aspect or summary of a larger system. Goodhart's law adds a further complication. When an indicator becomes a consequential target, the behavior of the system may change in ways that alter the relationship between the indicator and the broader objective.

For instance, an institution rewarded for improving a particular performance metric may focus disproportionately on activities that raise that metric. This may produce genuine improvement when the indicator is well aligned with the objective. But it can also encourage the neglect of dimensions that are unmeasured or given insufficient weight. Thus, two distinct problems can compound. First, the metric may provide an incomplete representation of the system. Second, optimization may encourage participants to improve the measured dimensions at the expense of unmeasured ones. The result is a system whose headline indicators improve without necessarily producing an equivalent improvement in the broader objective. This is particularly relevant to academic grading.

A GPA may initially summarize several dimensions of academic performance. But when the GPA becomes an important target, students and institutions may increasingly prioritize activities that raise the numerical average. The informational relationship between the indicator and broader competence can therefore be affected both by the original limitations of the assessment and by the behavioral responses it induces. The combined problem is not merely that the indicator loses information. It is that the system may reorganize itself around the information the indicator happens to preserve.

### Implications for Evaluating GPA

The preceding examples suggest that the question of GPA's usefulness cannot be answered independently of its purpose. If the objective is to summarize how successfully a student met the graded requirements of their courses, GPA is a meaningful administrative index. If the objective is to predict performance in subsequent academic environments, GPA may have useful predictive validity. If the objective is to determine whether a student understands a particular domain of knowledge, however, the appropriate evidence may need to be considerably more specific. A student may possess a high GPA without mastering some important aspects of a discipline.

Another may possess a lower GPA while demonstrating exceptional competence in the dimensions relevant to a particular task. These possibilities are not anomalies that necessarily invalidate GPA. They arise from the general limitations of reducing heterogeneous, multidimensional information into a scalar. The appropriate response is therefore not to reject quantitative indicators categorically. It is to evaluate their scope, their measurement properties, and their relevance to the question being asked. In many cases, richer evidence about particular competencies will be more informative than a single aggregate. This is analogous to consulting the broader national accounts rather than inferring an economy's condition from GDP growth alone, examining a health profile rather than one physiological measurement, or evaluating reliability over a specified operating interval rather than relying exclusively on average lifetime. The principle is the same across domains: **the appropriate measurement depends on the attribute and decision of interest.**

### Conclusion: A Statistic Is Not the System It Summarizes

The comparisons with national accounts, clinical measurements, and reliability engineering establish a distinction that is easy to overlook: a statistic may be accurately and consistently measured while remaining insufficient for evaluating the broader system. GDP measures production without fully capturing welfare; a clinical reading describes a particular physiological dimension rather than overall health; and MTTF summarizes an expected lifetime without determining survival probabilities at every operating duration. Each measure can be useful precisely because its scope is well defined.

GPA is similar in this respect. It summarizes recorded performance, but it does not reconstruct the distribution of competencies or all the circumstances under which that performance was assessed. More indicators can help when they cover genuinely complementary domains, although combining them into a new scalar reintroduces weighting and compensability assumptions. Nor does a clean numerical ranking necessarily yield an unambiguous ranking on a multidimensional objective. **The appropriate indicator is determined by the question, and its accuracy does not establish its sufficiency for another question.** The next issue is the uncertainty remaining when we use the GPA observation to infer a particular individual's knowledge.

## VII. The Inferential Problem: What Can We Conclude About an Individual?

The preceding measurement and representation problems converge on a statistical question: **given an observed grade or GPA, what can we infer about the knowledge of the particular person who received it?** This is different from asking whether grades are informative on average. A measure may predict meaningful differences across a population yet leave substantial uncertainty about an individual's actual competence, and a positive population association need not preserve every pairwise ranking. The issue becomes consequential when grades are used to rank applicants or make judgments about intellectual ability. Such decisions require moving backward from an observed outcome to a latent state that is not directly visible. To establish what that inference warrants, we must distinguish the forward process that generates grades from the inverse process of recovering knowledge, specify the required statistical assumptions, and examine how uncertainty changes between population-level relationships and individual-level conclusions.

### The Forward Measurement Process and the Inverse Inference Problem

A grade is generated through a process in which an individual's knowledge interacts with an assessment instrument, evaluation conditions, and grading rules. For simplicity, let $K_i$ represent a scalar attribute of knowledge that has been sufficiently defined for the particular evaluation. This assumption does not imply that knowledge is universally one-dimensional; it allows us to isolate the inferential problem before returning to the multidimensional case. Suppose the observed grade is generated by:

$$
G_i=f(K_i,C_i,R_i)+\varepsilon_i
$$

where $C_i$ represents the conditions under which performance is elicited, $R_i$ represents the relevant assessment and grading regime, and $\varepsilon_i$ captures residual influences. This is the *forward problem*: given an underlying knowledge state and information about the assessment environment, what grades might we expect to observe? In educational assessment, the observer typically faces the reverse question. The grade is known, but the underlying knowledge state is not. We observe:

$$
G_i=g
$$

and seek to infer:

$$
K_i=?
$$

The distinction matters because the existence of a forward relationship does not establish that it can be uniquely inverted. A function may map different knowledge states to the same grade. Additionally, random variation in performance and scoring may cause students with similar knowledge to receive different outcomes. Consequently, observing a grade does not generally identify a unique knowledge state. A higher grade can be evidence of greater knowledge without determining that knowledge precisely. The strength of the inference depends on how informative the measurement process is, how much uncertainty the observation eliminates, and whether the relevant assessment conditions are known. This is an ordinary problem of statistical inference, but it becomes particularly difficult when the measurement process is heterogeneous and partially unobserved.

### A Bayesian Interpretation of Grades as Evidence

Bayesian inference provides a useful framework for distinguishing the informational value of a grade from the certainty of the conclusions drawn from it. Suppose we are interested in the underlying knowledge state $K$ and observe a grade $G$ under contextual conditions $C$. Bayes' theorem gives:

$$
P(K\mid G,C)
=
\frac{P(G\mid K,C)P(K\mid C)}
{P(G\mid C)}
$$

For continuous knowledge variables, the corresponding expression uses probability densities. The terms have distinct interpretations. The likelihood, $P(G\mid K,C)$, describes how probable the observed grade is for individuals possessing a particular knowledge state under the specified assessment conditions. The prior, $P(K\mid C)$, represents the distribution of plausible knowledge states before observing the grade, conditional on the relevant population and context. The posterior, $P(K\mid G,C)$, describes how those beliefs change after observing the grade. This formulation reveals why the meaning of a grade cannot be separated from the circumstances under which it was generated.

An A on a demanding examination that comprehensively evaluates the relevant material may provide strong evidence of subject competence. An A on an assessment that primarily rewards the reproduction of familiar procedures may provide less information about broader conceptual understanding. The observed letter is identical, but the likelihood functions differ. Moreover, even when the likelihood is known, the posterior depends on the reference population. An A may have different implications in populations with different distributions of prior preparation and knowledge. This does not make inference arbitrary. It means that inference is conditional on the statistical relationships connecting observations to the attributes being evaluated. The mistake occurs when an observer treats the grade as though it directly reveals the knowledge state without considering those relationships.

### Why a Grade Can Be Informative Without Being Conclusive

A simple hypothetical example illustrates this distinction. Suppose we classify students into two knowledge categories for a particular subject: high competence, denoted $H$, and lower competence, denoted $L$. Assume that 20% of students in the relevant population possess high competence:

$$
P(H)=0.20
$$

Suppose the probability of receiving an A is 80% for a highly competent student and 30% for a student in the lower-competence group:

$$
P(A\mid H)=0.80
$$

$$
P(A\mid L)=0.30
$$

The assessment is informative because an A is substantially more likely among highly competent students. However, observing an A does not establish that the student belongs to the high-competence group. Applying Bayes' theorem:

$$
P(H\mid A)
=
\frac{P(A\mid H)P(H)}
{P(A\mid H)P(H)+P(A\mid L)P(L)}
$$

Substituting the hypothetical values:

$$
P(H\mid A)
=
\frac{0.80(0.20)}
{0.80(0.20)+0.30(0.80)}
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(H\mid A)=0.40
$$

The observation doubles the probability assigned to high competence, from 20% to 40%. That is a meaningful informational improvement. Nevertheless, the majority of students receiving an A would still belong to the lower-competence group under the assumptions of this particular example. The result is not an empirical estimate of actual grading accuracy. It demonstrates the distinction between an observation being positively associated with an attribute and that observation being sufficient to identify the attribute confidently. The same relationship can be expressed through posterior odds:

$$
\frac{P(H\mid A)}{P(L\mid A)}
=
\frac{P(A\mid H)}{P(A\mid L)}
\cdot
\frac{P(H)}{P(L)}
$$

The first ratio on the right-hand side is the likelihood ratio associated with observing an A. In the example:

$$
LR_A=\frac{0.80}{0.30}\approx2.67
$$

Receiving an A multiplies the prior odds of high competence by approximately 2.67. This is useful evidence, but it does not settle the question. If the prior distribution changed while the likelihood function remained the same, the posterior probability would also change. For example, if 70% of the relevant population possessed high competence, observing an A would imply:

$$
P(H\mid A)
=
\frac{0.80(0.70)}
{0.80(0.70)+0.30(0.30)}
\approx0.86
$$

Thus, the same grade under the same assessment likelihood can support different posterior conclusions depending on the reference population. The general lesson is that **a grade's evidential significance depends on its ability to discriminate among knowledge states, not merely on the numerical or categorical value assigned to the student.**

### The Problem of Unknown Assessment Conditions

The preceding Bayesian example assumes that the relationship between knowledge and grades is known. In practice, observers often lack sufficient information about the assessment regime to characterize that relationship. Suppose we observe a 3.8 GPA but know little about the courses completed, the examination methods, the instructor's standards, or the dimensions of competence evaluated. We cannot automatically assign a particular likelihood function to the observation. Let $R$ represent the assessment regime and $C$ the relevant conditions under which performance was generated. If those factors are unobserved, the likelihood of a grade conditional on knowledge must account for the possible regimes and conditions:

$$
p(g\mid k)
=
\int
p(g\mid k,r,c)
\,p(r,c\mid k)
\,dr\,dc
$$

The integral represents a mixture over the assessment environments that might have generated the observation. For discrete assessment regimes, the corresponding expression is a sum. Importantly, the distribution of regimes need not be independent of knowledge. Students select into programs, courses, and instructors, while educational institutions select students according to different criteria. This means that the observed relationship between GPA and knowledge depends partly on which individuals encounter which measurement processes. If the assessment conditions and selection mechanisms are unknown, the likelihood required to interpret GPA may be difficult to identify. The problem is therefore more serious than the existence of random noise around an otherwise stable measure. We may lack adequate information about the relationship between the observation and the underlying attribute in the first place. In such circumstances, assigning a precise probability to the student's level of knowledge may create an appearance of statistical rigor that the available evidence does not support. Bayesian inference does not eliminate the need for defensible assumptions about the measurement process. It makes those assumptions explicit.

### Observational Equivalence and Failure of Identification

The inferential problem becomes particularly clear when different underlying competence profiles generate the same observation. Return to the multidimensional representation developed in Section III. Let the student's knowledge state be a vector:

$$
\mathbf K_i=
\begin{bmatrix}
K_{i,\text{conceptual}}\\
K_{i,\text{mathematical}}\\
K_{i,\text{empirical}}\\
K_{i,\text{applied}}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Suppose an examination produces a numerical score according to:

$$
S_i=\mathbf a^\top\mathbf K_i
$$

where $\mathbf a$ represents the instrument's weighting of different competence dimensions. Even if the instrument is perfectly reliable and the score is measured without error, the result generally does not uniquely identify the underlying knowledge vector. A single scalar observation cannot ordinarily determine several independently varying attributes. The set of knowledge states consistent with an observed score $s$ can be written as:

$$
\mathcal K(s;\mathbf a)
=
\left\{
\mathbf k:
\mathbf a^\top\mathbf k=s
\right\}
$$

For a multidimensional construct, this set can contain many different competence profiles. If the assessment weights are themselves unknown, the set of plausible underlying knowledge states becomes larger because multiple assessment functions must also be considered. This provides a formal interpretation of observational equivalence: distinct knowledge states are indistinguishable given the available measurement. The financial engineering example illustrates the issue. A student proficient in solving the mathematical problems appearing on an examination may receive a high score despite lacking important conceptual understanding of hedging. Another student may possess stronger conceptual understanding while being less proficient in the specific computational procedures emphasized by the examination.

If the examination does not adequately distinguish these competence profiles, the observed grade cannot determine which student possesses stronger general understanding of financial engineering. The problem is not necessarily that the examination has been administered incorrectly or that the instructor has made an arithmetic error. The problem is that the observation does not contain enough information to identify the broader construct being inferred. Additional assessments can reduce this uncertainty, but only if they provide information about dimensions that were previously unobserved or inadequately distinguished. Repeating the same narrow measurement may produce increasingly reliable observations without resolving the underlying identification problem.

### Population-Level Associations Versus Individual-Level Inference

A central source of overinterpretation is the movement from relationships observed across populations to conclusions about particular individuals. Suppose a study finds a positive association between GPA and an independently assessed measure of subject-matter competence. Across the population, students with higher GPAs may, on average, possess greater competence. A simplified regression relationship might be:

$$
K_i=\alpha+\beta G_i+u_i
$$

where $\beta>0$ indicates a positive population-level association. This is meaningful evidence. It establishes that GPA contains information that may help predict competence. However, the residual term $u_i$ represents differences between the competence predicted from GPA and the actual competence of individual students. Even if the estimated relationship is statistically significant, the magnitude and distribution of the residuals determine how precisely it can support individual-level conclusions. Formally, an estimated conditional expectation:

$$
E[K\mid G=g]=m(g)
$$

describes the expected knowledge associated with a particular grade in the relevant population. It does not establish that every individual with that grade possesses exactly $m(g)$ units of knowledge. Nor does it imply that every student with a higher GPA has greater knowledge than every student with a lower GPA. The distinction can be expressed as:

$$
E[K\mid G=g_2]>
E[K\mid G=g_1]
$$

for $g_2>g_1$, while it may still be true that:

$$
P(K_A<K_B\mid G_A=g_2,G_B=g_1)>0
$$

The average relationship can be positive even though individual rankings are sometimes reversed. How frequently such reversals occur is an empirical question. It depends on the strength of the relationship, the variability of knowledge conditional on GPA, and the comparability of the relevant measurement processes. The existence of a statistically meaningful population relationship therefore does not establish deterministic individual rankings.

### How Uncertainty Affects Pairwise Comparisons

The distinction between population relationships and individual rankings can be developed more explicitly. Suppose two students have observed GPAs satisfying:

$$
G_A>G_B
$$

The question of interest is whether:

$$
K_A>K_B
$$

But the relevant object is a conditional probability:

$$
P(K_A>K_B\mid G_A,G_B,C_A,C_B)
$$

where $C_A$ and $C_B$ represent the contextual information associated with the two students. For scalar knowledge states, this probability can be written as:

$$
\int\!\!\int
\mathbf 1(k_A>k_B)
\,p(k_A,k_B\mid G_A,G_B,C_A,C_B)
\,dk_A\,dk_B
$$

where $\mathbf 1(k_A>k_B)$ equals one when Student A has greater knowledge and zero otherwise. The expression illustrates that a pairwise comparison requires a joint distribution of the underlying knowledge states conditional on the observations. A numerical GPA difference does not independently determine this probability. To illustrate the point more concretely, consider a simplified model in which standardized GPA and standardized knowledge are jointly normally distributed, each with mean zero and variance one. Let their correlation be $\rho$, where:

$$
0<\rho<1
$$

Under the bivariate normal assumption:

$$
K_i\mid G_i=g_i
\sim
N\left(
\rho g_i,
1-\rho^2
\right)
$$

For two independently sampled students from the same population, the difference between their knowledge states, conditional on their GPAs, is normally distributed:

$$
K_A-K_B\mid G_A,G_B
\sim
N\left(
\rho(G_A-G_B),
2(1-\rho^2)
\right)
$$

The probability that Student A possesses greater knowledge is therefore:

$$
P(K_A>K_B\mid G_A,G_B)
=
\Phi\left(
\frac{\rho(G_A-G_B)}
{\sqrt{2(1-\rho^2)}}
\right)
$$

where $\Phi$ denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Suppose the correlation between standardized GPA and knowledge is 0.6, and Student A's standardized GPA is 0.5 standard deviations higher than Student B's. Then:

$$
P(K_A>K_B\mid G_A-G_B=0.5)
=
\Phi\left(
\frac{0.6(0.5)}
{\sqrt{2(1-0.6^2)}}
\right)
$$

This yields approximately:

$$
P(K_A>K_B)\approx0.60
$$

Under the stated hypothetical assumptions, Student A is more likely to possess greater knowledge, but the GPA difference does not provide especially strong evidence for that conclusion. This illustration should not be interpreted as an empirical estimate of actual GPA-based ranking accuracy. It imposes a common standardized scale, a unidimensional knowledge construct, a bivariate normal relationship, and comparable measurement conditions. Indeed, several previous sections have questioned whether those assumptions can be justified across actual educational environments. The example nevertheless demonstrates a general statistical point: **even a moderately strong positive population-level correlation can coexist with substantial uncertainty about the relative knowledge of two particular individuals.** It also shows why the size of the observed GPA difference matters. A small difference may support only a modest change in the probability of one student being more knowledgeable than another, particularly when the underlying relationship contains substantial residual variation.

### Empirical Evidence: Predictive Validity and Its Individual-Level Limits

The educational literature provides an instructive example of this distinction. Geiser and Santelices (2007), in *Validity of High-School Grades in Predicting Student Success Beyond the Freshman Year*, examined the ability of high-school academic records and standardized admissions tests to predict longer-term outcomes at the University of California. The study analyzed a sample of nearly 80,000 students admitted to the university system and examined outcomes including cumulative college grades and graduation.

The researchers found that high-school GPA was consistently a stronger predictor of subsequent college performance than standardized admissions tests. Its predictive relationship remained meaningful across academic disciplines, university campuses, and entering cohorts. This finding provides substantial evidence in favor of GPA as an indicator of later educational outcomes. However, the authors also emphasized the limitations of predicting outcomes for particular individuals. Their most fully specified prediction model, incorporating high-school GPA, standardized test scores, and other information known at admission, explained approximately 30% of the variance in cumulative college GPA. Consequently, approximately 70% of the observed variation remained unexplained by the predictors included in that model. The study also reported considerable uncertainty around individual predictions.

For example, the authors described an approximately 95% prediction band of plus or minus 0.77 grade points around the predicted cumulative college GPA in their best-fitting model. For a student predicted to earn a college GPA of 3.0, this corresponds to a broad range of possible outcomes, approximately from 2.23 to 3.77. This uncertainty is particularly important because it occurs in a setting where the objective is comparatively well-defined: predicting subsequent academic performance using prior academic performance and other observed characteristics. The target is not some broad and difficult-to-operationalize construct such as comprehensive intellectual competence. It is a future academic outcome measured using a familiar grading scale. Yet even in this relatively favorable predictive setting, individual-level uncertainty remains substantial.

There are important limitations to the interpretation of these results. The study concerns students admitted to the University of California during a particular historical period, and its predictive relationships should not automatically be generalized to every institution or population. Moreover, the unexplained variance is not necessarily measurement error, nor does it imply that the grades themselves contain only 30% of the relevant information. Future college performance depends on many influences not known at admission, including subsequent educational experiences, student decisions, and personal circumstances. Nevertheless, the findings illustrate the broader inferential distinction. A measure can be among the strongest available predictors of an outcome while still providing limited precision for predicting that outcome in a particular individual. This is precisely why statistical evidence supporting GPA's predictive usefulness does not justify treating it as a definitive measure of knowledge.

### Predictive Validity Is Not Equivalent to Diagnostic Validity

The distinction becomes even more important when we ask what GPA is being used to predict or diagnose. Consider two propositions. The first is that GPA predicts subsequent academic performance. The second is that GPA accurately measures an individual's understanding of a particular subject. These propositions concern different relationships. The first asks whether an observed academic record contains information about a future outcome. The second asks whether an observed academic record identifies a particular underlying competence. A measure can perform well on the first task while providing incomplete information about the second.

For example, GPA may predict subsequent academic success because it captures persistence, time management, academic preparation, organizational ability, and the capacity to satisfy institutional expectations. These characteristics may contribute meaningfully to later educational outcomes. However, they are not identical to conceptual understanding of economic theory or competence in financial engineering. The distinction can be represented by introducing an outcome $Y_i$ describing future academic performance. Suppose GPA is generated through:

$$
G_i=f(K_i,B_i,C_i)
$$

where $B_i$ represents behavioral and noncognitive characteristics relevant to academic performance. Future academic success may depend on:

$$
Y_i=h(K_i,B_i,Z_i)
$$

where $Z_i$ represents additional circumstances affecting the future outcome. Because $G_i$ and $Y_i$ depend on some of the same underlying characteristics, GPA may be a useful predictor of future performance. But the predictive relationship:

$$
P(Y_i\mid G_i)
$$

does not establish that GPA precisely identifies:

$$
P(K_i\mid G_i)
$$

These are different conditional distributions. The correlation between prior GPA and future academic success may be partly attributable to behavioral characteristics that are valuable for educational achievement but distinct from the particular subject knowledge we want to assess. This is not a defect if the intended decision concerns future academic performance. Indeed, the multidimensional nature of GPA may contribute to its predictive usefulness. The problem emerges when evidence of predictive validity for one outcome is treated as evidence of diagnostic validity for a different construct. **The validity of a measurement-based inference must be evaluated in relation to the particular interpretation and use being proposed.** This principle is consistent with Messick's (1989) account of construct validity and the *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, which emphasize the importance of evidence supporting score interpretations and uses.

### The Limits of Inference From Extreme Grades

The preceding discussion does not imply that every grade provides equally weak evidence of knowledge. Extreme observations may be particularly informative under appropriate conditions. For example, a student who repeatedly fails representative assessments of elementary algebra may be less likely to possess strong algebraic competence than a student who consistently receives excellent marks. Likewise, a student who repeatedly demonstrates exceptional performance on demanding, representative examinations may provide substantial evidence of subject mastery. However, the implications of extreme grades depend on assumptions about the relationship between knowledge and observed performance.

Consider a student who receives an F. The grade may indicate an inability to demonstrate the required knowledge. But it may also arise through missed examinations, incomplete assignments, nonattendance, or other circumstances that do not uniquely identify the student's underlying competence. Similarly, an A may indicate that the student has satisfied demanding proficiency standards, or it may reflect strong performance on a relatively narrow or undemanding assessment. The letter itself does not distinguish these possibilities. To derive meaningful bounds on knowledge from grades, we need assumptions about the measurement function. Suppose a numerical score $S$ is related to a well-defined scalar competence $\theta$ through a known, strictly increasing function:

$$
S=f(\theta)
$$

If the function is invertible and measurement error is absent, observing the score identifies the competence state:

$$
\theta=f^{-1}(S)
$$

If the institution reports only a grade category, the observation identifies a range rather than a unique value. For example, suppose an A is assigned when:

$$
S\geq s_A
$$

Under the assumptions of a strictly increasing measurement function and no measurement error, observing an A establishes:

$$
\theta\geq f^{-1}(s_A)
$$

The grade then provides a meaningful lower bound on the target attribute. But this conclusion depends on the assumptions. If examination performance is affected by unobserved circumstances, if the assessment measures several dimensions of knowledge, or if an A can be obtained through different combinations of competencies, the inferred bound may no longer hold. In a stochastic setting, the interpretation is generally probabilistic rather than deterministic. For example, a high grade may increase the probability of high competence without establishing a strict lower bound. The resulting inference depends on the known or assumed relationship between competence and the probability of receiving the grade. This is why extreme grades can be useful evidence without constituting definitive proof of the individual's underlying knowledge state. The correct interpretation is conditional on the assessment's design, standards, and performance-generation process.

### Identification, Partial Identification, and Uncertainty

The preceding examples suggest that GPA-based inference can fall into several categories. In some circumstances, the available measurement system may identify a particular competence parameter with reasonable precision. This is most plausible when the target construct is clearly specified, the assessment is appropriately calibrated, and sufficient observations are available. In other circumstances, the measurement may only partially identify the underlying state. For example, a grade may rule out some competence levels while leaving a broad range of possibilities compatible with the observation. In still other circumstances, the measurement process may be so poorly specified that even meaningful bounds are difficult to establish. We can represent the set of competence states compatible with an observed grade and a set of assumptions $\mathcal A$ as:

$$
\Theta(G;\mathcal A)
$$

The interpretation depends on the size and structure of this set. If it contains a single value, the target parameter is point-identified under the assumptions. If it contains multiple values but excludes some possibilities, the parameter is partially identified. If the assumptions permit nearly every substantively relevant knowledge state, the observation provides little identifying information. This framework helps clarify the original concern about GPA. The problem is not that grades necessarily contain no evidence. It is that the range of underlying knowledge states compatible with a particular grade may be much broader than people assume. Furthermore, the identified set depends on the assumptions used to interpret the grade. A student receiving an A on a representative, well-calibrated assessment may support stronger restrictions on plausible competence than an A obtained under an unfamiliar grading regime. Without information about the assessment process, those restrictions cannot be confidently established. This is particularly important when comparisons involve students from different programs or institutions, where the measurement functions and relevant competence dimensions may differ.

### Selection, Reference Populations, and the Transportability of Inference

Another limitation concerns whether relationships estimated in one population can be applied to another. Suppose researchers estimate the relationship between GPA and subject competence among students in a highly selective university. The resulting relationship reflects both the assessment environment and the distribution of student characteristics within that population. Applying the same relationship to students in another institutional environment may require assumptions about the comparability of the populations and measurement processes. This is particularly relevant because educational selection is nonrandom. Students enter institutions and academic programs based partly on prior preparation, interests, opportunity, and admission requirements. The selection process influences the distribution of knowledge and other characteristics within the observed population. Formally, let $J$ denote the relevant institutional or population context. Then the conditional distribution of knowledge given GPA may differ across settings:

$$
P(K\mid G,J=A)
\neq
P(K\mid G,J=B)
$$

This inequality may arise even when GPA retains a positive relationship with knowledge in both populations. A GPA of 3.8 may correspond to different distributions of subject competence across programs because the curricula, student populations, and grading regimes differ. Moreover, the strength of an estimated relationship may depend on the range of observed student characteristics. A highly selected population may exhibit less variation in academic preparation than a broader population, influencing the correlations that can be observed and estimated. Statistical adjustments for selection and range restriction may help under appropriate assumptions, but they cannot automatically establish the transportability of a measurement relationship. The implication is that the evidential significance of GPA is not necessarily universal. A relationship established in one educational environment must be evaluated before being transferred to another. This extends the comparability problem from differences between individual measurements to the validity of statistical inferences across populations.

### The Difference Between Correlation and Individual Classification

The inferential problem can also be understood through the distinction between association and classification. Suppose a study reports a strong positive correlation between GPA and an independently measured indicator of competence. This relationship may establish that GPA is useful for predicting average differences in competence across groups. However, a decision-maker evaluating a particular student may wish to classify the student as possessing or lacking a specified level of proficiency. That is a different statistical task. Let $H$ denote possession of a defined competence threshold. A classification rule based on GPA might be:

$$
\widehat H_i=
\begin{cases}
1 & G_i\geq g^*\\
0 & G_i<g^*
\end{cases}
$$

where $g^*$ is the chosen GPA threshold. The quality of this rule depends on several properties, including its sensitivity and specificity. Sensitivity is:

$$
P(\widehat H=1\mid H=1)
$$

Specificity is:

$$
P(\widehat H=0\mid H=0)
$$

A GPA-based threshold may classify many competent students correctly while also admitting some students who do not possess the target competence. Alternatively, it may exclude some students who are competent but received lower GPAs under particular circumstances. The balance between these errors depends on the relationship between GPA and competence, the chosen threshold, and the population being evaluated. For a particular decision, false positives and false negatives may have different consequences. For example, admitting a student into advanced coursework without adequate prerequisite knowledge may create educational difficulties. Conversely, excluding a capable student because of a relatively low GPA may deny an opportunity to someone who could perform successfully. A high correlation does not, by itself, establish that a particular classification threshold is appropriate. The relevant evaluation must consider the actual decision, the distribution of knowledge conditional on GPA, and the consequences of classification errors. This reinforces the distinction between using GPA as an informative predictor and treating it as an authoritative decision rule.

### What Would Make GPA More Informative About Individual Knowledge?

The inferential limitations described here do not make the assessment of individual knowledge impossible. They identify the kinds of additional information needed to support stronger conclusions. First, the target competence must be defined with sufficient precision. If the objective is to evaluate mathematical economics, the assessment must distinguish the relevant mathematical and economic competencies rather than relying on an unspecified notion of general academic achievement. Second, the measurement process must be understood. Information about task selection, assessment difficulty, scoring rules, and grading standards helps establish the likelihood of observing a particular grade conditional on different knowledge states. Third, the assessment should provide sufficient coverage of the relevant domain. A narrow collection of tasks may fail to distinguish students with different conceptual or applied abilities. Fourth, independent observations can improve inference when they contribute complementary information rather than repeatedly measuring the same narrow attribute.

Fifth, comparisons should account for the populations and institutions from which the observations arise. A grade's relationship to knowledge may depend on the selection and grading mechanisms associated with a particular program. Finally, the uncertainty of the inference should be acknowledged explicitly. A high GPA may increase confidence in a student's competence, but the appropriate degree of confidence should depend on the evidence supporting the measurement relationship. This approach also suggests why richer academic records can be useful. A transcript containing course identities and individual grades provides more information than GPA alone. Technical projects, research, examinations, and demonstrations of applied competence can provide additional evidence about dimensions that may be poorly represented by the aggregate. The objective is not necessarily to construct a perfect measure of knowledge. It is to acquire enough relevant evidence to reduce uncertainty about the particular attributes needed for the intended inference or decision.

### Implications for Individual-Level Evaluation

The foregoing analysis challenges the assumption that GPA rankings should be treated as definitive rankings of knowledge. A student with a higher GPA may be more likely to possess stronger academic preparation, subject-matter knowledge, or characteristics associated with educational success. That is a defensible probabilistic interpretation when supported by appropriate empirical evidence. But a higher GPA does not logically establish greater competence in every relevant domain. Nor does a small numerical difference between GPAs necessarily identify a meaningful difference between individuals.

The strength of the conclusion depends on the relationship between the observations and the underlying attribute, the uncertainty surrounding that relationship, and the comparability of the measurement processes. This distinction becomes especially important when GPA is used in consequential individual decisions. A decision-maker may be justified in considering GPA as evidence. However, the weight assigned to that evidence should reflect its actual discriminatory and predictive value for the specific objective. If the available evidence suggests substantial uncertainty about individual competence, a rigid GPA ranking may project more precision than the measurement process supports. The appropriate response is not necessarily to abandon GPA, but to supplement it with evidence that more directly addresses the competencies relevant to the decision.

### Conclusion: Evidence Is Not Identification

The inferential problem is that an observed grade does not uniquely determine the knowledge state that generated it. GPA can be positively associated with competence and useful for predicting later academic outcomes, but neither relationship identifies every individual's understanding or guarantees that higher-GPA students are more knowledgeable in all relevant respects. Bayesian interpretation makes the dependence on assessment likelihoods, contextual information, and reference populations explicit; when those are uncertain, the posterior inference may remain broad or difficult to quantify. Extreme grades can be informative too, but strict bounds require further assumptions about task coverage and scoring. This is why **evidence of a relationship is not the same as identification of an individual state**. The practical strength of GPA-based comparisons depends on the measurement process, overlap in the distributions of competence associated with different grades, and the uncertainty tolerated by the decision. A useful predictor can still have substantial individual-level error, as research on subsequent academic performance illustrates. The remaining question is how to incorporate that uncertain evidence into decisions alongside other measures, rather than treating the administrative ranking as an automatically decisive one.

## VIII. The Decision Problem: How Much Weight Should GPA Receive?

Showing that GPA is an incomplete or context-dependent measure does not establish that it should be discarded. Decisions are routinely made with imperfect, costly, and heterogeneous evidence. The practical question is whether GPA improves a particular decision, given what is already known and what mistakes would cost. A record that helps predict success in subsequent coursework may be much less informative about a narrowly specified technical skill; conversely, it may be valuable when more direct assessments are unavailable. This section distinguishes a metric's statistical association with an outcome from its *incremental decision value*. GPA is one observation among several, and its appropriate weight depends on the attributes relevant to the task, the quality of competing evidence, and the consequences of false inclusion or exclusion. **Neither the familiarity of the GPA scale nor the fact that GPA predicts some outcomes supplies a universal weight for multicriteria evaluation.**

### Defining the Decision Before Evaluating the Indicator

A basic principle of decision analysis is that the value of information depends on the decision it is intended to inform. Consider several situations in which an individual's GPA might be relevant. A graduate admissions committee may wish to identify applicants likely to succeed in advanced coursework and complete a degree. A research organization may wish to identify individuals capable of constructing quantitative models. An employer may seek candidates who can solve unfamiliar problems, communicate effectively, and perform reliably under professional conditions. An academic evaluator may wish to determine whether a student has mastered a particular body of theoretical knowledge. These are different objectives. They require different competencies, and the relevance of GPA may differ substantially among them.

For predicting subsequent academic performance, prior grades may be informative because they summarize how successfully an individual has performed in similar educational environments. They may capture a combination of academic preparation, persistence, organizational ability, and familiarity with academic expectations. For evaluating competence in financial engineering, however, a general GPA may be considerably less informative than direct evidence of the individual's understanding of hedging, derivatives pricing, stochastic processes, and the assumptions underlying financial models. The difference is not necessarily that the latter decision is more important. It is that the relevant attributes and available sources of evidence differ. We can formalize the problem by defining the underlying attributes relevant to a decision as a vector:

$$
\boldsymbol{\theta}_i=
\begin{bmatrix}
\theta_{i1}\\
\theta_{i2}\\
\vdots\\
\theta_{id}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

where each component describes a competence or characteristic relevant to the decision. The evaluator observes some information set:

$$
\mathcal I_i=
\left\{
G_i,X_{i1},X_{i2},\ldots,X_{ip}
\right\}
$$

where $G_i$ represents GPA and the remaining observations represent other potentially relevant evidence. These observations might include individual course grades, completed coursework, technical examinations, research experience, independent projects, work samples, or other demonstrations of competence. The objective is to use the available information to select an action that produces a desirable outcome. This immediately reveals why GPA cannot be assigned a universally appropriate decision weight. The same measure may contribute substantial information toward one objective and relatively little toward another. Before deciding how important GPA should be, we must establish what the decision is intended to accomplish.

### A Formal Decision-Theoretic Framework

Suppose a decision-maker must select an action $a$ from a set of possible actions $\mathcal A$. The desirability of an action depends on the individual's underlying attributes $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, which are not fully observed. Let:

$$
U(a,\boldsymbol{\theta})
$$

represent the utility associated with selecting action $a$ when the true underlying state is $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. Because the evaluator does not know the true state, the decision must be based on the available evidence. A Bayesian decision rule selects the action that maximizes expected utility conditional on the information available:

$$
a^*(\mathcal I)
=
\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal A}
E\left[
U(a,\boldsymbol{\theta})
\mid\mathcal I
\right]
$$

This formulation makes the role of GPA explicit. GPA is not itself the objective. It is one possible observation within the information set used to estimate the consequences of alternative actions. The relevant question is whether adding GPA changes the expected utility of the decision. If GPA provides information about characteristics that affect the outcome, it may improve the decision. If it contributes little information beyond what is already known, its marginal usefulness may be small. The distinction can also be expressed through a loss function:

$$
L(a,\boldsymbol{\theta})
$$

where $L$ represents the cost associated with selecting action $a$ when the underlying state is $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. Under this formulation, the decision-maker seeks to minimize expected loss:

$$
a^*(\mathcal I)
=
\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal A}
E\left[
L(a,\boldsymbol{\theta})
\mid\mathcal I
\right]
$$

The two formulations are equivalent when utility and loss are appropriately related. Their significance is that **the usefulness of a measurement is determined by how it changes the quality of the decision, not merely by how strongly it correlates with an underlying attribute**. A measurement can be strongly correlated with a characteristic that is relatively unimportant for the task. Conversely, a measurement that provides modest but highly specific information about an essential competency may have substantial decision value. This is why neither the numerical precision of GPA nor its historical association with academic achievement is sufficient to determine how much weight it should receive.

### GPA as One Criterion in Multicriteria Decision Analysis

Many evaluations of individuals involve multiple criteria. A graduate admissions committee may consider academic preparation, research experience, technical skills, intellectual interests, and evidence of independent work. An employer may consider subject knowledge, professional judgment, communication, problem-solving ability, and the requirements of the position. These criteria are multidimensional, and their relative importance depends on the decision. A common approach is to construct an aggregate evaluation score:

$$
V_i=
\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_jX_{ij}
$$

where:

- $V_i$ represents the overall evaluation score for individual $i$.
- $X_{ij}$ represents the individual's assessed performance on criterion $j$.
- $w_j$ represents the importance assigned to that criterion.

If GPA is one of the criteria, its contribution can be represented by a term such as:

$$
w_GG_i
$$

The central question is how $w_G$ should be determined. There is no general reason to assign GPA a large weight simply because it is numerical, familiar, or easily compared across applicants. A high weight requires justification based on the relevance of GPA to the objective, the quality of its measurement, and its informational contribution relative to other evidence. The linear formulation also introduces assumptions that should not be overlooked. The criteria must be placed on scales compatible with the chosen aggregation procedure, and the weights must have a meaningful interpretation. In particular, the numerical value of a weight cannot be interpreted independently of the scale of the corresponding criterion.

A criterion measured from zero to one and another measured from zero to one hundred cannot be meaningfully compared through their coefficients without considering how those scales affect the result. Furthermore, an additive model assumes that performance in one dimension can compensate for performance in another according to the specified weights. That may be appropriate in some decisions, but not in others. The problem resembles the scalar compression issues developed in Section III. An overall score simplifies a multidimensional competence profile and imposes a particular relationship among its components. The fact that a decision model produces a single numerical ranking does not establish that its weighting assumptions are appropriate.

### Compensatory and Noncompensatory Decision Criteria

Consider two applicants for a position involving quantitative economic research. Suppose their competence profiles are represented by the following hypothetical standardized assessments.

| Attribute | Candidate A | Candidate B |
|---|---:|---:|
| GPA | 3.95 | 3.55 |
| Mathematical competence | 0.55 | 0.90 |
| Economic intuition | 0.85 | 0.80 |
| Empirical research competence | 0.70 | 0.85 |

Candidate A possesses the higher GPA and slightly stronger measured economic intuition. Candidate B performs considerably better on assessments of mathematical and empirical competence. Which candidate is preferable? The answer depends on the requirements of the position. If the role primarily involves quantitative modeling and empirical analysis, Candidate B's strengths may be more relevant. If the role requires a different set of capabilities, the evaluation could change. An additive scoring model may rank the candidates according to a weighted combination of their attributes. However, suppose the position requires a minimum level of mathematical competence because the individual must independently develop and evaluate quantitative models. The decision-maker might establish a threshold:

$$
K_{i,\text{mathematical}}\geq0.80
$$

Under the hypothetical competence scores, Candidate B satisfies the threshold while Candidate A does not. A sufficiently high GPA should not necessarily compensate for failing an essential requirement. This distinction can be represented by a constrained decision problem:

$$
\max_i V_i
$$

subject to:

$$
K_{im}\geq\tau_m
\qquad
\forall m\in\mathcal E
$$

where $\mathcal E$ denotes the set of essential competencies and $\tau_m$ represents the minimum acceptable level of each. The relevant competence scores and thresholds would need to be validated in any actual application. The example is intended to illustrate the logical distinction between compensatory rankings and minimum proficiency requirements. The broader point is that **a high aggregate score does not necessarily establish that an individual possesses every essential competency required for a particular task**. This applies to GPA and to any other composite index. An applicant may perform exceptionally well across many dimensions while possessing a deficiency that is particularly consequential for the intended role. Treating the aggregate as sufficient can conceal that deficiency.

### The Incremental Informational Value of GPA

One of the most important decision-theoretic questions concerns the information GPA adds after accounting for evidence already available. Suppose we are attempting to predict a relevant outcome $Y$, such as success in advanced coursework or performance on a technical task. Let $\mathbf X$ represent other observed characteristics and assessment results. We can compare two predictive relationships:

$$
E[Y\mid\mathbf X]
$$

and:

$$
E[Y\mid G,\mathbf X]
$$

The first uses the available evidence without GPA. The second incorporates GPA as an additional predictor. The relevant question is whether the second provides meaningfully better predictions. In a linear regression framework, one measure of incremental predictive value is:

$$
\Delta R^2
=
R^2(Y\sim G,\mathbf X)
-
R^2(Y\sim\mathbf X)
$$

This quantity describes the additional variation explained by introducing GPA into the specified model. For ordinary least squares regression evaluated on the same training sample with nested models, adding a predictor cannot reduce the unadjusted $R^2$. This mathematical property does not establish that the additional predictor improves generalization to new observations. The relevant predictive improvement should therefore be evaluated using appropriate out-of-sample procedures, such as cross-validation or a separate validation sample. A predictor that improves the fit of historical data may provide little or no benefit when applied to new individuals. The issue is especially important when educational environments change, since the relationships among GPA, other characteristics, and future outcomes may not remain stable across institutions or cohorts.

#### Incremental information versus redundant information

A GPA may correlate strongly with a relevant outcome while contributing little additional information once more direct measures of competence are available. For example, suppose a candidate's GPA is positively associated with mathematical ability. If the evaluator has already administered a well-designed assessment that directly measures the mathematical competencies required for the position, GPA may provide comparatively little additional evidence about those particular competencies. This does not imply that GPA is uninformative in general. It means that the relevant information may already be captured by another observation. We can formalize this distinction using conditional mutual information. Let $K$ represent the target competence, $G$ represent GPA, and $\mathbf X$ represent other available evidence. Then:

$$
I(K;G\mid\mathbf X)
$$

measures the additional statistical information GPA provides about $K$ conditional on $\mathbf X$. If:

$$
I(K;G\mid\mathbf X)=0
$$

then, under the relevant joint distribution, GPA provides no additional information about the target competence once the other evidence is known. If the conditional mutual information is positive, GPA contributes information not entirely contained in the other observations. However, additional statistical information does not automatically imply a substantial improvement in decision quality. The information may concern distinctions that do not affect the available actions, or it may be too weak to change the preferred decision. The distinction between statistical information and decision value is therefore important. A metric may improve prediction without materially changing a decision, and a relatively small improvement in prediction may be extremely valuable when it concerns a high-consequence outcome.

### Empirical Evidence for Context-Dependent Predictive Value

The educational measurement literature provides direct support for evaluating predictors in relation to specific institutional objectives. Sawyer (2013), in *Beyond Correlations: Usefulness of High School GPA and Test Scores in Making College Admissions Decisions*, examined data from 192 institutions. The study investigated the usefulness of high-school GPA and admissions test scores under different levels of institutional selectivity and different definitions of academic success. The results indicated that high-school GPA was more useful than admissions test scores in some settings, particularly those involving lower selectivity and lower academic performance thresholds. Conversely, admissions test scores were more useful in certain highly selective settings where the objective was to identify students likely to achieve high levels of academic performance.

The study also found that test scores offered incremental predictive usefulness beyond high-school GPA in nearly all of the contexts examined. These findings are important because they demonstrate that the relative usefulness of an indicator depends partly on the characteristics of the population and the definition of a successful outcome. There is no universal weighting scheme that is necessarily optimal across all institutions and decisions. A related finding comes from Galla et al. (2019), in *Why High School Grades Are Better Predictors of On-Time College Graduation Than Are Admissions Test Scores: The Roles of Self-Regulation and Cognitive Ability*.

The researchers found that high-school GPA was a stronger predictor of on-time college graduation than standardized admissions test scores in their analyses. Their work also examined the roles of self-regulation and cognitive ability, providing evidence that grades reflect characteristics beyond performance on standardized cognitive assessments. This is a useful qualification to the criticism of GPA. If the objective is predicting educational persistence or graduation, the multidimensional nature of GPA may be an advantage rather than a defect. Persistence, self-regulation, and the ability to satisfy academic requirements may be directly relevant to completing a degree. But the usefulness of those characteristics for predicting graduation does not establish that GPA is a precise measure of conceptual understanding in a particular discipline. The same variable can be useful for one objective and inadequate for another. This is the central lesson of criterion-specific validation: **the evidential weight assigned to GPA must depend on what the evaluator is attempting to predict or assess.**

### GPA's Value Depends on the Other Evidence Available

Consider two hypothetical evaluation environments. In the first, an admissions committee possesses little information beyond an applicant's transcript and GPA. Under these circumstances, GPA may be among the most informative available indicators of previous academic achievement and preparation. In the second, the committee has access to detailed course records, research projects, independently assessed technical work, and demonstrated proficiency in the subjects relevant to the program. The second environment offers a richer information set. GPA may still contribute useful evidence, particularly about consistency of academic performance or characteristics not fully captured by the other assessments. However, its incremental value must be evaluated rather than presumed. The distinction can be expressed through the information sets:

$$
\mathcal I_1=\{G\}
$$

and:

$$
\mathcal I_2=\{G,\mathbf X\}
$$

where $\mathbf X$ represents the additional evidence. Under a correctly specified probabilistic model, possessing the larger information set cannot make the optimal expected decision worse if the decision-maker is free to ignore irrelevant information and acquiring or processing it is costless. However, this theoretical property does not imply that every additional measurement is worth acquiring in practice. Evidence can be expensive, time-consuming, and imperfect. It may require administering examinations, reviewing projects, conducting interviews, or evaluating prior work. Consequently, the decision-maker must consider both the expected improvement in the decision and the cost of obtaining the additional information. This leads naturally to the concept of the value of information.

### The Expected Value of Additional Information

Suppose the decision-maker currently possesses information $\mathcal I$ and is considering acquiring an additional measurement $Z$. The current optimal expected utility is:

$$
V(\mathcal I)
=
\max_{a\in\mathcal A}
E[U(a,\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid\mathcal I]
$$

If the additional measurement becomes available, the preferred action may change. Before observing $Z$, the expected value of having that additional information is:

$$
EVSI(Z\mid\mathcal I)
=
E_Z\left[
\max_{a\in\mathcal A}
E[U(a,\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid\mathcal I,Z]
\;\middle|\;\mathcal I
\right]
-
\max_{a\in\mathcal A}
E[U(a,\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid\mathcal I]
$$

This is the expected value of sample information. Under the assumptions of a correctly specified model, optimal decision-making, and costless use of the information, the expected value is nonnegative. The decision-maker can always ignore the additional observation if it does not improve the available choice. However, acquiring information ordinarily involves costs. The net value can therefore be represented as:

$$
NEVSI
=
EVSI-C_Z
$$

where $C_Z$ represents the relevant costs of collecting and using the information. The decision-maker should acquire the additional information when its expected net value is positive, subject to relevant practical and ethical constraints.

#### Application to assessing subject knowledge

Suppose an employer is selecting between two candidates for a technical research position. Candidate A has a higher GPA, but Candidate B possesses a more demanding quantitative academic background. The employer could administer an assessment designed to evaluate the specific mathematical and analytical skills required for the position. Whether that assessment is worth administering depends on how much it is expected to reduce uncertainty about the candidates' relevant abilities, whether it is likely to change the hiring decision, and the costs associated with administering it. If the existing evidence already identifies one candidate as clearly preferable across all relevant criteria, the additional examination may have little decision value. But if the candidates possess different apparent strengths and the relevant competence is highly consequential, the additional information may be valuable. This example illustrates why a decision-oriented approach is more informative than assigning GPA a fixed weight independent of the context. The weight attached to GPA should reflect not only its general relationship to academic outcomes but also the value of the additional information it contributes to the particular decision.

### Errors, Thresholds, and the Consequences of Misclassification

Decision quality depends not only on the accuracy of predictions but also on the consequences of incorrect decisions. Suppose a program uses a minimum GPA threshold to identify candidates with adequate prerequisite knowledge. A student whose GPA exceeds the threshold is classified as eligible, while a student below it is classified as ineligible. This creates the possibility of two kinds of error. A false positive occurs when the criterion identifies someone as adequately prepared even though they lack the relevant competence. A false negative occurs when it excludes someone who possesses the competence required for success. The consequences of these errors may differ. Admitting an inadequately prepared student could impose costs on the student and institution. Excluding a capable applicant could deny an educational or professional opportunity without sufficient justification. The decision threshold should therefore depend on both the statistical relationship between GPA and competence and the relative consequences of classification errors. Let $H=1$ denote possession of the required competence and $H=0$ its absence. Let:

$$
p=P(H=1\mid\mathcal I)
$$

represent the probability that the individual possesses the required competence conditional on the available evidence. Suppose accepting an unqualified applicant produces loss $C_{FP}$ and rejecting a qualified applicant produces loss $C_{FN}$. For simplicity, suppose correct decisions have zero loss. The expected loss of accepting the applicant is:

$$
L_{\text{accept}}=C_{FP}(1-p)
$$

The expected loss of rejecting the applicant is:

$$
L_{\text{reject}}=C_{FN}p
$$

Accepting is preferable when:

$$
C_{FP}(1-p)<C_{FN}p
$$

Rearranging:

$$
p>
\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}}
$$

This simple result demonstrates that the appropriate decision threshold depends on the relative consequences of different errors. The model is intentionally simplified and should not be interpreted as a complete admissions or employment policy. Real decisions also involve limited capacity, competing applicants, legal requirements, fairness considerations, and consequences extending beyond a binary classification. Nevertheless, it demonstrates an important point: **even if GPA accurately estimates the probability of possessing a competence, that probability alone does not determine the appropriate decision.** The decision also depends on the consequences of acting incorrectly. A fixed GPA cutoff does not automatically capture those considerations. Moreover, if GPA is not calibrated to the relevant competence, the cutoff may compound the original measurement problem by imposing a rigid decision rule on an uncertain and context-dependent indicator.

### The Problem of Arbitrary Weighting

An evaluator who assigns GPA a dominant role in a multicriteria decision is implicitly making several assumptions. First, the evaluator assumes that GPA measures attributes relevant to the objective. Second, the evaluator assumes that its relationship to those attributes is sufficiently reliable and comparable across the individuals being evaluated. Third, the evaluator assumes that GPA contributes enough information beyond the available alternatives to justify its importance. Fourth, the evaluator assumes that the decision rule appropriately balances GPA against other characteristics. These assumptions are rarely made explicit in informal comparisons of academic records.

Consider an evaluator who assigns 70% of a candidate's overall assessment to GPA and 30% to all other evidence. What justifies that weighting? The fact that GPA predicts some academic outcomes does not establish that 70% is the appropriate weight for evaluating technical competence. Nor does the fact that GPA is reported on a numerical scale establish its relative importance. If the decision involves predicting a continuous outcome, statistical models can help estimate how different predictors relate to that outcome. But a regression coefficient should not automatically be interpreted as a universal normative decision weight. Regression coefficients depend on the variables included, their units, the underlying population, and the statistical model.

More importantly, predicting an outcome and deciding how much to value different outcomes are distinct problems. A variable may improve predictive accuracy without being normatively appropriate to reward, and a decision-maker may assign importance to considerations that are not reducible to predictive performance alone. The distinction is particularly relevant when evaluating individuals because different competencies may be valued for reasons that extend beyond their contribution to a single observed outcome. Thus, a multicriteria framework requires both empirical evidence and explicit judgment about the objectives of the decision. The role of GPA should emerge from that framework rather than being imposed in advance.

### Predictive Accuracy, Calibration, and Decision Utility

The usefulness of GPA-based predictions depends on several statistical properties that should be distinguished. **Predictive discrimination** concerns whether a measure can distinguish individuals likely to experience different outcomes. If students with higher GPAs are more likely to succeed in subsequent coursework, GPA possesses some discriminatory value for that outcome. **Calibration** concerns whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed frequencies. For example, among students assigned a 70% probability of successfully completing a program, approximately 70% should succeed if the model is well calibrated for that population. **Decision utility** concerns whether using those predictions improves decisions relative to the available alternatives. These properties are related but not equivalent.

A model may distinguish relatively stronger and weaker candidates without producing accurately calibrated probabilities. It may be well calibrated while offering little ability to discriminate among individuals. And even a statistically accurate model may provide limited decision value if the available actions or consequences make its predictions irrelevant. This is especially important when considering GPA as a screening measure. A positive relationship between GPA and an outcome does not establish that a particular cutoff is optimal. Nor does it establish that the relationship is stable across different institutions or student populations. The appropriate validation strategy must therefore be tied to the actual decision.

For example, if GPA is used to predict success in advanced economics coursework, the model should ideally be evaluated against outcomes reflecting success in that coursework. If GPA is used to assess research competence, the relevant validation evidence should concern research performance or appropriate demonstrations of that competence. A model validated for predicting college grades cannot automatically be treated as validated for predicting professional judgment, creativity, or the understanding of financial engineering. The outcome used to validate the measure is part of the interpretation.

### Fairness and the Distribution of Decision Errors

A further issue concerns the distribution of errors across individuals and groups. The preceding sections established that socioeconomic conditions can affect academic preparation, opportunities to demonstrate knowledge, and, in some settings, evaluator judgments. These factors may influence the relationship between observed GPA and the attributes relevant to a decision. A decision rule that relies heavily on GPA may therefore produce different error rates across populations, particularly when the grading systems or educational opportunities differ. However, the existence of group differences in GPA does not independently establish that a decision rule is biased or invalid. Differences may arise through multiple mechanisms, including genuine differences in acquired knowledge, unequal educational opportunities, or differential evaluation of comparable work. These mechanisms require separate investigation. The decision-theoretic problem is to determine whether the measure and decision rule are appropriate for their intended use and whether the resulting outcomes are defensible. For example, suppose the relationship between GPA and a defined competence differs across two educational contexts. Then:

$$
P(H=1\mid G=g,J=A)
\neq
P(H=1\mid G=g,J=B)
$$

where $J$ identifies the relevant context. Under those conditions, applying the same GPA threshold to both populations may result in different classification performance. A common numerical cutoff does not guarantee common sensitivity, specificity, or predictive interpretation. The appropriate response may involve improving the measurement instruments, examining contextual information, validating predictions across relevant populations, or incorporating other evidence. Such adjustments must themselves be evaluated for validity, fairness, and consistency with applicable institutional and legal requirements. The broader principle is that fairness cannot be established merely by applying the same numerical rule to observations generated through different measurement processes. Nor can fairness be established by assuming that differences in measured outcomes are necessarily artifacts of the measurement system. A defensible evaluation requires understanding the relevant mechanisms and the consequences of the decision.

### Why a Portfolio of Evidence Is Often Preferable

The limitations of GPA suggest that consequential evaluations of individual competence may benefit from multiple sources of evidence. A portfolio of assessments can distinguish between different dimensions of knowledge and reduce reliance on any single instrument. For example, an assessment of economics competence might include evidence from:

- Relevant coursework and GPA.
- Conceptual explanations of economic mechanisms.
- Mathematical and econometric problem-solving.
- Interpretation of empirical evidence.
- Independent research or applied projects.
- Performance on unfamiliar problems.
- Demonstrations of the ability to connect theory with economic applications.

The purpose of combining these observations is not simply to produce a larger collection of scores. It is to acquire information about dimensions that would otherwise remain uncertain. Ideally, the selected instruments should provide complementary evidence about the competencies relevant to the decision. This distinction matters because multiple indicators may share systematic biases or assess overlapping attributes. A collection of five examinations emphasizing essentially identical mathematical procedures may offer more reliable evidence about computational proficiency without contributing much information about conceptual understanding. Likewise, multiple subjective evaluations may provide limited additional information if they rely on the same underlying assumptions or institutional expectations. A useful portfolio should therefore be designed around the dimensions of the construct and the objectives of the evaluation, rather than assembled merely for the appearance of comprehensiveness.

#### The importance of direct performance evidence

One advantage of task-specific assessments is that they can target competencies that GPA may only indirectly represent. If the objective is to evaluate whether someone understands hedging, an assessment requiring the individual to explain the construction and economic rationale of a hedging strategy is more directly aligned with the target than a general academic average. If the objective is to evaluate mathematical modeling, the individual can be asked to formulate, analyze, and interpret a relevant model. If the objective is empirical research, a project requiring appropriate data analysis, causal reasoning, and interpretation may provide more specific evidence than the average grade across unrelated courses. These assessments are not automatically perfect or free from measurement error. Their reliability, validity, and comparability still require attention. But their relevance to the target construct can often be specified more clearly. The appropriate comparison is therefore not between a supposedly objective GPA and inherently subjective alternatives. It is between different sources of evidence, each possessing its own measurement properties, costs, and limitations.

### The Danger of Replacing GPA With Another Unjustified Scalar

An important qualification concerns the temptation to replace GPA with a more elaborate composite score. Suppose an institution combines GPA, test scores, interviews, projects, and research experience into a single numerical ranking. This may improve decision quality if the measures are appropriately selected, calibrated, weighted, and validated. But it can also reproduce many of the same problems associated with GPA. Different dimensions are again compressed into one number. The weights may be arbitrary. Some attributes may compensate for deficiencies in essential competencies. The resulting index may be treated as though its numerical precision established the quality of the underlying evaluation.

And if the new score becomes consequential, individuals may begin optimizing their behavior around its components. Thus, the broader lesson is not simply that GPA should be replaced by a different aggregate. It is that **the structure of the decision should determine which measurements are relevant and whether aggregation is appropriate**. In some cases, a scalar score may be justified. In others, a multidimensional profile combined with explicit minimum requirements may be more informative. The correct approach depends on the purpose of the evaluation and the properties of the available evidence.

### Robustness and Sensitivity to Decision Weights

A useful extension of multicriteria decision analysis is to examine how sensitive an evaluation is to the chosen weights. Suppose two candidates have assessment profiles $\mathbf X_A$ and $\mathbf X_B$, and an additive scoring rule produces:

$$
V_A=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf X_A
$$

$$
V_B=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf X_B
$$

Candidate A is preferred when:

$$
\mathbf w^\top
(\mathbf X_A-\mathbf X_B)>0
$$

But the ordering may change when the weights change. If GPA receives substantial weight, the candidate with the higher GPA may rank first. If task-specific competence receives greater weight, the ordering may reverse. This raises a practical question: is the decision robust to reasonable alternative weighting schemes? Let $\mathcal W$ represent a set of plausible weights consistent with the decision's objectives. A particularly strong preference for Candidate A would require:

$$
\mathbf w^\top
(\mathbf X_A-\mathbf X_B)>0
\qquad
\forall\mathbf w\in\mathcal W
$$

If the result changes across plausible weights, then the ranking is sensitive to assumptions about the relative importance of different criteria. This does not necessarily make the decision indefensible. It means the choice depends on substantive judgments that should be acknowledged. In the context of GPA, this is especially relevant when an evaluator assigns a large weight to academic performance without demonstrating that the weight is appropriate. A ranking that reverses when GPA's weight is reduced may be driven primarily by the chosen weighting convention rather than by a clear overall superiority in the relevant competence profile. Sensitivity analysis makes this dependence visible. Rather than presenting a ranking as a direct consequence of objective measurements, it reveals which assumptions are responsible for the ordering.

### The Role of GPA Under Limited Information

A fair assessment should also recognize the practical advantages of GPA. It is widely available, relatively inexpensive to obtain, and summarizes performance across multiple academic experiences. In some circumstances, this makes it an efficient screening variable. An institution evaluating thousands of applicants may lack the resources to conduct detailed technical assessments of every candidate. GPA can provide useful preliminary information and help allocate limited evaluation resources. The relevant question is whether its use improves decisions compared with feasible alternatives, not whether an idealized assessment system would contain more information. However, the convenience of GPA does not justify treating it as a universally sufficient criterion. A preliminary screening measure and a definitive assessment of competence serve different purposes.

The use of GPA may be more defensible when it is employed to identify candidates for further evaluation than when it is treated as conclusive evidence of knowledge. Even in preliminary screening, however, the consequences of excluding applicants deserve attention. A rigid cutoff may eliminate individuals whose relevant competencies are poorly represented by their academic averages. The tradeoff between efficiency and the risk of erroneous exclusion should therefore be evaluated explicitly. The decision-maker must consider the cost of acquiring better information, the consequences of classification errors, and whether the measure performs adequately across the populations to which it is applied. This is a more defensible basis for using GPA than assigning it authority simply because it is familiar and quantitative.

### Integrating the Measurement and Decision Problems

The central arguments developed throughout this critique can now be understood as a sequence. First, the evaluator must define the underlying attribute of interest. If the objective is to assess knowledge, competence, or intellectual understanding, those constructs must be specified sufficiently to determine what would constitute relevant evidence. Second, the evaluator must examine the measurement process. Grades are produced through assessments that differ in content, difficulty, scoring procedures, and conditions of administration. Third, the evaluator must consider representation and aggregation. Course grades compress multidimensional performance, while GPA combines heterogeneous grades into a scalar that may conceal important differences between competence profiles.

Fourth, the evaluator must consider comparability. A common numerical scale does not establish that grades possess equivalent meanings across instructors, institutions, and populations. Fifth, the evaluator must consider incentives. Students and institutions respond to grading systems, and those responses can affect the relationship between GPA and the underlying educational objectives. Sixth, the evaluator must assess the inference. The observed grade provides probabilistic evidence about knowledge, but its strength depends on the measurement process and the reference population. Finally, the evaluator must determine how the evidence should influence a decision. Each stage introduces assumptions that can affect the interpretation of the resulting metric. The complete sequence can be represented conceptually as:

$$
\text{Construct}
\longrightarrow
\text{Measurement}
\longrightarrow
\text{Representation}
\longrightarrow
\text{Inference}
\longrightarrow
\text{Decision}
$$

The incentives and institutional context influence several stages simultaneously. A failure to establish the validity of one transition can undermine the conclusions drawn at later stages. For example, if the measurement instruments do not adequately represent conceptual competence, no amount of numerical precision in the final GPA can resolve that deficiency. If grades are not comparable across institutions, a decision rule assigning equal meaning to identical GPAs may rest on unsupported assumptions. And even if GPA possesses predictive validity, assigning it dominant weight may remain inappropriate if other evidence is more directly relevant to the decision. This is why the critique cannot be reduced to the statement that GPA is noisy. The concern is broader: **the informational significance of GPA is conditional on the entire process connecting underlying knowledge to observed grades and, ultimately, to the decisions made from those observations.**

### Conclusion: The Proper Role of GPA in Individual Evaluation

GPA is a legitimate and potentially useful indicator of cumulative academic performance, preparation, persistence, and other characteristics associated with educational success. Its limitations do not imply that it should be ignored. They imply that **its role must be justified by the decision being made**: which attributes matter, what information GPA contributes beyond other evidence, how comparable the observations are, and what different classification errors would cost. Multicriteria analysis can make these choices explicit. A task may call for direct demonstrations of essential skills, a portfolio of complementary assessments, or minimum requirements that cannot be offset by strengths elsewhere. Where information is scarce, GPA may be an efficient initial signal; where more direct evidence is available, its incremental value may be smaller. A single overall index can aid evaluation, but its weights and compensatory assumptions should be tested rather than hidden behind a numerical ranking. The central error is not believing that GPA contains information; it is giving the metric more authority than its demonstrated relevance and incremental value warrant.

## IX. Conclusion: The Limits of GPA as a Measure of Knowledge

This critique has not claimed that grades lack information, that academic performance is unrelated to knowledge, or that quantitative evaluation is illegitimate. GPA can summarize genuine achievement and help predict some educational outcomes. The mistake is to treat that usefulness as evidence that GPA is a universally calibrated, comprehensive measure of what an individual knows. **GPA is an observed result of a complex assessment process; knowledge is an underlying attribute inferred from that result.** The connection depends on instruments, scoring rules, institutional conditions, student characteristics, and incentives. A common numerical reporting scale does not ensure a common measurement procedure, and the precision of the reported number does not determine the precision of the inference. What follows draws together the consequences of those distinctions for interpreting GPA and deciding how much authority to grant it, without rejecting its legitimate uses.

### The Relationship Between Knowledge and Observed Grades

The conceptual foundation of the argument begins with the distinction between knowledge, competence, and observed academic performance. Knowledge is not directly observable in its entirety. Instead, educational institutions administer assessments intended to elicit performances that reveal something about the student's underlying abilities. These performances are evaluated according to particular grading rules, converted into numerical or categorical outcomes, and eventually aggregated into GPA. Each stage introduces assumptions about the relationship between the observation and the underlying attribute. The selection of assessment tasks determines which dimensions of knowledge become observable. The conditions of administration affect how students demonstrate their abilities. Grading rules determine how different aspects of performance contribute to the recorded outcome. Institutional practices determine how the resulting evaluations are classified and aggregated. A general representation of the process is:

$$
G_{ic}
=
Q_c\left[
f_c(\mathbf K_{ic},T_c,C_{ic},E_{ic})
+\varepsilon_{ic}
\right]
$$

where $\mathbf K_{ic}$ represents the student's multidimensional knowledge state relevant to course $c$, $T_c$ represents the assessment instrument, $C_{ic}$ represents the conditions under which performance is elicited, $E_{ic}$ represents effort and other performance-related behavior, and $Q_c$ represents the relevant scoring and grading transformation. The student's GPA is then constructed through an additional aggregation:

$$
\operatorname{GPA}_i
=
\frac{\sum_{c=1}^{C_i}w_cg_{ic}}
{\sum_{c=1}^{C_i}w_c}
$$

where $g_{ic}$ denotes the numerical grade-point value associated with the course outcome and $w_c$ represents the course weight. These expressions reveal that GPA is not ordinarily a direct measurement of one homogeneous quantity. It is an aggregate of evaluations produced by potentially different functions of different dimensions of knowledge, under different assessment conditions and institutional rules. The resulting statistic may summarize accumulated academic performance coherently within the institution's administrative framework. However, its relationship to a broader construct such as disciplinary understanding requires separate justification. The fact that GPA is mathematically well-defined does not establish that the underlying competence it is presumed to measure is equally well-defined, nor that the aggregation preserves the information required to assess it.

### Measurement Quality, Representation, and Comparability

The argument also demonstrates why the limitations of GPA cannot be reduced to ordinary measurement error. Some of the difficulties associated with grades concern the reliability of the assessment process. Instructors may evaluate equivalent work differently, examination results may depend on administration conditions, and repeated assessments may produce variation unrelated to changes in the knowledge of interest. These are familiar problems of instrumentation and measurement reliability. Other difficulties concern representation. Subject-matter competence is often multidimensional, while grades reduce different aspects of that competence to a scalar outcome. Distinct knowledge profiles can therefore generate identical grades, and high performance in one dimension may compensate arithmetically for deficiencies in another. The resulting number can accurately describe performance under the chosen weighting scheme while failing to characterize the broader distribution of the individual's abilities.

Still other difficulties concern comparability. Instructors, departments, and institutions differ in their curricula, assessment methods, grading criteria, and standards. A common letter-grade convention does not establish that the observations were produced by equivalent instruments or that their numerical differences represent comparable differences in knowledge. These problems are related, but they are not interchangeable. Improving inter-rater reliability does not necessarily correct construct underrepresentation. Standardizing grade-point conversions does not establish equivalence between curricula. Aggregating additional examinations may reduce random error without recovering dimensions of knowledge that were never assessed.

The comparison with physical measurement illustrates the importance of these distinctions. The measurement of height benefits from a relatively well-defined physical attribute, standardized units, calibrated instruments, and established procedures for verifying observations. Educational assessment can also be designed systematically and rigorously, but common grade categories do not automatically supply equivalent calibration or portability. Consequently, the principal obstacle is not simply that grades contain noise. It is that **the relationship between a grade and the underlying competence may differ across measurement regimes in ways that are not captured by the reported number**. This creates uncertainty about the interpretation of both differences and similarities between observed GPAs. Two students with identical GPAs may possess substantially different competence profiles, while two students with different GPAs may have been evaluated under sufficiently different conditions that their numerical ordering does not establish a corresponding ordering of subject-specific knowledge.

### The Endogeneity of Academic Performance Indicators

The measurement process is further complicated by the incentives surrounding grades. Students, instructors, and institutions are not passive participants in the generation of academic outcomes. They respond to the rewards, penalties, and opportunities associated with the reported indicators. The importance of GPA in admissions, scholarships, employment, and academic advancement can encourage greater effort and genuine learning. However, it can also encourage strategies that improve recorded grades without producing equivalent improvements in the underlying educational objectives. Course selection, narrow examination preparation, strategic allocation of effort, changes in grading thresholds, and institutional responses to performance incentives can all affect the distribution of observed grades. Goodhart's law and Campbell's law identify the general vulnerability: when a performance indicator becomes consequential, the behavior of the system may adapt to the indicator, potentially changing the relationship that originally made it informative. This creates a dynamic problem that extends beyond the static limitations of measurement. Let $\pi$ represent the incentive regime governing academic evaluation. The relationship between grades and knowledge may then depend on that regime:

$$
P(G\mid\mathbf K,\pi)
$$

There is no general reason to assume that this conditional relationship remains invariant when the incentives facing students and institutions change. Thus, even a measurement relationship that was empirically useful under one set of circumstances may not retain the same interpretation under another. Importantly, this does not imply that high grades necessarily result from gaming, or that students who optimize their academic performance are somehow less knowledgeable. Improving grades through genuine learning is entirely consistent with the intended purpose of assessment. The concern is that grade improvements can arise through multiple mechanisms, some of which produce substantially greater improvements in knowledge than others. The observed GPA does not independently distinguish among these mechanisms. The measurement system is therefore partly endogenous to the incentives created by its use. As grades become more consequential, the process producing them may change, and the informational relationship between the statistic and the underlying objective may change with it.

### The Distinction Between an Indicator and the System It Represents

The broader significance of these problems becomes evident when GPA is compared with quantitative indicators in other domains. Gross domestic product measures economic production, but it does not provide a comprehensive representation of economic welfare. Clinical measurements describe particular physiological characteristics, but no single observation fully characterizes an individual's health. Mean time to failure summarizes expected component lifetime, but does not determine the probability that a component survives a particular operating interval. These are not necessarily defective measurements. They are measurements whose legitimate informational scope is narrower than the broader systems people sometimes attempt to evaluate through them. The same distinction applies to GPA. A statistic can be accurately calculated, reproducible, and predictive of certain outcomes while remaining insufficient for other inferences. Its usefulness must be evaluated in relation to the substantive question being asked. Represent the broader system state as $\mathbf X$ and a summary indicator as:

$$
M=f(\mathbf X)
$$

Different underlying states may generate the same observed value. Consequently, the statistic may fail to preserve distinctions that are important for a particular evaluation. The issue is not whether a summary statistic is useful in some general sense, but whether it preserves the information necessary for the intended inference. A strong condition for the sufficiency of an indicator relative to some target attribute $Y$ is:

$$
P(Y\mid\mathbf X)=P(Y\mid M)
$$

Under this condition, observing the complete information $\mathbf X$ would provide no additional information about $Y$ once the summary $M$ is known. There is no general basis for assuming that GPA satisfies such a condition when the target is an individual's multidimensional knowledge or competence. The courses completed, the assessments administered, the distribution of grades across subjects, and the student's demonstrated abilities may all provide information that the aggregate discards. This is why the limitations of GPA are not resolved merely by calculating the statistic more accurately. A perfectly measured summary can remain insufficient for the broader question. **Measurement accuracy and inferential sufficiency are distinct properties.**

### What GPA Can Legitimately Tell Us

Recognizing these limitations does not require dismissing the empirical value of GPA. Grades can reflect genuine academic achievement, sustained effort, preparation, persistence, and the ability to satisfy educational requirements. Because these characteristics are relevant to subsequent academic performance, GPA may possess considerable predictive value in some settings. Indeed, the multidimensional nature of grades can be an advantage when the objective is to predict outcomes that depend on several academic and behavioral characteristics. A measure need not isolate pure subject knowledge to be useful for predicting degree completion or future academic performance. Persistence, self-regulation, and the ability to fulfill academic requirements may be directly relevant to those outcomes.

The critical distinction is between predicting an outcome and diagnosing a particular underlying attribute. Evidence that GPA predicts future academic success does not establish that it measures conceptual understanding with comparable accuracy. Nor does a positive correlation between grades and knowledge imply that every pairwise GPA comparison correctly orders individuals according to their competence. The informational significance of GPA is probabilistic and context-dependent. Given an observed grade $G$ and contextual information $C$, the relevant inference concerns:

$$
P(\mathbf K\mid G,C)
$$

rather than an assumed deterministic mapping from the numerical observation to a unique knowledge state. A high GPA may increase our confidence that an individual possesses strong academic preparation or subject competence. The extent of that increase depends on the likelihood of observing the grade under different competence states, the relevant reference population, and the assessment conditions. When these elements are known and the measurement process is well validated, the inference may be relatively strong. When they are unknown or heterogeneous, the range of plausible underlying competence states may remain broad. The appropriate conclusion is therefore neither that GPA proves knowledge nor that GPA tells us nothing. It is that the strength of the inference must be proportional to the evidence supporting the relationship between the grade and the underlying attribute.

### The Limits of Individual Rankings

These considerations are especially important when GPA is used to compare individuals. A higher GPA may be associated with greater expected knowledge in a particular population, but this does not establish that the higher-GPA individual necessarily possesses greater competence. The distinction can be expressed as:

$$
E[K\mid G=g_2]>E[K\mid G=g_1]
$$

for $g_2>g_1$, without implying:

$$
K_A>K_B
$$

for every pair of individuals satisfying $G_A>G_B$. A positive population-level relationship can coexist with considerable uncertainty about particular individuals. That uncertainty may become larger when the students were evaluated through different instruments, completed different curricula, or came from different institutional environments. Furthermore, when knowledge is multidimensional, an overall ordering may be underspecified even if the underlying competence profiles were observed without error. One individual may possess stronger mathematical proficiency while another demonstrates greater conceptual understanding. Ranking the individuals according to general subject knowledge then requires a weighting scheme or decision criterion specifying how those dimensions should be valued.

GPA supplies an ordering because the institution has already imposed a numerical aggregation rule. But the existence of that ordering does not establish that it is the appropriate ordering for every substantive question. The numerical comparison may be exact within the administrative scale while its interpretation as a comparison of underlying knowledge remains uncertain. This is why GPA differences, particularly small differences, should not automatically be treated as meaningful distinctions in intellectual competence. The uncertainty associated with the underlying inference may be substantial relative to the observed numerical difference.

### The Appropriate Role of GPA in Decision-Making

The practical implications follow from the distinction between an indicator's informational content and its decision value. A decision-maker should begin by identifying the attributes relevant to the objective. An evaluation of advanced mathematical economics requires different evidence from an evaluation of conceptual policy reasoning or the prediction of academic persistence. GPA may contribute useful information to each of these decisions, but the nature and magnitude of its contribution may differ. In a general decision-theoretic framework, the appropriate action depends on the available evidence and the consequences of alternative choices:

$$
a^*(\mathcal I)
=
\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal A}
E[U(a,\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid\mathcal I]
$$

Here, GPA is one possible component of the information set $\mathcal I$, not the objective being maximized. Its importance should depend on whether it improves the decision relative to the other evidence available. Where GPA provides unique and relevant information, assigning it meaningful weight may be justified. Where other assessments provide more direct, reliable, or decision-specific evidence, GPA may contribute comparatively little additional information. Similarly, if an essential competence cannot be compensated for by strengths in unrelated areas, an aggregate GPA may be an inappropriate substitute for demonstrating that competence directly.

The appropriate evaluation may therefore require a portfolio of measurements, including subject-specific examinations, conceptual questioning, technical demonstrations, research, and independent work. Such a portfolio is not necessarily free from measurement error or bias. Its advantage depends on whether the instruments capture relevant and complementary aspects of the target construct. Nor should multiple indicators automatically be collapsed into another universal scalar. Aggregation requires its own justification, particularly when the competencies being evaluated are multidimensional and not fully compensatory. The defensible approach is to select and weight evidence according to the decision's objectives, rather than treating one familiar numerical summary as an authoritative measure of individual merit or understanding.

### Final Synthesis

The critique of GPA ultimately concerns the relationship between measurement, representation, inference, and decision-making. Grades are generated by assessment instruments that sample particular dimensions of knowledge under particular conditions. Their outputs depend on scoring rules, institutional standards, and characteristics of the students being evaluated. GPA aggregates these heterogeneous outcomes into a scalar whose numerical precision does not guarantee an equivalent precision in representing the underlying competence. These limitations are compounded by variation across institutions and populations, as well as by incentives that can change the behavior of students and the measurement practices of instructors and institutions. As a consequence, GPA cannot generally be interpreted as a universally calibrated measure of subject-matter knowledge. Its relationship to competence is conditional on the process that generated it, and the uncertainty surrounding that relationship must be considered when drawing conclusions about individuals.

The existence of population-level associations between GPA and subsequent outcomes does not eliminate this uncertainty. Predictive validity for academic success is not identical to diagnostic validity for conceptual mastery, and the informational value of a measure depends on the specific outcome or attribute being evaluated. The broader argument is therefore one of proportionality between evidence and inference. A numerical observation justifies conclusions only to the extent that the relationship between the observation and the underlying attribute has been adequately established. When the measurement process is heterogeneous, the construct multidimensional, and the relationship between the metric and the objective influenced by incentives, strong individual-level conclusions require correspondingly stronger evidence. This is not an objection to quantification. It is an objection to treating the products of quantification as though they automatically possess the properties necessary for the conclusions drawn from them.

GPA remains a useful administrative statistic and, in many circumstances, a meaningful predictor of educational outcomes. But it should not be mistaken for a complete representation of intellectual competence, nor should its familiarity and numerical form confer an authority that its measurement properties do not independently justify. **The fundamental mistake is not using GPA as evidence of knowledge. It is treating the observed grade as though it were the knowledge itself, or as though the relationship between the two were sufficiently stable, comprehensive, and precise to support definitive judgments without additional contextual information.**

A defensible evaluation must instead ask what the measure represents, how the observation was generated, what uncertainty remains about the underlying attribute, and how much information the statistic contributes to the decision at hand. The validity of the conclusion depends on the answers to those questions, not merely on the numerical value of the grade. Ultimately, the appropriate standard is neither uncritical reliance on GPA nor categorical rejection of it. It is an interpretation disciplined by measurement theory, statistical inference, and the requirements of the decision being made. **GPA should be treated as one imperfect, context-dependent source of evidence whose importance is proportional to its demonstrated ability to inform the specific question under consideration.**
