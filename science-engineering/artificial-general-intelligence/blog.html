Skepticism About Artificial General Intelligence

This essay asks a narrower question than whether advanced AI can be dangerous: what would justify classifying AGI as an existential risk rather than as a powerful source of systemic disruption? I argue that the distinction matters because deep uncertainty changes both what can responsibly be claimed and what kinds of risk-management strategies are most defensible.

Core claim. The evidence may justify treating AGI as a transformative risk amplifier, but it does not presently justify confident placement in an existential-risk quadrant. Under deep uncertainty, policy should emphasize robustness, reversibility, monitoring, redundancy, and institutional adaptability rather than false precision about unknowable long-run probabilities.

Introduction — The Claim Under Examination

I want to begin with a disclosure: I am generally skeptical that “AGI,” however loosely we define the concept, is possible. I discussed some of the resources behind that skepticism in my other post, “Why Large Language Models are Not and Will Not Become Artificial General Intelligence.” For the purposes of this essay, however, I will grant the premise that AGI is possible and likely to emerge in the near future. The question I want to examine is narrower: how much confidence should we place in claims that such a system would constitute an AI existential risk? My tentative conclusion is that we do not know whether a near-term AGI would be comparable to risks such as nuclear proliferation or global pandemics without assuming very specific trajectories, capabilities, and interactions within a complex socioeconomic system.

The claim under examination is this: if Strong AI (AGI) were to emerge, human society would move toward the upper-right region of the scope–severity grid introduced by Nick Bostrom in “Existential Risk Prevention as Global Priority” (see also the linked collection of existential-risk papers). That is the proposition I want to evaluate, not a conclusion I intend to assume.

The strongest version of the claim is that unleashing unconstrained AGI would push society toward the “Cosmic–Hellish” region of the chart. At face value, I find that conclusion difficult to justify. This is not to say that we should be unconcerned about unforeseen risks from releasing a powerful new technology. Rather, many of those risks may be unknowable in advance, while some of the alarm surrounding AGI depends on strong assumptions about what an AGI could do. I expect meaningful risks; I am simply not persuaded that AGI, by itself, establishes that we are moving into the existential-risk region of the chart.

What this essay is—and is not—claiming

The argument is not that AGI would be safe, or that existential risks do not exist. It is a methodological claim about classification: a technology can be dangerous, destabilizing, and worthy of serious precaution without our having sufficient grounds to assign it a meaningful probability of causing human extinction or irreversible civilizational collapse.

Part I The Epistemic Problem Before asking how dangerous AGI might be, we need to ask whether the relevant possibility space, variables, consequences, and probabilities can be specified with enough stability for conventional risk analysis.

1. The Problem of Predicting Transformative Technology

The first step is to distinguish ordinary uncertainty inside a known model from uncertainty about the model, state space, and future functions themselves.

In essence, the problem we are attempting to solve has eluded people for centuries: if a new technology is introduced into a complex system, what will happen? This is a question financial markets care about for an obvious reason: technological change creates opportunities for alpha. Yet predicting the consequences of industry-disrupting technologies has been extraordinarily difficult, and I take comprehensive prediction to be impossible in principle. It would require us to anticipate forms of human ingenuity and recombination that do not yet exist.

We do not have models that can fully account for that kind of open-ended complexity. This is why I find Stuart Kauffman’s paper, “No entailing laws, but enablement in the evolution of the biosphere,” relevant to the x-risk discussion. The abstract makes the underlying point especially clearly:

Biological evolution is a complex blend of ever changing structural stability, variability and emergence of new phenotypes, niches, ecosystems. We wish to argue that the evolution of life marks the end of a physics world view of law entailed dynamics. Our considerations depend upon discussing the variability of the very "contexts of life": the interactions between organisms, biological niches and ecosystems. These are ever changing, intrinsically indeterminate and even unprestatable: we do not know ahead of time the "niches" which constitute the boundary conditions on selection. More generally, by the mathematical unprestatability of the "phase space" (space of possibilities), no laws of motion can be formulated for evolution. We call this radical emergence, from life to life.

I should note that Kauffman is a visionary in the study of complexity. He is a theoretical biologist, among other things, associated with the Santa Fe Institute, and much of his work asks how far mathematical methods can take us when we study the evolution of the biosphere. In physics, a model generally begins with a prestated phase space: the relevant variables and possible states are defined by the model and its boundary conditions, allowing us to write equations that trace dynamics from specified initial conditions.

Kauffman argues that biological evolution is different because functions themselves become part of the evolving phase space. Evolution continually opens novel possibilities, and the set of possible functions is indefinite rather than simply a very large list that can be written down in advance. His “Screwdriver Argument” makes the intuition concrete: how many possible uses does a screwdriver have? The answer depends on the environment and on forms of ingenuity that may not yet have occurred. The number of uses need not be infinite for the list to be non-prestatable.

Biological exaptation and repurposing illustrate the point: structures can acquire functions that were not the reason they originally appeared. Kauffman frames the exhaustive enumeration of possible uses as an undecidable problem. On this view, new functionality is not entailed by a fixed list of law-like possibilities; there are pre-adaptations that cannot be known by the researcher ahead of time. His conclusion is that if we cannot fully state the biological phase space, then we cannot fully specify the relevant variables and write differential equations that entail the future motion of the evolving biosphere.

If the relevant possibility space is open-ended, the next question is what a formal risk framework actually requires us to know before it can produce meaningful probabilities or expected losses.

2. What Risk Analysis Actually Requires

Different risk traditions use different terminology, but they repeatedly depend on some combination of objectives, events, consequences, uncertainty, probabilities, controls, and background knowledge.

How is this relevant to x-risk? Well, I consider socio-economic systems to be just as creative as biological systems, perhaps even more. We literally cannot state the possible functions of an AGI, so we have little basis for asserting what outcomes are more likely than others. If we take "risk" to mean "the possibility of something bad happening", more specifically "the potential for negative downside measured by loss in some relevant variable", I don't see how we can construct a space of possibilities and assign probabilities to calculate expected loss in any meaningful way. Let's take the ISO definition of Risk to see if a bit of clarity can help us consider whether constructing a risk model is feasible in this situation. 

ISO Guide 73:2009 defines risk as, effect of uncertainty on objectives

Note 1: An effect is a deviation from the expected – positive or negative.

Note 2: Objectives can have different aspects (such as financial, health and safety, and environmental goals) and can apply at different levels (such as strategic, organization-wide, project, product and process).

Note 3: Risk is often characterized by reference to potential events and consequences or a combination of these.

Note 4: Risk is often expressed in terms of a combination of the consequences of an event (including changes in circumstances) and the associated likelihood of occurrence.

Note 5: Uncertainty is the state, even partial, of deficiency of information related to, understanding or knowledge of, an event, its consequence, or likelihood.

Note 2 seems relevant in this discussion because something can only be risky with respect to some pre-specified desired outcome. Note 5 is interesting as well because it describes risk as being related to our state of knowledge; something I alluded to above is that we tend to be in complete ignorance in situations like these. There are other proposed definitions listed on the wikipedia page, I'll list a few and comment on the ones I think are relevant. 

"Measurable uncertainty". This definition comes from Knight's "Risk, Uncertainty and Profit" (1921).[9] It allows "risk" to be used equally for positive and negative outcomes. In insurance, risk involves situations with unknown outcomes but known probability distributions.[10]

"Volatility of return". Equivalence between risk and variance of return was first identified in Markovitz's "Portfolio Selection" (1952).[11] In finance, volatility of return is often equated to risk.[12]

"Statistically expected loss". The expected value of loss was used to define risk by Wald (1939) in what is now known as decision theory.[13] The probability of an event multiplied by its magnitude was proposed as a definition of risk for the planning of the Delta Works in 1953, a flood protection program in the Netherlands.[14] It was adopted by the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (1975),[15] and remains widely used.[7]

"Consequences and associated uncertainty". This was proposed by Kaplan & Garrick (1981).[16] This definition is preferred in Bayesian analysis, which sees risk as the combination of events and uncertainties about them.[17]

"Uncertain events affecting objectives". This definition was adopted by the Association for Project Management (1997).[18][19] With slight rewording it became the definition in ISO Guide 73.[3]

"Asset, threat and vulnerability". This definition comes from the Threat Analysis Group (2010) in the context of computer security.[21]

Across these definitions, a few themes recur: risk is evaluated relative to objectives; it concerns undesirable or preferred outcomes; and it is usually characterized through events, consequences, uncertainty, and—where possible—probabilities. Risk management then becomes an effort to change the distribution of outcomes or to control the conditions that produce them. I have no objection to these definitions. My concern is whether they can be applied to AGI at the level required to support an existential-risk classification.

The connection to biological phase space is important. How feasible is it to map the relevant sources of AGI risk and assign useful probabilities to the resulting outcomes? I have little doubt that advanced AI could have transformative effects, but moving directly from “transformative” to the x-risk quadrant without well-defined outcomes or measurable mechanisms seems premature. Contrast this with a more concrete environmental hazard such as severe water pollution: we understand important causal mechanisms affecting human health, can model geographic exposure, and can estimate at least some downstream economic and social consequences. The analysis may still be uncertain, but the objects of analysis are comparatively well specified.

In the AGI x-risk discussion, I have encountered far more reliance on a priori scenarios—for example, an AGI recursively producing a more capable successor that ultimately destroys humanity—than on a comparable empirical risk model. The more concrete concerns, in my view, involve mechanisms such as cybersecurity, biological misuse, critical infrastructure, and other pathways that can be tied to identifiable vulnerabilities. At the broader existential level, however, I have not yet encountered a comprehensive analysis that identifies the relevant sources of risk, events, causal pathways, and consequences with enough completeness to justify stable probabilities. By the standards of risk analysis being used here, several basic tasks therefore remain unresolved:

  1. Determined the sources, causes and drivers of risk
  2. Investigated the effectiveness of existing or new controls
  3. Been able to analyze possible consequences and their likelihood
  4. Understood the interactions and dependencies between risks
  5. Determined any measures of risk with respect to AGI
  6. Verified or validated any results, since there are none
  7. Conducted uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

To fully appreciate the problem, let’s use the formal risk description introduced by Terje Aven in Quantitative Risk Assessment:

Risk is described as a tuple

where:

A is an event that might occur
C is the consequences of the event
U is an assessment of uncertainties
P is a knowledge-based probability of the event
K is the background knowledge that U and P are based on

Aven’s tuple Events (A), consequences (C), uncertainty (U), probabilities (P), and background knowledge (K). Weak K and incomplete A make P unstable and C difficult to characterize comprehensively.

The difficulty is therefore not merely that our numerical estimates are noisy. The deeper possibility is that some of the objects we would need to estimate are not stably defined before the technology interacts with society.

3. From Risk to Deep Uncertainty

This is where the distinction between measurable risk and Knightian uncertainty becomes central: the problem may concern ignorance about possible events and models, not simply uncertainty about parameters inside a known distribution.

Recall that x-risk, as I am using the term here, refers to outcomes approaching the “Cosmic–Hellish” region of the scope–severity grid. Risk management is concerned with avoiding unfavorable outcomes by understanding and changing the procedures, actions, decisions, exposures, and controls that lead to them. In Aven’s notation, that immediately makes the quality of the background knowledge, \(K\), important.

In the AGI case, I think \(K\) is limited: we have little historical background knowledge and no close analogue for the deployment of a system with the assumed capabilities. That weakens \(P\), the knowledge-based probability assigned to events. For any event in \(A\), the corresponding probability may therefore be unstable or highly assumption-dependent. The problem is not merely imprecise parameters; the event set \(A\) itself may be incomplete.

This is where Kauffman’s reasoning matters. If there is an indefinite number of future functions, scenarios, and interactions enabled by AGI, then we cannot assume that \(A\) exhausts the relevant possibilities. An incomplete event space also makes \(C\), the set of consequences, difficult to characterize comprehensively, while \(U\) cannot be reduced to uncertainty over a fixed list of outcomes. My main point so far is that we are operating in a regime characterized by Knightian Uncertainty, so it’s hard to say where AGI will place us on the Scope-Severity chart with any degree of confidence, let alone asserting confidently that AGI introduces us to x-risk. Frank Knight distinguished risk from uncertainty. On this view, we are lacking quantifiable knowledge about possible occurrences, accounting for essential unpredictability:

"Uncertainty must be taken in a sense radically distinct from the familiar notion of Risk, from which it has never been properly separated.... The essential fact is that 'risk' means in some cases a quantity susceptible of measurement, while at other times it is something distinctly not of this character; and there are far-reaching and crucial differences in the bearings of the phenomena depending on which of the two is really present and operating.... It will appear that a measurable uncertainty, or 'risk' proper, as we shall use the term, is so far different from an unmeasurable one that it is not in effect an uncertainty at all."

In the risk model above, it is useful to distinguish uncertainty caused by statistical noise from uncertainty caused by ignorance about the relevant model or possibility space. In sufficiently complex systems there may be a degree of unknowability that does not disappear simply by collecting more observations. In that sense, Knightian risk is amenable to calculation under specified probabilities, while Knightian uncertainty is not straightforwardly computable in the same way because the relevant distribution may not be known.

Nassim Taleb’s Ludic Fallacy is relevant here. In The Black Swan, he contrasts the “domesticated” uncertainty of games with uncertainty in the real world. In a casino game, the rules and relevant probability distributions are specified; the odds can be calculated because the game defines the sample space. Real social systems are different: the odds may have to be discovered, the sources of uncertainty may be incompletely defined, and the rules of the effective game can change as participants adapt.

That is closely related to model uncertainty, although the distinction needs care. Epistemic uncertainty often refers to uncertainty arising from limited knowledge of a model or its parameters and may be reduced by better information, measurement, or modeling. Knightian uncertainty, as I am using the term here, refers to the deeper problem that the relevant possibilities or probability structure may themselves be unknown. A technological innovation is a paradigmatic case because it can create behaviors, uses, institutions, and interactions that were not available for direct observation beforehand. The Ludic Fallacy arises when we assume that more data or a better-fitted model can always convert this form of uncertainty into a well-behaved probability distribution.

Bringing this back to x-risk, my concern is that a comprehensive AGI risk model would be highly assumption-sensitive because we are operating partly under Knightian uncertainty. We do not know what the full transition will look like, and trying to predict a Black Swan in advance is self-defeating. Even a reasonably good forecast can become unstable once it is embedded in a socioeconomic system populated by adaptive agents.

Those agents respond to interventions in ways that can undermine the relationships on which the forecast was based—the intuition behind the Lucas Critique. Feedback dynamics are notoriously difficult to represent in models of complex systems. If I grant the strong premise that AGI would possess superior intelligence and processing power relative to humans, that only makes the adaptive interaction between the system, its users, and attempts to control it more important.

This is why runaway AI is not the only—or, in my view, the most useful—risk framing. Socioeconomic systems are not simple control systems; they are complex adaptive systems that require different methods of analysis. Economists have often relied on state-estimation techniques such as the Kalman Filter in macroeconometrics, but concepts such as path dependence and niche construction are also crucial when the system itself changes as agents learn and adapt.

Checkpoint: what has been established so far?

The argument is not that every AGI consequence is unknowable. Some bounded mechanisms—cybersecurity failures, misuse pathways, infrastructure faults, or biological hazards—may be analyzable. The narrower point is that moving from those mechanisms to a comprehensive probability of existential catastrophe requires a much stronger model of the total state space and its interactions.

Part II From Uncertainty to Argument Deep uncertainty does not settle the policy debate by itself. It changes the burden of argument and forces a distinction between warranted precaution and unwarranted confidence in a specific catastrophic forecast.

4. What Follows—and What Does Not

The strongest version of the argument is methodological: lack of a stable probability model weakens confident x-risk classification, but it does not imply safety, complacency, or inaction.

The precautionary objection. One can accept the claim that AGI involves Knightian uncertainty and respond that this is a reason for more caution: if the downside could be enormous and the probabilities are poorly known, why not act conservatively?

I think that objection is important, but it changes the kind of policy that follows rather than restoring precise x-risk estimates. Under deep uncertainty, the most defensible strategy is not to optimize against a single speculative probability distribution. It is to favor robustness across many plausible futures: staged and reversible deployment, monitoring, redundancy, containment of cascading failures, institutional learning, and the preservation of options as information changes.

In other words, “we cannot confidently quantify existential risk” is not the same proposition as “there is nothing to manage.” The first is an epistemic constraint; the second would be a substantive safety claim. My argument depends on maintaining that distinction.

Once that distinction is clear, we can ask a separate question: how much of the x-risk debate is being driven by evidence, and how much by competing priors, intuitions, heuristics, and burdens of proof?

5. Psychology, Bias, and the Burden of Proof

Appeals to cognitive bias cut in both directions. If bias is invoked to explain why others underestimate x-risk, the same argumentative move can be mirrored to explain why others may overestimate it.

So far I have argued for a more limited conclusion: we do not yet have a strong basis for confidently placing AGI in the x-risk quadrant. First, the relevant risks have not been identified and characterized with the completeness that a conventional risk model would require. Second, even a sophisticated model may remain fragile if the state space is open-ended and the agents inside the system adapt to the intervention. That raises a separate question: why are existential-catastrophe scenarios nevertheless so psychologically compelling?

Existential dread and apocalypticism are recurring features of human psychology and history. At the same time, humans are also poor at recognizing slow, diffuse, and cumulative risks that do not present an immediate threat. These tendencies can coexist. In the AGI debate, one especially relevant tendency is to anthropomorphize: it is cognitively easy to imagine a single agent acquiring agency, becoming an overlord, and deciding to destroy us.

A systemic framing is less narratively simple. “Alignment” can also be understood as another instance of longstanding problems of collective decision-making, institutional incentives, coordination, and collective error. Systemic instability, structural uncertainty, and risk amplification are harder to dramatize than a single catastrophic agent, but they may be more useful objects of analysis. This does not prove that x-risk advocates are biased; it means that risk assessment itself takes place inside human psychology. “Cognitive bias” has also been used to justify the opposite position. Some advocates argue that existential risks are systematically underestimated because of biases such as scope neglect or the availability heuristic: people have difficulty representing enormous but unfamiliar harms, and we have no lived example of human extinction to make the possibility salient.

The difficulty is that this style of argument is symmetric. In the absence of a principled method for determining whether a novel technology is an existential risk, one can invoke one family of biases to explain skepticism and another family to explain alarmism. Selecting only the biases that undermine one’s opponents does not establish that one has reached the rational benchmark. A cognitive bias is, after all, a deviation from some rational ideal; invoking the label does not by itself demonstrate that the true probabilities have been estimated correctly. An advocate might structure the argument as follows:

  • P1. X Poses an Existential Threat
  • P2. People do not see what I see is an existential threat because of cognitive biases A,B,C
  • Assumption: Cognitive biases A,B,C are actively instantiated in the detractor
  • Warrant: prima facie, we should assume X is an Existential Threat, absent any defeaters
  • C. The assumption of Existential Threat holds

On its face we should be able to see that a symmetric argument can be constructed yielding the negation of this chain of reasoning. This is called a symmetry breaker in philosophical argumentation:

  • P1. Person Y claims X poses an Existential Threat
  • P2. Person Y is overhyping, amplifying, or being alarmist due to alternative cognitive biases D,E,F
  • Assumption: Cognitive biases D,E,F are actively instantiated in the detractor
  • Warrant: prima facie, we should not accept such a claim in the absence of a principled analysis, or if there are any overriding defeaters
  • C. There is no existential threat of X 

I give more weight to the second argument structure because the first leaves too much room for an unsupported existential-risk claim to become the default. Under the first structure, anyone can assert that \(X\) is an existential risk and then shift the burden of proof onto anyone who objects. The second structure imposes a burden of production: a claim this strong should be supported by a principled analysis rather than by a shared sense of dread.

That does not prove the negation of x-risk. It specifies the null position I think is methodologically preferable: do not treat the existential classification as established until sufficient evidence is produced. I also suspect that some AGI x-risk alarmism is influenced by a different set of cognitive biases and prior beliefs than the biases invoked to explain skepticism. A Bayesian vocabulary is useful for separating three things that are often run together: background knowledge, evidential support, and the interpretive effects of cognitive bias.

A formal Bayesian representation

Let \(X\) denote the proposition that AGI constitutes an existential risk. I will compare the hypothesis that this proposition is false with the hypothesis that it is true, while treating the two bias sets as alternative interpretive regimes rather than as different datasets.

SymbolDefinition
\(H_1\)\(\neg X\): AGI does not constitute an existential risk.
\(H_2\)\(X\): AGI does constitute an existential risk.
\(E\)The relevant observed evidence or data.
\(K\)The common stock of background knowledge held fixed across the comparison.
\(B_1\)A set of cognitive biases or interpretive commitments tending to favor \(H_1\), the negation of x-risk.
\(B_2\)A set of cognitive biases or interpretive commitments tending to favor \(H_2\), the affirmation of x-risk.

To evaluate the hypotheses formally, treat \(B_1\) and \(B_2\) as alternative cognitive or interpretive regimes applied to the same evidence \(E\) and background knowledge \(K\). For each regime \(B_j\), where \(j\in\{1,2\}\), define \(D_j=(E,K,B_j)\). The posterior odds can then be decomposed into background-conditioned prior odds and a Bayes factor comparing the likelihood of the same evidence under \(H_1\) and \(H_2\).

\[ PR_j = \frac{P(H_1\mid K,B_j)}{P(H_2\mid K,B_j)}, \qquad j\in\{1,2\}, \]
Background-conditioned prior odds
\[ BF_j = \frac{P(E\mid H_1,K,B_j)}{P(E\mid H_2,K,B_j)}, \]
Bayes factor: the likelihood ratio for the same evidence \(E\) under the two hypotheses.
\[ PO_j = \frac{P(H_1\mid E,K,B_j)}{P(H_2\mid E,K,B_j)} = PR_j\,BF_j. \]
Posterior odds

Thus, within either bias regime, \(PO_j>1\) means that \(H_1\) has greater posterior support than \(H_2\); \(PO_j<1\) means that \(H_2\) has greater posterior support. Likewise, \(BF_j>1\) means that the observed evidence is more probable under \(H_1\), while \(BF_j<1\) means that it is more probable under \(H_2\).

Bias as a sensitivity analysis

The two regimes can then be compared directly. Define the bias-sensitivity ratio as the ratio of the posterior odds produced under \(B_1\) and \(B_2\):

\[ R_B = \frac{PO_1}{PO_2} = \frac{PR_1}{PR_2} \times \frac{BF_1}{BF_2}. \]

Here, \(R_B>1\) means that the \(B_1\) regime shifts the total inference more strongly toward \(H_1\) than the \(B_2\) regime does; \(R_B<1\) means the reverse. In logarithmic form the same comparison becomes additive:

\[ \Delta_B = \log PO_1-\log PO_2 = \log\!\left(\frac{PR_1}{PR_2}\right) + \log\!\left(\frac{BF_1}{BF_2}\right). \]

This decomposition is useful because it distinguishes two possible sources of disagreement: bias can alter the prior plausibility assigned to the hypotheses, and it can alter the interpretation of how diagnostic the same evidence is. Neither effect should be confused with changing the underlying evidence itself.

A bias-neutral benchmark can also be written by omitting \(B_1\) and \(B_2\) altogether:

\[ \frac{P(H_1\mid E,K)}{P(H_2\mid E,K)} = \frac{P(H_1\mid K)}{P(H_2\mid K)} \times \frac{P(E\mid H_1,K)}{P(E\mid H_2,K)}. \]

On this formulation, \(B_1\) and \(B_2\) are best understood as sensitivity analyses around the benchmark rather than as evidence in their own right. The framework therefore preserves the substantive point I want to make: before attributing disagreement to “the evidence,” we should ask how much comes from prior commitments and how much comes from different interpretations of the same observations. According to Jeffreys, Bayes factors are often interpreted using the following evidential scale:

Bayes factor Evidence category
> 100 Extreme evidence for H1
30 - 100 Very strong evidence for H1
10 - 30 Strong evidence for H1
3 - 10 Moderate evidence for H1
1 - 3 Anecdotal evidence for H1

1/3 - 1 Anecdotal evidence for H0
1/10 - 1/3 Moderate evidence for H0

1/100 - 1/30 Very strong evidence for H0

In the bias-neutral benchmark, my contention is that the available information does not presently warrant posterior odds strongly favoring \(H_2\). An x-risk advocate who argues that \(H_2\) should dominate must therefore locate the disagreement in the evidential term, the prior term, or both. If the Bayes factor favors \(H_2\), the available evidence should be substantially more probable under the x-risk hypothesis than under its alternative. My question is what concrete evidence, as opposed to a hypothetical mechanism, supports consequences at the “Cosmic–Hellish” scale.

I readily grant evidence for important AI risks. But the hypothesis being tested here is not “AGI can cause harm”; it is the stronger existential-risk proposition. The evidence therefore has to discriminate between severe but bounded harms and outcomes that threaten humanity’s long-run survival or potential.

The other source of disagreement is the prior. If the posterior favors \(H_2\) despite weak discriminating evidence, then prior beliefs are doing substantial work. My suggestion is that cultural narratives, fear, and other background commitments may influence those priors. That is a hypothesis about the debate, not a demonstration that every x-risk advocate has irrational priors, and it is precisely why the evidential and prior components should be kept conceptually separate.

Part III From Prediction to Resilience If long-run outcomes cannot be exhaustively predicted, risk management should shift toward concrete vulnerabilities, cascading mechanisms, governance, and the capacity to adapt under surprise.

6. AGI as a Risk Amplifier

The constructive alternative is to move away from an exclusive focus on superintelligence and toward the ways advanced AI may amplify existing institutional, infrastructural, and collective-action failures.

I want to clarify the scope of the claim before turning to an alternative risk framework. I obviously accept that humanity faces existential risks. My contention is narrower: the present basis for classifying AGI itself as an existential risk is weak, and some of the confidence placed in that classification may reflect priors shaped by media, cinema, and familiar catastrophe narratives. I offer those as possible explanatory factors, not as a comprehensive empirical account of what determines anyone’s beliefs.

More generally, “futures studies” faces a difficult methodological problem: distinguishing a risk with very large consequences from a genuinely existential risk before the relevant future system exists. My reading of the literature is that many arguments rely heavily on thought experiments and devote less attention to the side effects of intervention in a complex adaptive system. The public discussion of existential risk from artificial general intelligence also often moves quickly from AGI to the stronger idea of superintelligence, associated with arguments by Nick Bostrom and David Chalmers.

I think that transition needs to be defended rather than assumed. AGI and superintelligence are not synonyms, and intelligence itself is neither perfectly defined nor fully understood. Strong superintelligence arguments can also depend on contestable theories of mind and agency. A concept this underspecified should not do more policy work than the evidence allows. Otherwise, the debate can be pulled away from concrete mechanisms—misuse, institutional overreliance, infrastructure vulnerabilities, cascading failures, governance, and adaptation—that can be investigated more directly.

So where should the discussion be directed? I have a few thoughts, influenced by Carla Cremer (linked below) and Igor Krawczuk. The risks posed by AGI are best understood, in my view, as human and institutional problems rather than as problems that require a mythical superintelligence. AGI can serve as a risk amplifier: it may introduce new instabilities into systems that are already unstable and interact with those systems in ways that cannot be fully known a priori.

That framing changes the policy question. Our task should not be to discover and program a single “best ethical system” into an AGI. We often do not know what the good decision is ahead of time and can rationalize outcomes after the fact through hindsight bias. Some moral problems resist clean algorithmic resolution; the trolley problem is a familiar example. If AGI contributes to a regime shift, the transition is likely to have an uncertain duration and uncertain consequences. The strategy should therefore be adaptive: build enough institutional and technical agility to absorb unforeseen shocks while learning from the transition as it unfolds.

Here are several practical considerations:

  1. Premature deployment and overconfidence. AGI risk is primarily a consequence of deploying tools that are not quite ready to be applied because their environment or context is too complicated or complex. If we prematurely deploy AGI as a subsystem within a larger system, introducing uncertainty, the tool cannot capture all of the complexity. This leads to poorer performance. In other words, our problem will be that we have too much confidence in these systems.
  2. Replace the overlord model with systemic interaction. Instead of conceiving of a runaway-intelligence overlord who takes control over everyone (a very abstract and implausible story) where we all die due to one single event, think of the problem in terms of entangled systemic interacting problems that are hard to disentangle, creating particular scenarios (that we don’t know ahead of time) where something blows up (this depends on my deflationary view of AGI as being merely a collection of statistics and algorithms). Shift the conversation away from superintelligence as much as possible. 
  3. Interrogate assumptions. Interrogate your assumptions. Many strong AI-doom arguments base their claims on a set of assumptions that rest on additional assumptions, that ultimately seem like spooky science fiction. Focus your attention on democratic, participative solutions. 
  4. Treat governance as political and organizational. The risks come from system design and collective social choice problems from economics and political science. How to aggregate preferences democratically. It’s not about writing an algorithm for ethical behavior or figuring out the one master algorithm. It is very much a political problem, organizational in nature. Questions of risk imply a power dynamic that should involve all stakeholders in a society, not just a few people in Silicon Valley. 
  5. Map cascading vulnerabilities. A corollary to number two, we ought to identify points of vulnerability that lead to cascading failures. If we begin replacing well-tested control systems with probabilistic systems like an AGI, there might be faults that could propagate through the system, leading to a crash. This will be very crucial for vital infrastructure. 
  6. Invest in network and complexity science. A corollary to number 5, if we are to roll out systems with inherent uncertainty, we should invest more in our understanding of concepts like Percolation Theory, Interdependent networks, diffusion, and many other concepts from network theory and complexity science so we can better anticipate domino effects and vicious cycles that are difficult to escape. We need to understand how uncertain events can lead to second and third order events in a principled and reliable way. We need better sources of information to help us identify structural instabilities that lead to progressive collapse. 

Transformative innovations absolutely require adaptive mechanisms to withstand such shocks. This means creating massive research programs to identify solutions in this new environment. Brittle institutions will not be able to withstand shocks. We cannot know all of the shocks ahead of time. We need to adopt a strategy that generates the tools necessary to remain adaptive as possible in this new regime.

7. Historical Illustrations of Disruptive Regime Change

Historical analogies cannot tell us what AGI will do, but they can illustrate a narrower institutional point: technologies can reorder social systems dramatically without fitting the category of existential risk.

Fragile systems will almost certainly fail under sufficiently large shocks; that is close to a truism. Before concluding, however, it is useful to consider historical cases in which highly disruptive innovations produced social and institutional chaos without producing an existential catastrophe.

Consider the printing press, arguably one of the most transformative technologies ever created. It generated enormous gains, including the expansion of literacy, but existing institutions were not prepared for the information environment it helped create. Revolts and wars followed; the Protestant Reformation began; civilization changed. Questions about authority, knowledge, and even the nature of truth were reopened. Yet, for all of that destabilization, the printing press was not an existential risk. Political and religious institutions had to adapt to an educated population, the recirculation of ancient texts, and a level of pluralism Europe had not experienced for centuries. The broader lesson is not that every information technology repeats this history, but that rigid institutions can fail when the informational environment changes faster than their capacity to adapt.

The fall of Tsar Nicholas II provides a second illustration. Obviously, the overthrow of the Tsar cannot be reduced to a single technological innovation. Nevertheless, the case illustrates how rigid institutions can collapse amid rapid technological and social change. Citizens increasingly demanded representation; contact with Western liberal democracies encouraged people to reconsider their political arrangements; industrialization altered standards of living, expectations, and social organization. The shocks facing Russian society in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries contributed to systemic collapse. The monarchy appeared increasingly unable to understand or adapt to the scale of disruption and resentment around it. Again, the outcome was catastrophic for an existing regime without constituting an existential threat to humanity.

8. Conclusion: Uncertainty Is Not Safety

The practical conclusion is not that the future is benign. It is that deep uncertainty calls for a different kind of seriousness—one centered on resilience, concrete mechanisms, and the capacity to adapt.

Technological disruptions arrive with consequences that vary enormously in severity. We cannot reliably predict every effect in advance, but we can adopt strategies that reduce the damage from exogenous shocks and help a system move into a new regime without cascading into collapse. In that sense, an exclusive focus on existential risk and superintelligence can obscure a more actionable discussion: how to build institutions, infrastructures, and decision processes that remain robust when the nature of the shock is itself uncertain.

Bottom line. AGI may produce severe and nonlinear disruptions. The case for treating those disruptions as a confidently characterizable existential risk is much weaker than the case for treating AGI as a transformative source of systemic uncertainty. The appropriate response to that uncertainty is neither complacency nor speculative precision, but robust and adaptive risk management.
