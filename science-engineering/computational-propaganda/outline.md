# Computational Propaganda in Action: From a YouTube Honey Cure Ad to an Opaque Influence Funnel

## Working Thesis

This essay examines a deceptive YouTube advertisement claiming that a honey-based drink can reverse Alzheimer’s or dementia. What initially appeared to be a bizarre miracle-cure ad revealed a much larger system: religious targeting, celebrity deepfakes, fake journalism, medical misinformation, affiliate-style funneling, opaque sponsorship, and industrial-scale ad variation. This case study shows how computational propaganda operates not only through politics, but through health scams, influencer dynamics, identity targeting, and the broader attention economy.

---

# III. Rhetorical Strategy: How the Ad Manufactures Belief

## 5. Religious in-group targeting

Analyze “Christians, drink this.”

Sub-points:

* The viewer is addressed as part of a moral community.
* The product is framed as spiritually appropriate.
* Honey is transformed from food into sacred medicine.
* Biblical references create “sacred-text laundering.”

Possible phrase:

> The ad does not simply sell honey. It baptizes honey.

## 6. Pseudo-specificity

Claims like:

* “Mentioned 27 times in the Bible”
* “Reverses Alzheimer’s in 30 days”
* “99 percent of drugs do not work”
* “No dementia in Israel”

Explain:

* Numbers create the feeling of precision.
* The claims sound researched even when unsupported.
* Specificity substitutes for evidence.

## 7. The vague enemy: “They”

Analyze the recurring “they.”

“They” may mean:

* Big Pharma
* Doctors
* The FDA
* Google/YouTube
* Mainstream media
* Secular elites
* Globalists
* Medical researchers
* Anyone the viewer already distrusts

Key point:
The vagueness is strategic. The viewer supplies their own villain.

## 8. Persecution marketing

Claims like:

* “They revoked this ad three times.”
* “They do not want you to see this.”
* “Watch before it is removed.”

Explain:

* Platform moderation becomes proof of truth.
* Missing evidence becomes evidence of suppression.
* The ad becomes self-sealing: if it stays up, the truth got through; if it disappears, the conspiracy is confirmed.

## 9. Authority flooding

The ad and landing page use many authorities:

* Bill Gates
* Denzel Washington
* Samuel L. Jackson
* Sanjay Gupta-style medical authority
* Harvard doctors
* Today Show-style presentation
* CNN-style branding
* Brain scans and medical visuals
* Biblical references

Explain:
This is not logical coherence. It is credibility accumulation. The viewer is surrounded by authority cues.

Term to introduce:
**Authority flooding** — overwhelming the viewer with multiple borrowed credibility sources so that skepticism has to fight too many battles at once.

# V. The Video Script: A Step-by-Step Conspiracy Funnel

## 13. The fake celebrity testimony layer

Discuss:

* Samuel L. Jackson deepfake.
* Bill Gates deepfake.
* Fake Today Show-style segment.

Explain:

* Their voices and faces create emotional familiarity.
* The viewer receives the claim through trusted cultural figures.

Caution:
Avoid claiming the real people participated. State clearly that these appear to be unauthorized synthetic or manipulated endorsements.

## 14. The medical redefinition

The script reframes Alzheimer’s.

Claims:

* Alzheimer’s is not caused by old age.
* Plaque theory is wrong or a cover-up.
* The real cause is toxins, pesticides, contaminated water, glucose resistance, or “type 3 brain diabetes.”
* Existing medications keep people dependent.

Explain:
This is how the funnel replaces medical complexity with a simple hidden cause.

## 15. Exploiting partial truths

Discuss “type 3 diabetes” and metabolic research.

Key point:
The most effective misinformation often begins near real scientific ambiguity. It takes a legitimate area of research and turns it into an absolute, conspiratorial claim.

Structure:

* Real science: metabolism and insulin resistance may be relevant to Alzheimer’s research.
* Funnel distortion: Alzheimer’s is secretly type 3 diabetes.
* Scam leap: honey fixes the real cause.
* Conspiracy seal: doctors know but hide it.

## 16. Leaked documents and buried cases

Analyze:

* “Leaked case”
* “Buried by the media”
* “Data team”
* “Harvard doctor”
* “They stopped the research”

Explain:
These phrases create investigative theater. They imitate the form of whistleblowing without providing verifiable evidence.

## 17. Visual pseudo-evidence

Discuss:

* Brain scans with circles.
* Charts or medical graphics.
* Before-and-after claims.
* Scientific-sounding percentages.

Term:
**Visual scientism** — the use of scientific-looking imagery without scientific standards.

The conversion may be:

* Buying a product.
* Joining a list.
* Distrusting doctors.
* Believing a conspiracy.
* Sharing the content.
* Entering a community.
* Becoming more susceptible to future funnels.

# XII. Participatory Disinformation: When the Network Becomes the Propagandist

## 48. Why add a second case study

After the essay analyzes the honey-cure ad as a paid, platform-delivered propaganda funnel, introduce a second model: propaganda that emerges through distributed participation rather than a single visible ad buy.

Purpose of this section:

* Show that computational propaganda does not always look like an advertisement.
* Contrast centralized funneling with decentralized network amplification.
* Explain how ordinary users, influencers, politicians, journalists, and debunkers can all become part of the same information cascade.
* Connect the honey ad to a broader ecosystem where the network itself helps manufacture salience, credibility, and belief.

Core framing:

> The honey ad shows computational propaganda as a funnel. The Springfield rumor shows computational propaganda as a cascade.

## 49. Use the Springfield “they’re eating the dogs” episode as the example

Introduce the Springfield, Ohio pet-eating rumor as a contrasting case.

Key timeline:

* Springfield was already under national attention because of debate over Haitian immigration, housing pressure, public services, and rapid demographic change.
* A specific pet-eating claim appears to have originated in a Facebook post based on hearsay, not firsthand observation.
* Screenshots of the post circulated across social media.
* Unrelated images, videos, and incidents were attached to the rumor as supposed corroboration.
* Influential online accounts amplified it.
* Politicians repeated the claim.
* Local officials repeatedly said they had no credible reports supporting the allegation.
* Even after debunking, the phrase became culturally and politically salient.

Important distinction:

The Springfield case is not identical to the honey ad. The honey ad was a paid, affiliate-tracked persuasion funnel. The Springfield rumor was a decentralized information cascade. But both show how weak claims can become powerful when they are routed through identity, emotion, platform amplification, and high-centrality messengers.

## 50. From weak signal to emotional selection

Explain the first transformation: a low-credibility rumor becomes transmissible because it is emotionally optimized.

The original signal had:

* Few sources.
* No direct verification.
* Low credibility.
* Limited reach.

Most such rumors die. This one had features that increased its “fitness” in the attention ecosystem:

* Disgust.
* Fear.
* Moral outrage.
* Threats to pets.
* Threats to community safety.
* Shocking alleged out-group behavior.
* A connection to an already politicized immigration narrative.

Core point:

Truth is not the same as transmissibility. The rumor spread because it was emotionally reproductive.

Possible phrase:

> Emotion acts like an amplification coefficient. It increases the probability that a claim will be reshared before it is verified.

## 51. Information laundering from network periphery to political center

Describe how the rumor moved through successive credibility layers.

Chain:

```text
private or local Facebook rumor
→ screenshots and reposts
→ small political accounts
→ medium influencers
→ large influencers
→ politicians
→ national media coverage
→ mass public awareness
```

Explain why this matters:

At each step, the original source becomes harder to see. Viewers stop asking whether the first post was verified and start asking why influential figures are talking about it.

Core concept:

**Information laundering** — a weak or unverified claim gains apparent credibility as it passes through higher-status messengers, even when no new evidence has been added.

Connect back to the honey ad:

The honey ad launders credibility through fake institutions and celebrity deepfakes. The Springfield rumor launders credibility through social repetition, influencers, politicians, and media coverage.

## 52. Authority feedback and the “now it is news” effect

Once a politician or major influencer repeats a rumor, the object of discussion changes.

Before:

```text
Is the rumor true?
```

After:

```text
A major political figure said the rumor.
```

This creates an attention trap. Journalists, critics, researchers, and local officials may have to repeat the claim in order to debunk it. Even debunking increases exposure.

Key point:

Attention is not the same as endorsement, but attention still changes the information environment.

Possible phrase:

> The rumor becomes news because powerful people repeated it, and powerful people can then cite the fact that everyone is talking about it.

## 53. Network cascades and high-centrality nodes

Explain the network dynamics.

A rumor does not need millions of independent believers to become nationally salient. It needs enough high-centrality nodes to repeat or discuss it.

Relevant network concepts:

* High-centrality accounts reduce the number of hops needed for a claim to reach large audiences.
* Influencers and politicians function as superspreaders.
* Screenshots detach claims from context and make them portable.
* Quote-posts and reaction content spread the claim even when framed critically.
* Memes and AI-generated images mutate the claim into more transmissible forms.

Epidemiological analogy:

```text
R₀ = average resharing rate
superspreaders = high-centrality influencers and politicians
mutation = added images, anecdotes, memes, and unrelated incidents
immunity = skepticism, local knowledge, verification habits
vaccination = prebunking and media literacy
```

Core insight:

Once a claim crosses a certain threshold, its influence becomes partly decoupled from the strength of its original evidence.

## 54. Why corrections struggle

Compare rumor transmission with correction transmission.

Rumor:

```text
“They’re eating pets!”
```

Features:

* Short.
* Shocking.
* Visual.
* Emotionally charged.
* Easy to remember.
* Identity-activating.

Correction:

```text
“Local officials say they have received no credible reports substantiating the allegation.”
```

Features:

* Procedural.
* Bureaucratic.
* Less emotionally satisfying.
* Cognitively demanding.
* Dependent on institutional trust.

Core line:

> The rumor is optimized for human attention. The correction is optimized for institutional accuracy. Those are different optimization functions.

Connect back to the honey ad:

The honey ad uses the same asymmetry. “Doctors hid the cure” is emotionally simpler than an accurate explanation of Alzheimer’s research, clinical uncertainty, and evidentiary standards.

## 55. Identity becomes the filter

As the Springfield rumor spread, many people stopped evaluating it as a factual claim and began evaluating it as an identity signal.

Questions shift from:

```text
What evidence supports this claim?
```

to:

```text
Who is saying this?
Which side is denying it?
What does my community believe?
What does accepting or rejecting it signal about me?
```

Discuss:

* Motivated reasoning.
* Identity-protective cognition.
* Out-group threat narratives.
* The transformation of a factual claim into a loyalty test.
* How debunking can be interpreted as elite suppression.

Connect to the honey ad:

Both cases show prior-shifting. The immediate belief may matter less than the deeper update: “officials deny what ordinary people know,” “experts hide the truth,” or “my community sees what others refuse to see.”

## 56. Participatory disinformation and networked sensemaking

Introduce Kate Starbird’s concept of participatory disinformation.

Key idea:

Many misinformation events do not resemble a single centrally orchestrated propaganda campaign. They emerge through the interactions of thousands of participants who each contribute small pieces to a developing narrative.

Roles can include:

* Original rumor posters.
* Sincere amplifiers.
* Engagement farmers.
* Ideological influencers.
* Partisan media accounts.
* Politicians.
* Meme creators.
* AI-image generators.
* Debunkers.
* Journalists.
* Ordinary users trying to make sense of events.

Important nuance:

Participation does not require malicious intent. Some people believe they are warning others. Some are chasing engagement. Some are making jokes. Some are using the rumor strategically. Some are trying to correct it. The final information environment is collectively produced by people with different motivations.

Core concept:

**Networked sensemaking** — people collectively assemble fragments of posts, anecdotes, screenshots, images, rumors, and commentary into an explanatory narrative, even when the network fails to distinguish evidence from speculation.


Springfield model:

```text

```

Shared mechanisms:

* Emotional arousal.
* Identity activation.
* Distrust of institutions.

* Prior-shifting.
* Difficulty of correction.
* Incentives that reward attention over verification.

Conclusion of section:

The honey ad shows how computational propaganda can be engineered as a funnel. The Springfield rumor shows how computational propaganda can emerge as a participatory cascade. Together, they show why the field must study both paid manipulation and networked amplification.

Possible transition:

The same infrastructure that delivers a fake dementia cure ad also enables rumors, influencers, politicians, memes, and corrections to interact inside a system optimized for attention rather than truth.

# XIV. Research Under Pressure: Why Studying Computational Propaganda Is Becoming Politically Vulnerable

This section links the case study to the broader institutional conditions that make this kind of research possible. After the essay shows how the honey-cure ad works, this section asks whether scholars, journalists, and public-interest researchers will retain the capacity to study computational propaganda at all.

## 62. Introduce the institutional stakes

After showing how the honey-cure ad works as a computational propaganda funnel, broaden the essay from the propaganda system itself to the research ecosystem needed to study it.

Core transition:

The honey ad could be investigated only because certain transparency mechanisms and research methods still exist: ad archives, platform metadata, public records, source tracing, landing-page analysis, misinformation scholarship, health-communication research, synthetic-media analysis, and platform-governance frameworks.

The question becomes: what happens if the institutions capable of studying these systems are politically chilled, defunded, or subordinated to shifting administrative priorities?

## 63. Introduce the OMB proposal

Briefly explain the proposed OMB rewrite of federal financial-assistance rules.

Points to include:

* The proposal concerns federal financial assistance rules, especially 2 CFR Part 200, the Uniform Guidance governing federal grants, cooperative agreements, subawards, cost principles, audits, and termination.
* The stated goals include transparency, accountability, oversight, regulatory clarification, and burden reduction.
* But the proposal also appears to move federal funding toward stronger political-administrative review.
* It could affect not only NIH or NSF-style science, but also universities, nonprofits, hospitals, state and local governments, Tribal governments, public-health programs, and other grant recipients.

Important framing:

This is not “the end of science” in a literal sense. The concern is more structural: federal science funding could become less independent, less stable, less international, and more politically filtered.

## 64. Explain the specific mechanisms that matter for science

Discuss the parts of the proposal most relevant to research independence.

Key mechanisms:

* Senior political appointee review of discretionary awards before issuance.
* Grant programs expected to align with administration policies and priorities.
* Peer review preserved formally but treated as advisory rather than controlling.
* Awards potentially terminated if they no longer fit agency priorities or the “national interest.”
* More restrictive treatment of international collaboration.
* Added constraints around conferences, memberships, subscriptions, publications, and dissemination.

Core argument:

The danger is not that peer review disappears. The danger is that expert review may become subordinate to political review, especially in fields that study politically sensitive topics.

## 65. Explain why misinformation and disinformation research is especially exposed

Misinformation research sits at the intersection of science, politics, elections, platforms, national security, public health, speech regulation, journalism, AI, and content moderation.

That makes it unusually vulnerable to political reframing.

Topics at risk include:

* Election misinformation and election-integrity research.
* Computational propaganda and coordinated influence operations.
* Platform manipulation and coordinated inauthentic behavior.
* Content moderation and platform governance.
* Domestic extremism and online radicalization.
* Conspiracy theory ecosystems.
* Health and vaccine misinformation.
* Climate misinformation.
* AI-generated misinformation, deepfakes, and generative propaganda.
* Foreign influence and information warfare.
* Crisis informatics and rumor research.
* Media literacy, prebunking, debunking, and misinformation interventions.
* Journalism, fact-checking, and information disorder studies.
* Algorithmic amplification and recommender systems.
* Independent technology auditing and platform data access.

Connect directly to the honey ad:

The honey ad touches many of these categories at once: health misinformation, AI/deepfake manipulation, fake journalism, religious targeting, platform advertising, affiliate tracking, opacity, conspiracy rhetoric, and computational persuasion.

## 66. Describe the chilling effect

The most likely danger is not a formal ban on misinformation research. It is a softer process of chilling, filtering, and institutional risk avoidance.

Possible effects:

* Grant programs avoid terms like “misinformation,” “disinformation,” “election integrity,” “platform accountability,” “online harms,” or “content moderation.”
* Review panels may rank proposals highly, but political appointees could override or delay awards.
* Agencies may decline renewals for politically sensitive projects.
* Universities may avoid submitting grants that could attract scrutiny.
* Junior scholars may leave the field because the legal, political, funding, and harassment risks become too high.
* Platforms may face less independent scrutiny if researchers lose funding or institutional support.

Core line:

> The propagandist can iterate. The researcher has to justify their existence.

## 67. Connect this to the attention economy’s asymmetry

Computational propaganda already has an enormous advantage.

Bad actors can:

* Generate new scripts quickly.
* Swap celebrities.
* Rotate domains.
* Create new landing pages.
* Test variants.
* Use affiliates.
* Reframe takedowns as persecution.
* Move between ads, influencers, private groups, and fake news pages.

Researchers, by contrast, need:

* Funding.
* Data access.
* Ethics review.
* Legal protection.
* Institutional support.
* Platform transparency.
* Publication channels.
* Time.

If federal research funding becomes more politically conditional, this asymmetry widens. The propaganda system accelerates while the research system becomes more cautious.

## 68. Analyze “national interest” as a flexible filter

Discuss how the phrase “national interest” can become politically elastic.

Examples:

* Foreign-influence research may be welcomed when it focuses on adversaries, but become controversial when it shows domestic actors amplifying foreign narratives.
* Vaccine misinformation research may be treated as public-health protection under one administration and censorship infrastructure under another.
* Election misinformation research may be framed as defending democracy or as suppressing dissent.
* Climate misinformation research may be seen as protecting science or attacking politically favored industries.

Core point:

The ambiguity of “national interest” can encourage researchers to avoid the most important questions precisely because those questions are politically risky.

## 69. The self-sealing attack on disinformation research

Connect the rhetoric of the honey ad to attacks on misinformation scholarship.

The honey ad says:

* Doctors are hiding the truth.
* The media is suppressing the truth.
* Experts are corrupt.
* Fact-checking is censorship.
* Platform removal proves the claim is real.

Anti-disinformation-research rhetoric can mirror this structure:

* Universities are partisan.
* Platform researchers are government proxies.
* Misinformation studies is narrative control.
* Fact-checkers are ideological enforcers.
* Content-moderation research is censorship infrastructure.

Important nuance:

This does not mean all criticism of disinformation research is invalid. The field should be scrutinized. It should be transparent about methods, careful about civil liberties, and honest about uncertainty. But when the entire field is delegitimized as censorship, the practical effect is to protect propagandists from being studied.

Possible phrase:

> The propaganda system attacks the immune system.

Computational propaganda does not merely exploit public ignorance. It benefits when the institutions capable of investigating it are weakened.
