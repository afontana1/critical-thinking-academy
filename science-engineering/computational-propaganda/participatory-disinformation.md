# Eating the Dogs

The short version is: **yes, the claim appears to have originated as unverified social media rumor, was amplified by influential online accounts, then repeated by major politicians despite repeated denials from local officials that there was evidence to support it.** The famous line "they're eating the dogs" was the culmination of that process, not the beginning.

Here's the timeline.

### 1. Springfield was already under national attention

In 2024, Springfield had become a focus of political debate because thousands of Haitian immigrants had moved there legally over several years. Residents and officials were openly discussing real issues such as housing shortages, school crowding, healthcare capacity, and rapid population growth.

Those genuine concerns became mixed with increasingly sensational rumors online. ([Politico][1])

### 2. The pet-eating rumor began as hearsay on Facebook

The specific accusation about pets appears to trace back to a Facebook post in a private community group.

The post did **not** describe something the author personally witnessed. Instead it relied on multiple layers of hearsay, essentially:

> my neighbor → her daughter's friend → supposedly saw...

The story claimed that a missing cat had been found butchered by Haitian neighbors and also repeated additional rumors about ducks and geese.

After the story became nationally famous, both the Facebook poster and the neighbor whose story she had repeated acknowledged that they had no firsthand knowledge. The original poster said she regretted posting it because it "just exploded," and the underlying story had been based on rumor rather than direct observation. ([WLWT][2])

### 3. Social media accounts amplified it

The Facebook screenshot spread rapidly on X (Twitter), Facebook, TikTok, Reddit, and other platforms.

Several things happened simultaneously:

* screenshots of the Facebook rumor circulated as though it were established fact;
* unrelated images and videos were attached to it;
* an Ohio incident involving a mentally ill American woman in another city who was accused of killing a cat became falsely connected to Springfield and to Haitian immigrants;
* photos of a man carrying geese were circulated without evidence that they had anything to do with eating pets.

Researchers who studied the spread concluded that the rumor grew through repeated reposting by high-follower political accounts before entering mainstream political discourse. ([Wikipedia][3])

### 4. Politicians repeated the claims

After the rumor had already gone viral online, JD Vance posted about Haitian immigrants eating pets in Springfield.

The next day, during the presidential debate, Donald Trump said:

> "They're eating the dogs... they're eating the cats... they're eating the pets..."

That debate moment is why the phrase became internationally famous. ([Wikipedia][3])

### 5. Local officials said they had no evidence

Before and after those statements, Springfield officials repeatedly said they had **no credible reports** supporting the allegations.

This included statements from:

* the Springfield Police Division,
* the city manager,
* local government officials,
* and later Mike DeWine, a Republican, who called the rumors "garbage."

Importantly, this does **not** mean officials claimed no crimes had ever occurred in Springfield. Rather, they specifically said they had received **no credible reports** that Haitian immigrants were stealing and eating residents' pets. ([Business Insider][4])

### 6. The story continued even after being debunked

By that point, the rumor had become politically and culturally significant in its own right.

Even after repeated official denials:

* memes spread widely;
* AI-generated images circulated;
* many supporters continued believing the story;
* Springfield experienced bomb threats and disruptions that officials linked to the national attention surrounding the rumors. ([Business Insider][4])

## Why it spread so easily

The rumor had several characteristics that make misinformation spread rapidly:

* it appealed to existing concerns about immigration;
* it was emotionally shocking ("people are eating pets");
* it appeared to come from a "local resident";
* every repost made it seem more credible, even though nearly all versions ultimately traced back to the same unverified hearsay.

This is a classic example of what's sometimes called **information laundering**: an unverified claim originates in a local social media post, is repeated by increasingly influential accounts, then gains an appearance of legitimacy once prominent public figures repeat it.

So your understanding is essentially accurate. The available reporting indicates the chain was:

> **Unverified hearsay in a Facebook group → viral reposts by influential social media accounts → repetition by politicians → nationwide attention**, while local authorities consistently said they had **no credible evidence** that the alleged pet-eating incidents had occurred. ([Wikipedia][3])

# Computational Propaganda

From a computational propaganda perspective, the Springfield "they're eating the dogs" episode is interesting not because it's unique, but because it follows a pattern that has been observed repeatedly in the study of online information ecosystems. It resembles the way low-credibility information can move from the network periphery to the political center.

One way to think about it is as a series of transformations rather than a single act of "misinformation."

### Stage 1: A rumor exists as a weak signal

Initially, the claim is just one node in a massive graph of online conversation.

It has:

* one or a few sources,
* no independent verification,
* low credibility,
* almost no reach.

Most rumors die here.

Network scientists sometimes describe this as a piece of information with very low "fitness." On its own, it has almost no reproductive success.

---

### Stage 2: Emotional selection

What gives a rumor fitness isn't necessarily truth—it's transmissibility.

Research consistently finds that certain features increase the probability of resharing:

* disgust
* fear
* moral outrage
* threats to children
* threats to pets
* out-group behavior that seems shocking

Notice how the rumor combines several of these.

Instead of saying:

> "Some immigrants may have different food traditions."

(which would generate almost no engagement)

it became

> "They're stealing people's pets."

That produces immediate emotional arousal.

Emotion functions almost like an amplification coefficient in the network.

---

### Stage 3: Information laundering

This is perhaps the most interesting computational phenomenon.

Instead of

> Facebook → millions

the path looks more like

Facebook post

↓

small political accounts

↓

medium influencers

↓

large influencers

↓

politicians

↓

television

↓

millions of ordinary people

Each layer acts as an implicit credibility filter.

People don't ask:

> Did this originate from hearsay?

They ask:

> Why is this senator talking about it?

The source becomes obscured.

In network science, this is similar to **path-dependent trust**.

Credibility is inherited from the messenger rather than the evidence.

---

### Stage 4: Authority creates feedback

Once a politician repeats it, something changes.

The network is no longer merely discussing the rumor.

Now it's discussing:

> "The fact that the candidate said the rumor."

This distinction matters.

Even people trying to debunk it must repeat it.

That dramatically increases its visibility.

Communication scholars sometimes summarize this as:

> Attention is platform-agnostic.

Whether you're endorsing or denying the statement, you're still increasing exposure.

---

### Stage 5: Network cascades

The network now undergoes something like a phase transition.

Before:

```
few hundred people
```

After:

```
millions
```

The reason isn't that millions independently verified anything.

It's that enough high-centrality nodes repeated it.

In graph theory terms, highly connected hubs dramatically reduce the number of hops required for information to reach the entire network.

---

## Why corrections struggle

One of the most robust findings in computational propaganda is that corrections propagate differently from rumors.

Rumor:

> "They're eating pets!"

This is:

* novel
* visual
* emotionally charged
* easy to remember

Correction:

> "Police have received no credible reports substantiating these claims."

This is:

* procedural
* bureaucratic
* less emotional
* cognitively demanding

One is optimized for human attention.

The other is optimized for institutional accuracy.

Those are different optimization functions.

---

## Identity becomes the filter

As the story grows, people stop evaluating evidence directly.

Instead they ask:

> Which side is saying this?

The network reorganizes around identity rather than truth.

Political scientists call this **motivated reasoning** or **identity-protective cognition**.

Now two people can consume the identical information and update in opposite directions.

---

## Preference falsification and pluralistic effects

Once the story is everywhere, many users infer:

> "Everyone is talking about this."

That does not necessarily mean everyone believes it.

It means everyone believes everyone else is discussing it.

Network scientists distinguish between:

* prevalence of discussion
* prevalence of belief

These are very different quantities.

A rumor can dominate discourse while remaining weakly believed.

---

## Why this is called computational propaganda

Computational propaganda doesn't necessarily require bots or fabricated media.

A common definition in the academic literature is the strategic use of digital networks, algorithms, and social media to shape public opinion at scale.

The computational component comes from the interaction between:

* recommendation algorithms,
* engagement-based ranking,
* influencer networks,
* platform affordances (reposts, quote tweets, screenshots),
* and human psychology.

Whether or not any single actor intentionally engineered the Springfield rumor, the **system** exhibited dynamics that computational propaganda researchers study: a low-credibility claim was algorithmically and socially amplified into a nationally salient narrative.

---

## An epidemiological analogy

Network scientists often compare information spread to infectious disease.

Imagine:

* **R₀** (basic reproduction number) ≈ average number of people who reshare something.
* **Superspreaders** ≈ high-centrality accounts (major influencers, politicians, media organizations).
* **Mutation** ≈ changes to the story as it spreads (e.g., adding unrelated images or incidents).
* **Immunity** ≈ skepticism, fact-checking, prior knowledge.
* **Vaccination** ≈ prebunking or media literacy that equips people to recognize common misinformation patterns before encountering them.

The Springfield rumor appears to have crossed a threshold where each "infected" node (person or account sharing it) generated enough additional exposure that the narrative became self-sustaining for a period.

## One broader insight

From a network-dynamics perspective, perhaps the most striking feature is that **the eventual influence of a claim can become largely decoupled from the strength of its original evidence**. Once enough high-centrality nodes adopt or even merely discuss it, the network's behavior is driven less by the claim's evidentiary basis and more by attention, social identity, and the topology of the communication graph. That's why researchers often focus as much on *how* information moves through networks as on whether it is true or false.

# Participatory Disinformation

Yes—with an important nuance. I think it's reasonable to say the Springfield rumor is a **strong example of the dynamics that** Kate Starbird **describes as participatory disinformation**, even if one avoids making claims about the intentions of every individual who participated.

Starbird's work argues that many contemporary misinformation campaigns don't resemble a centrally orchestrated propaganda operation. Instead, they emerge from the interactions of thousands of participants who each contribute a small piece to the evolving narrative.

The Springfield case fits that model remarkably well.

Instead of a single actor inventing and broadcasting a false story, you see a distributed process:

* An unverified local rumor appears.
* Ordinary users repeat it, often believing they are warning others.
* Other users add screenshots, anecdotes, or unrelated images that seem to corroborate it.
* Influential accounts amplify it to larger audiences.
* Politicians cite the apparent volume of discussion as evidence that "people are talking about this."
* News organizations cover the politicians' statements, exposing the rumor to an even wider audience.
* Participants interpret the increased attention as validation, reinforcing the cycle.

This is very different from the classic Cold War conception of disinformation as a fabricated story injected by an intelligence agency. The "production" of the narrative is distributed across the network.

Another key aspect of Starbird's framework is that **participation doesn't require malicious intent**. Different people play different roles:

* Some originate rumors.
* Some sincerely believe they're sharing important information.
* Some amplify because it aligns with their worldview.
* Some amplify for engagement or political advantage.
* Some debunk it—but by repeating it, they can inadvertently increase its visibility.

The resulting information ecosystem is produced collectively, even though participants have different motivations.

The Springfield case also illustrates what Starbird and other researchers describe as **networked sensemaking**. Faced with a rapidly changing social situation—large-scale immigration into a relatively small city—people tried to make sense of events by assembling fragments of information from Facebook posts, local anecdotes, news reports, and political commentary. The problem is that the network doesn't reliably distinguish between evidence and speculation. Stories that are emotionally compelling or identity-affirming can become integrated into a shared narrative despite lacking evidentiary support.

One of Starbird's broader insights is that social media platforms blur the distinction between **information production** and **information consumption**. Every reshare, quote post, comment, or meme is also an act of production that can reshape the narrative. In that sense, the rumor wasn't merely transmitted—it was continually reconstructed as it moved through the network.

I would only avoid saying it is a *definitive* example of participatory disinformation in the sense of proving that every stage was deliberately coordinated. The publicly available evidence doesn't support that stronger claim. Rather, it is a compelling example of the **network dynamics** Starbird studies: decentralized participation, collaborative narrative construction, authority amplification, and the emergence of a widely believed public narrative from an initially weak and unverified signal. Those dynamics can arise with little or no centralized coordination, which is precisely what makes them so important to understand.
