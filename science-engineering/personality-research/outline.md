Yes. Given the earlier essay, I would **not** make this primarily a “here are the psychometric problems with MBTI” article. That risks turning into a narrow debunking piece. The stronger essay uses MBTI/Enneagram as a **case study in the epistemology of explanation**, then asks how much better scientific personality psychology actually does once we impose stricter standards.

That gives you a natural continuation:

> **Previous essay:** What makes an explanation informative rather than merely possible? Why are unconstrained and ad hoc explanations epistemically weak?
> **This essay:** Personality typologies provide an unusually clear real-world example of what happens when weakly constrained descriptions are mistaken for explanations—and even scientific personality research warrants much more modest interpretation than popular discourse usually gives it.

For 10–12 pages, I would aim at roughly **3,500–4,500 words**, depending on formatting.

## Proposed outline

### I. Introduction: When a Description Starts Pretending to Be an Explanation

**~400–500 words / 1–1.5 pages**

Open with something ordinary rather than immediately discussing psychometrics.

For example:

> Why did she withdraw during the argument?
> “She's an INTP.”
>
> Why did he confront his manager?
> “He's an ENTJ.”

The intuitive problem is that these answers *sound* explanatory. They locate the behavior inside a seemingly coherent personality architecture. But what uncertainty have they actually removed?

Connect explicitly to your previous essay:

* An explanation should constrain expectations.
* An explanation that can accommodate almost every possible outcome explains very little.
* Auxiliary hypotheses become ad hoc when they are introduced retrospectively merely to preserve a theory.
* Plausibility is not the same thing as evidential support.

Then introduce personality typologies as the case study.

Your thesis could be something close to:

> **Popular personality systems such as MBTI and the Enneagram are problematic not merely because their categories have weak empirical foundations, but because they encourage an ad hoc mode of explanation: behavior is observed first and then retrospectively assimilated into an elastic personality narrative. More rigorous trait psychology avoids many of these problems, but its findings are considerably narrower than popular interpretations suggest. Reliable covariance, factor structure, or modest predictive validity do not establish fixed psychological essences, causal mechanisms, or explanations of individual behavior.**

That thesis gives you both halves of the essay.

# Part I — Personality as an Explanatory Story

### II. Classification Is Not Explanation

**~500 words / 1–1.5 pages**

This should establish the conceptual distinction before attacking MBTI.

Use simple examples.

Suppose:

> “People who buy coffee four times per week are Frequent Coffee Buyers.”

Then:

> “Why did Jane buy coffee today?”
> “Because Jane is a Frequent Coffee Buyer.”

The category summarizes a pattern partly constituted by the behavior. It doesn't necessarily explain the behavior.

Apply that to:

> “Why did John avoid the party?”
> “Because John is introverted.”

Depending on how introversion was inferred, this may amount to:

> “John tends to avoid social situations because John is someone characterized by tending to avoid social situations.”

Introduce the distinction:

[
\text{classification of regularity} \neq \text{causal explanation of an event}.
]

You can then say that this distinction gets obscured particularly easily in personality discourse because personality vocabulary grammatically resembles causal vocabulary.

“He did it **because he is X**” feels different from “people who frequently do this are called X,” even when the empirical content is nearly identical.

This section lays the philosophical groundwork.

---

### III. MBTI and Enneagram as Unconstrained Explanatory Frameworks

**~600–700 words / 1.5–2 pages**

Now bring in your earlier work on unconstrained explanation directly.

The basic structure:

[
E \rightarrow \text{find personality narrative compatible with }E.
]

Someone withdraws:

> Type 5 withdrawal.

Someone becomes confrontational:

> stress response, wing, function dynamics, integration/disintegration, etc.

Someone behaves contrary to the stereotypical type:

> developed functions, shadow functions, masking, unusual circumstances, subtype variation.

Your point is **not** that every one of those concepts is necessarily logically impossible.

It is that the framework possesses so many adjustable interpretive resources that it becomes difficult to specify observations that would seriously count against it.

You could formalize this:

[
\forall E,\exists A_E:\quad T+A_E\text{ accommodates }E.
]

Here (T) is the personality framework and (A_E) is an auxiliary interpretation introduced after observing (E).

Then connect explicitly to ad hocness:

A legitimate auxiliary hypothesis has evidence independently of the phenomenon it rescues.

An ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis exists primarily because the observation otherwise threatens the theory.

That's one of the strongest bridges to your previous essay.

### IV. Why “It Fits Me” Is Weak Evidence

**~400–500 words / ~1 page**

This is where Barnum effects, confirmation bias, flexible interpretation, and retrospective selection belong.

Emphasize the size of the hypothesis space.

Humans have enormous behavioral histories. People have been:

* outgoing and withdrawn,
* confident and uncertain,
* generous and selfish,
* spontaneous and deliberate,
* emotional and detached.

A rich enough description can almost always retrieve confirming episodes.

So:

[
\text{large behavioral history}
+
\text{flexible description}
+
\text{selective memory}
\rightarrow
\text{subjective accuracy}.
]

Then connect this to explanatory discrimination.

The relevant question isn't:

[
P(E\mid T)\text{ seems high}.
]

It's whether:

[
\frac{P(E\mid T)}{P(E\mid T')}
]

meaningfully discriminates (T) from competing accounts.

If MBTI, Enneagram, attachment styles, childhood narratives, incentives, ordinary situational explanations, and even astrology can all produce plausible retrospective stories about (E), then mere narrative compatibility carries very little evidence.

That would nicely echo the methodological argument from the earlier essay.

# Part II — From Pseudomechanism to Identity

### V. Scientific-Sounding Vocabulary Is Not a Mechanism

**~400–500 words / ~1 page**

This section should address why these systems can feel more sophisticated than horoscopes.

Discuss vocabulary such as:

* dominant/inferior functions,
* introverted intuition,
* cognitive stacks,
* wings,
* instinctual variants,
* integration/disintegration.

The vocabulary gives the appearance of intervening causal structure:

[
\text{Type}\rightarrow\text{cognitive mechanism}\rightarrow\text{behavior}.
]

But ask:

1. Can the supposed mechanism be measured independently of the behavior?
2. Does it generate distinctive predictions?
3. Can its operation be distinguished from competing mechanisms?
4. Do observations capable of falsifying it exist?

Otherwise:

> “She behaves this way because of Ni.”

may merely relocate the original observation behind another noun.

A nice sentence here would be:

> **Naming an invisible intermediary is not the same thing as discovering a mechanism.**

This section also lets you make the astrology comparison carefully: not “MBTI literally equals astrology,” but both can achieve explanatory richness by proliferating interpretive vocabulary without corresponding increases in constraint.

---

### VI. From Category to Identity: When the Model Enters the Data-Generating Process

**~600–700 words / 1.5–2 pages**

This is probably the most original and interesting section of the essay.

The problem isn't finished when somebody incorrectly classifies themselves.

The label can become performative:

[
\text{label}
\rightarrow
\text{self-conception}
\rightarrow
\text{attention}
\rightarrow
\text{decision}
\rightarrow
\text{behavior}
\rightarrow
\text{apparent confirmation}.
]

Examples:

> “I'm an INTP, so networking just isn't me.”

> “I'm a Five, so withdrawing is simply how I process conflict.”

> “We're incompatible because I'm X and she's Y.”

> “This career doesn't suit my personality type.”

Your econometric framing would work beautifully here.

Normally we imagine the personality measure (L_i) as measuring something that predicts future (Y):

[
L_i\rightarrow Y_{i,t+1}.
]

But once the individual learns the classification:

[
L_i\rightarrow M_{i,t+1}\rightarrow Y_{i,t+1},
]

where (M) is their self-model.

Now the measurement itself has become a treatment.

This introduces:

* endogenous sorting,
* treatment effects from classification,
* missing counterfactuals,
* self-confirmation,
* selective exposure.

Your dating example is perfect.

If someone believes certain types are incompatible and therefore never dates those people, the supposed theory helps determine which potential outcomes are ever observed.

You might make this your transition into therapy:

> That epistemic problem becomes considerably more consequential when the narrative is supplied or reinforced by someone occupying a position of psychological authority.

---

### VII. Why This Matters More in Therapy and Relationships

**~400–500 words / ~1 page**

Keep this focused rather than turning the essay into an indictment of therapists generally.

The concern is that therapy influences interpretation.

Contrast:

> “You withdraw because you're a Type 5.”

with:

* When does withdrawal happen?
* Around whom?
* What happened immediately before it?
* What did you expect would happen if you stayed engaged?
* What incentives or fears were operating?
* When have you behaved differently?
* What changed in those cases?

The first compresses heterogeneous events into an essence.

The latter preserves variation and potentially identifies mechanisms.

You can make an important ethical/epistemic point:

> **The danger of a personality narrative is not only that it may incorrectly describe a person. It may unnecessarily narrow the set of selves that person considers available to them.**

That's stronger than “MBTI isn't scientific.”

# Part III — What Happens When Personality Research Becomes Scientific?

### VIII. The Big Five Is Not MBTI—but That Doesn't Mean What People Think It Means

**~450–550 words / 1–1.5 pages**

Now make the essay intellectually fair.

Say explicitly:

Scientific trait psychology should **not** simply be conflated with MBTI or Enneagram.

The Big Five has:

* much stronger empirical development,
* systematic psychometric research,
* replication across many datasets,
* reliability evidence,
* factor-analytic research,
* predictive associations,
* longitudinal research.

But then draw the crucial boundary:

[
\text{better measurement}\not\Rightarrow\text{causal ontology}.
]

You can characterize the Big Five modestly as something like:

> a relatively successful low-dimensional description of covariance among personality-related observations.

That is a significant scientific accomplishment.

But it doesn't establish five psychological organs, modules, essences, or causal forces.

This section prevents critics from dismissing the essay as “the author doesn't understand modern personality research.”

---

### IX. The Psychometric Validity Staircase

**~700–900 words / ~2 pages**

This should probably be the technical centerpiece.

Instead of exhaustively explaining every COSMIN category at textbook length, organize them around a progressive inference problem.

Start with:

**Content validity:** What exactly is the construct supposed to contain? Is the item universe independently specified, or defined partly through the same personality vocabulary being validated?

**Structural validity:** CFA/EFA can demonstrate an approximate covariance structure conditional on modeling assumptions. It does not identify causal structure.

**Internal consistency:** coherent responses demonstrate covariance, not why the covariance exists.

**Test–retest reliability:** stable scores demonstrate persistence of scores, not whether that persistence originates in genes, stable environments, habits, occupational circumstances, identity, measurement bias, or a latent causal trait.

**Construct/convergent validity:** personality inventory A correlating with personality inventory B shows agreement among operationalizations but can become partially self-referential without independent criteria.

**Criterion validity:** personality constructs generally lack the equivalent of an independently observable gold standard.

**Cross-cultural invariance:** instability across language/culture raises questions about whether the same quantities are being measured in comparable ways.

**Measurement error and interpretability:** population-level research can tolerate uncertainty that becomes much more problematic when making claims about an individual.

Then present the staircase:

[
\text{Items covary}
]

[
\Downarrow
]

[
\text{Scores are reliable}
]

[
\Downarrow
]

[
\text{Low-dimensional model summarizes covariance}
]

[
\Downarrow
]

[
\text{Structure generalizes across populations}
]

[
\Downarrow
]

[
\text{Score predicts some future outcomes}
]

[
\Downarrow
]

[
\text{Factor is a causal psychological property}
]

[
\Downarrow
]

[
\text{Factor explains this person's behavior}
]

[
\Downarrow
]

[
\text{Factor should guide this person's decisions}.
]

Then make the main point:

**Evidence at one rung does not automatically validate the next.**

MBTI-style discourse tends to race from the first couple of rungs to the last three.

Scientific personality psychology generally doesn't—but its popular interpretation frequently does.

### X. Prediction Is Not Explanation

**~450–550 words / 1 page**

This section can reconnect personality research directly to your previous essay.

Conscientiousness may predict occupational or academic outcomes at some level.

Okay.

But suppose:

[
P(Y\mid C)
]

differs somewhat by conscientiousness score.

That does not tell us:

[
C\rightarrow Y
]

nor does it explain why particular person (i) produced outcome (Y_i).

Predictive usefulness and causal understanding are different achievements.

You can invoke an econometric analogy:

A variable can improve out-of-sample prediction without being an interpretable structural parameter.

Likewise, predictive personality coefficients may summarize stable heterogeneity without identifying the causal machinery generating it.

This allows a balanced formulation:

> “Conscientiousness contains no information” is too strong.

But:

> “Conscientiousness explains why this employee succeeds”

is also far too strong.

### XI. What Might “Personality” Actually Be?

**~500–600 words / 1–1.5 pages**

Now offer the constructive alternative.

Rather than saying personality doesn't exist, ask what ontology the evidence warrants.

Maybe:

[
Y_{it}
======

f(H_{it},S_{it},I_{it},C_i,B_{it},\epsilon_{it})
]

where behavior depends on:

* history,
* current situation,
* incentives,
* culture,
* physiological state,
* learning,
* relationships,
* relatively persistent individual heterogeneity.

What we call personality might summarize the person's **distribution of states across situations**, rather than represent a fixed substance inside them.

This preserves:

* persistence,
* behavioral regularities,
* some predictability,

while allowing:

* development,
* cultural dependence,
* situational variation,
* path dependence,
* behavioral plasticity.

A strong formulation:

> Personality may be less like a hidden object that causes behavior and more like a statistical description of the trajectories a person tends to occupy.

Then emphasize that even this is an empirical/modeling proposal, not something established merely by factor analysis.

---

# Conclusion

### XII. The Seduction of Feeling Explained

**~350–450 words / ~1 page**

Return to the initial example.

The attraction of MBTI and Enneagram is understandable. Human beings are impossibly complicated. These systems compress that uncertainty into a small number of intelligible narratives.

That compression is psychologically satisfying.

But epistemically:

[
\text{feeling understood}\neq\text{being explained}.
]

Your ending could distinguish three claims:

> “I often behave this way.”
> “This questionnaire summarizes that tendency.”
> “I behave this way because this is what I fundamentally am.”

Each claim is dramatically stronger than the previous one.

Likewise:

> “This framework gave me useful language for reflection”

does not imply

> “this framework has discovered the causal structure of my personality.”

And I'd close by returning to the theme of the previous essay:

> **A good explanation earns its power by constraining possibilities. Personality systems become most seductive precisely when they do the opposite—when their conceptual flexibility allows them to make nearly every human behavior intelligible after the fact. The result may be a compelling narrative, but compelling narratives are cheap. The harder question is what the framework predicted, what it ruled out, what evidence could have contradicted it, and what causal uncertainty it actually resolved.**

That gives the essay a satisfying circular structure.

## The overall architecture

I'd think of the essay as three escalating arguments:

**First:** MBTI/Enneagram fail as serious explanatory theories because their flexibility enables retrospective accommodation.

**Second:** their greater danger is performative—the categories can become identities and thereby begin producing some of the behavior they supposedly merely describe.

**Third:** rigorous personality psychology is vastly more defensible, but we still need to resist the slide from **measurement → covariance → prediction → mechanism → essence → prescription**.

That last move is important because it keeps the piece from sounding like “MBTI is fake; Big Five is real.” Your conclusion is more philosophically interesting:

> **There is a spectrum of epistemic warrant, not a binary division between scientific and nonscientific personality language. The strongest research supports narrower claims than ordinary personality discourse wants it to support. MBTI and Enneagram are especially problematic because they routinely make the largest claims with the weakest constraints.**

For a 10–12 page essay, I would make Sections III, VI, and IX the longest. Those are your three pillars: **ad hoc explanation, performative identity, and the psychometric validity staircase.** Everything else should serve those arguments rather than becoming a comprehensive history of personality psychology.
