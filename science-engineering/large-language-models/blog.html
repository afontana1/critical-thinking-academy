Large Language Models and AGI

I originally intended to write a multi-post analysis of large language models (LLMs) and artificial general intelligence (AGI), especially the problems with conflating the two or assuming that progress in the former makes the latter likely. I eventually decided that it would be more useful to organize the resources that led me to this conclusion and explain how they fit together, rather than reproduce each argument in a watered-down form.

Core thesis. The impressive capabilities of current LLMs do not, by themselves, resolve the deeper problems of reasoning, semantic understanding, agency, embodiment, relevance, memory, and cognitive architecture that arise when we move from language modeling to claims about general intelligence. This is not an argument that LLMs are useless, nor does every source below establish that AGI is impossible. The narrower claim is that “better language modeling” and “general intelligence” should not be treated as interchangeable propositions.

Part 1

Framing the Claim

Separate operational AGI, agency, intelligence, consciousness, and LLM capability before evaluating the critiques.

Before evaluating the sources, it helps to separate several questions that are often collapsed into one another. Artificial General Intelligence is usually framed operationally: can an intelligent agent perform across the range of intellectual tasks associated with humans? That framing is already different from asking whether a system has a mind, consciousness, or intrinsic semantic understanding.

Path through this section: definitions → varieties of agency → Strong AI versus operational competence → risk → technical background

An agent, in the broadest sense, is an abstract actor capable of acting in an environment. In classical computer-science discussions, the computational substrate is usually assumed to be expressible in the Turing-machine model. The concepts of agency and intentionality are deep philosophical topics, and I will not try to resolve them here. In a technical sense, agency can refer to the capacity to set goals, form criteria for evaluating those goals, construct a model of an environment, and adapt that model in response to feedback.

That definition is not comprehensive because different disciplines operationalize agents differently. In economics, agents may be idealized buyers and sellers optimizing an objective function. In agent-based models, agents can be autonomous objects following predefined update rules. Computer science adds software agents and multi-agent systems. Calling all of these things “agents” does not mean that they possess agency in the richer psychological or philosophical sense.

Within AGI discussions, an agent is often treated as generally intelligent if it can act autonomously and match or surpass humans across human tasks. That is an operational criterion. It says very little by itself about whether such a system would be conscious or possess a “mind” in the Strong AI sense. From an engineering perspective, those philosophical questions may be outside the immediate design objective, even though they become difficult to ignore once systems exhibit unexpectedly sophisticated behavior.

One aim of this essay is therefore to resist a slide from “LLMs produce increasingly competent behavior” to “LLMs are thereby approaching Strong AI.” I do not think a statistical model of language, simply by becoming larger, gives us a theory of consciousness. More importantly for the narrower AGI question, the sources below identify unresolved problems in reasoning, semantic grounding, memory, relevance, agency, embodiment, and cognitive architecture.

A note on risk

I am also skeptical of framing existential risk from AGI primarily through anthropomorphic stories about a machine acquiring human-like motives. That skepticism should not be confused with saying that advanced AI systems are harmless. The more immediate risk, in my view, comes from inserting probabilistic subsystems into increasingly interconnected and complex higher-level systems, where errors can propagate into cascading failures.

Once these systems are embedded in environments characterized by Knightian uncertainty, the problem becomes a form of epistemic risk whose full probability distribution may not be calculable in advance. In that sense, the danger can remain an all-too-human systems-engineering problem even if the underlying model has no consciousness or human-like intentionality.

Finally, AGI presupposes a particular operational definition of intelligence that does not necessarily align with cognitive science, psychology, or animal intelligence. A recurring theme below is whether intelligence can be abstracted away from the evolutionary and embodied history through which biological cognition develops.

Technical background. Before reading the critiques, it is useful to understand what pretrained generative transformers are doing under the hood. Stephen Wolfram’s “What Is ChatGPT Doing … and Why Does It Work?” gives an accessible account of next-token prediction, embeddings, training/validation/test data, and neural networks. Familiarity with different theories of mind, philosophy of mind, and related concepts in philosophy of mind will also help because those questions recur throughout the critiques.

Statistical Learning, Reasoning, and Relevance

What does general intelligence require beyond statistical generalization?

The first cluster concerns reasoning rather than consciousness. Even if we bracket the question of whether a model “really understands,” an AGI claim still has to explain abstraction, analogy, prior knowledge, relevance selection, open-ended inference, and adaptation to new contexts.

Deep Learning: A Critical Appraisal by Gary Marcus

Summary:

The author lists ten concerns for deep learning

  • Deep learning thus far is data hungry
  • Deep learning thus far is shallow and has limited capacity for transfer
  • Deep learning thus far has no natural way to deal with hierarchical structure
  • Deep learning thus far has struggled with open-ended inference
  • Deep learning thus far is not sufficiently transparent
  • Deep learning thus far has not been well integrated with prior knowledge
  • Deep learning thus far cannot inherently distinguish causation from correlation
  • Deep learning presumes a largely stable world, in ways that may be problematic
  • Deep learning thus far works well as an approximation, but its answers often cannot be fully trusted
  • Deep learning thus far is difficult to engineer with

My analysis

Several of Marcus's criticisms overlap with issues raised elsewhere in this essay. I also think some are more tractable than others. Engineering difficulty, for example, has improved substantially as open-source tools and model ecosystems have matured, and some approximation problems may be reduced through better systems design.

The deeper concerns are the ones involving hierarchical structure, transfer, prior knowledge, open-ended inference, and the distinction between correlation and causation. If a neural network is fundamentally a statistical model, there is always some room for error in relations that humans often treat as structurally basic—for example, recognizing a relation such as “on top of” and carrying it systematically into a new context.

Lexical semantics contains many such relations: taxonomic and meronomic relations, contrast, congruence, chaining, ranking, grading, degree, synonymy, homonymy, hyponymy, and many others. My concern is that systematic command of these relations requires conceptual schemas and reasoning over them, not only statistical proximity in a learned representation space.

Neural Networks are not Reasoning Systems

My analysis

When you interact with a chatbot, its output can look like reasoning and persuasion. My claim is narrower: the underlying language model is not, merely by virtue of next-token prediction, an explicit reasoning system with its own intentional goals. It does not natively come with a formal mechanism for abductive reasoning grounded in common knowledge, a stable formal ontology, or an explicit system of knowledge representation and reasoning.

That distinction matters because humans routinely use defeasible reasoning and commonsense reasoning, revising conclusions when the context changes. Humans can often adapt after a small amount of new evidence instead of requiring large-scale iterative retraining. The open question is whether the statistical machinery of an LLM can supply these capabilities systematically or whether additional representational and inferential machinery is required.

Melanie Mitchell's work on abstraction and analogy is especially relevant here. One of the most useful features of human intelligence is the ability to construct analogies and abstract relations between concrete objects or concepts, then carry those relations into a new domain. Machine-learning systems certainly generalize, but their generalization is learned statistically. The question is whether that is equivalent to the kind of structured conceptual generalization humans perform, or only an approximation to it.

The Frame Problem

Summary:

In the confined world of a robot, surroundings are not static. Many varying forces or actions can cause changes or modifications to it. The problem of forcing a robot to adapt to these changes is the basis of the frame problem in artificial intelligence. Information in the knowledge base and the robot's conclusions combine to form the input for what the robot's subsequent action should be. A good selection from its facts can be made by discarding or ignoring irrelevant facts and ridding of results that could have negative side effects. (Dennett) (Fischler 304-5).

A robot must introduce facts that are relevant to a particular moment. That is, a robot will examine its current situation, and then look up the facts that will be beneficial to choosing its subsequent action. The robot should also search for any changeable facts. It then examines these facts to determine if any of them have been changed during a previous examination. There are two basic types of change:

  • Relevant Change: inspect the changes made by an action
  • Irrelevant Change: do not inspect facts that are not related to the task at hand
  • Facts may be examined utilizing two levels:
    • Semantic Level: This level interprets what kind of information is being examined. Solutions should become obvious by the assumptions of how an object should behave. There are believers in a purely semantic approach who believe that correct information can be reached via meaning. However, this hypothesis has yet to be proven.
    • Syntactic Level: This level simply decides in which format the information should be inspected. That is, it forms solutions based on the structure and patterns of facts.
  • When inspecting the facts, various problems can occur:
    • Sometimes an implication can be missed.
    • Considering all facts and all their subsequent side effects is time-consuming.
    • Some facts are unnecessarily examined when they are unneeded.

My analysis

The frame problem leads directly to the problem of relevance. John Vervaeke, for example, discusses a family of ideas under the heading “relevance realization.” I first became sensitive to the issue while studying argumentation: how do we determine which possible counterargument is actually relevant to a topic rather than merely logically available?

There is no simple working definition of relevance that tells an agent which facts to consider in every situation. More importantly, we do not fully understand how sentient agents search an enormous combinatorial space while retaining only the salient features. I suspect a solution will require a deeper account of defeasible reasoning and heuristics—mechanisms that reduce the effective search space without exhaustively enumerating it.

That is where the comparison with LLMs becomes important. We improve statistical generalization by exposing models to enormous amounts of information, whereas humans can often make useful choices without searching anything like the full state space. I do not think current AI methods yet explain that gap.

The Chinese Room Argument

Summary:

Searle's thought experiment begins with this hypothetical premise: suppose that artificial intelligence research has succeeded in constructing a computer that behaves as if it understands Chinese. It takes Chinese characters as input and, by following the instructions of a computer program, produces other Chinese characters, which it presents as output. Suppose, says Searle, that this computer performs its task so convincingly that it comfortably passes the Turing test: it convinces a human Chinese speaker that the program is itself a live Chinese speaker. To all of the questions that the person asks, it makes appropriate responses, such that any Chinese speaker would be convinced that they are talking to another Chinese-speaking human being. The question Searle wants to answer is this: does the machine literally "understand" Chinese?

Or is it merely simulating the ability to understand Chinese?[6][d] Searle calls the first position "strong AI" and the latter "weak AI". He begins with three axioms (A1) "Programs are formal (syntactic)." A program uses syntax to manipulate symbols and pays no attention to the semantics of the symbols. It knows where to put the symbols and how to move them around, but it does not know what they stand for or what they mean. For the program, the symbols are just physical objects like any others. (A2) "Minds have mental contents (semantics)." Unlike the symbols used by a program, our thoughts have meaning: they represent things and we know what it is they represent. (A3) "Syntax by itself is neither constitutive of nor sufficient for semantics." This is what the Chinese room thought experiment is intended to prove: the Chinese room has syntax (because there is a man in there moving symbols around).

The Chinese room has no semantics (because, according to Searle, there is no one or nothing in the room that understands what the symbols mean). Therefore, having syntax is not enough to generate semantics. Searle posits that these lead directly to this conclusion: (C1) Programs are neither constitutive of nor sufficient for minds. This should follow without controversy from the first three: Programs don't have semantics. Programs have only syntax, and syntax is insufficient for semantics.

Every mind has semantics. Therefore no programs are minds.

My analysis

Searle's central distinction is between syntax and semantics. A digital computer ultimately implements formal transformations over representations according to rules. At the level of a Turing machine, the description is entirely syntactic: the machine changes states and manipulates symbols without the formalism itself supplying an interpretation of what those symbols mean.

The Chinese example makes that gap intuitive. Suppose I see a Chinese symbol and consult a registry that maps it to an English symbol. Following the registry could let me produce correct outputs without implying that I understand Chinese. Searle's question is why a sufficiently elaborate version of that formal procedure should suddenly count as semantic understanding rather than increasingly successful symbol manipulation.

I do not find the standard responses compelling enough to dissolve the problem. That does not mean AGI is impossible: a different computational paradigm, perhaps including analog or other nonstandard approaches, could change the analysis. But if Searle's anti-functionalist conclusion is right, then behavioral equivalence implemented on the current digital paradigm would not, by itself, establish that a machine has a mind.

Symbol Grounding Problem by Stevan Harnad

Abstract:

There has been much discussion recently about the scope and limits of purely symbolic models of the mind and about the proper role of connectionism in cognitive modeling. This paper describes the symbol grounding problem : How can the semantic interpretation of a formal symbol system be made intrinsic to the system, rather than just parasitic on the meanings in our heads? How can the meanings of the meaningless symbol tokens, manipulated solely on the basis of their shapes, be grounded in anything but other meaningless symbols? The problem is analogous to trying to learn Chinese from a Chinese/Chinese dictionary alone. A candidate solution is sketched: Symbolic representations must be grounded bottom-up in nonsymbolic representations of two kinds: iconic representations, which are analogs of the proximal sensory projections of distal objects and events, and categorical representations, which are learned and innate feature-detectors that pick out the invariant features of object and event categories from their sensory projections.

Elementary symbols are the names of these object and event categories, assigned on the basis of their categorical representations. Higher-order symbolic representations, grounded in these elementary symbols, consist of symbol strings describing category membership relations. Connectionism is one natural candidate for the mechanism that learns the invariant features underlying categorical representations, thereby connecting names to the proximal projections of the distal objects they stand for. In this way connectionism can be seen as a complementary component in a hybrid nonsymbolic/symbolic model of the mind, rather than a rival to purely symbolic modeling. Such a hybrid model would not have an autonomous symbolic module, however; the symbolic functions would emerge as an intrinsically dedicated symbol system as a consequence of the bottom-up grounding of categories ' names in their sensory representations.

Symbol manipulation would be governed not just by the arbitrary shapes of the symbol tokens, but by the nonarbitrary shapes of the icons and category invariants in which they are grounded

My analysis

I see the symbol-grounding problem as closely related to the Chinese Room. The basic question is deceptively simple: how does an arbitrary word or symbol become connected to meaning? The philosophical problem of reference runs from Frege and Peirce to Kripke. Is meaning a property a symbol can possess on its own, or does meaning require a mind, a practice, or a communicative context that establishes what the symbol refers to?

This matters for language models because an LLM—or another statistical model of language such as latent Dirichlet analysis—can discover regularities in a corpus without the statistical operation itself specifying what the tokens refer to in the world. The model processes relations among symbols; the grounding question asks how those relations acquire semantic content rather than merely additional symbolic structure.

Could we construct an algorithm that generates intrinsic semantic content? I regard that problem as fundamentally unsolved, and I am skeptical that it can be solved within a system whose representations are grounded only in other symbols. Harnad's own proposal is important precisely because it points toward nonsymbolic sensory and categorical grounding rather than treating more symbol manipulation as sufficient.

Dancing with pixies: strong artificial intelligence and panpsychism.

Abstract:

The argument presented in this paper is not a direct attack or defence of the Chinese Room Argument (CRA), but relates to the premise at its heart, that syntax is not sufficient for semantics, via the closely associated propositions that semantics is not intrinsic to syntax and that syntax is not intrinsic to physics. However, in contrast to the CRA’s critique of the link between syntax and semantics, this paper will explore the associated link between syntax and physics. The main argument presented here is not significantly original – it is a simple reflection upon that originally given by Hilary Putnam (Putnam 1988) and criticised by David Chalmers and others: instead of seeking to justify Putnam’s claim that, “every open system implements every Finite State Automaton (FSA)”, and hence that psychological states of the brain cannot be functional states of a computer, I will seek to establish the weaker result that, over a finite time window every open system implements the trace of a particular FSA Q, as it executes program (p) on input (x). That this result leads to panpsychism is clear as, equating Q (p, x) to a specific Strong AI program that is claimed to instantiate phenomenal states as it executes, and following Putnam’s procedure, identical computational (and ex hypothesi phenomenal) states (ubiquitous little ‘pixies’) can be found in every open physical system.

My analysis

Bishop is arguing against the Computational Theory of Mind and, more broadly, the functionalist view of mind. The argument starts from the idea that if mental states are nothing more than computational states—ultimately describable as the operation of a deterministic finite automaton within a Turing-style computational framework—then the same relevant computational traces can be mapped onto ordinary physical systems.

The resulting conclusion is a form of panpsychism: if the computation alone is sufficient for mind, then we appear committed to finding “mind” in mundane objects such as rocks whenever the appropriate computational description can be imposed on them. Bishop therefore uses a reductio ad absurdum: identifying mind with a sufficiently abstract finite-state machine seems to generate consequences that are difficult to accept.

Artificial Intelligence is stupid and causal reasoning won't fix it

Abstract:

Artificial Neural Networks have reached Grandmaster and even super-human performance across a variety of games: from those involving perfect-information (such as Go) to those involving imperfect-information (such as Starcraft). Such technological developments from AI-labs have ushered concomitant applications across the world of business - where an AI brand tag is fast becoming ubiquitous. A corollary of such widespread commercial deployment is that when AI gets things wrong - an autonomous vehicle crashes; a chatbot exhibits racist behaviour; automated credit scoring processes discriminate on gender etc. - there are often significant financial, legal and brand consequences and the incident becomes major news. As Judea Pearl sees it, the underlying reason for such mistakes is that, 'all the impressive achievements of deep learning amount to just curve fitting'. The key, Judea Pearl suggests, is to replace reasoning by association with causal-reasoning - the ability to infer causes from observed phenomena.

It is a point that was echoed by Gary Marcus and Ernest Davis in a recent piece for the New York Times: 'we need to stop building computer systems that merely get better and better at detecting statistical patterns in data sets - often using an approach known as Deep Learning - and start building computer systems that from the moment of their assembly innately grasp three basic concepts: time, space and causality'. In this paper, foregrounding what in 1949 Gilbert Ryle termed a category mistake, I will offer an alternative explanation for AI errors: it is not so much that AI machinery cannot grasp causality, but that AI machinery - qua computation - cannot understand anything at all.

My analysis

This is closely related to Bishop's argument in Dancing with Pixies. The paper's conclusion is stronger than the familiar claim that current AI lacks adequate causal reasoning: it argues that computation, considered purely as computation, does not understand anything at all.

I find that line of argument persuasive, and it connects directly to the Chinese Room and symbol-grounding problems discussed above. The important point for the structure of this essay is that “add causal reasoning” and “solve understanding” are not necessarily the same project.

Important distinction

These arguments target different claims and should not be treated as interchangeable. An operational AGI criterion based on task performance does not logically require consciousness. The Chinese Room and symbol-grounding arguments are stronger when used to challenge claims about intrinsic understanding or mind than when treated as automatic refutations of every behaviorally defined form of AGI.

That distinction also identifies the strongest burden for this essay: if semantic understanding is not required for an operational definition of AGI, then the case against LLM-to-AGI must also stand on the reasoning, relevance, learning, memory, agency, and architecture arguments developed elsewhere in the article.

Intelligence as Embodied, Evolved, and Situated

Biological intelligence is not blank-slate induction; it develops through evolved priors, bodies, environments, and histories.

Human cognition did not arise as a generic learner exposed to a text corpus. It developed within bodies, ecologies, social systems, and evolutionary histories. The sources in this section motivate that observation from several directions.

Core knowledge: Elizabeth S. Spelke

Abstract:

Complex cognitive skills such as reading and calculation and complex cognitive achievements such as formal science and mathematics may depend on a set of building block systems that emerge early in human ontogeny and phylogeny. These core knowledge systems show characteristic limits of domain and task specificity: Each serves to represent a particular class of entities for a particular set of purposes. By combining representations from these systems, however human cognition may achieve extraordinary flexibility. Studies of cognition in human infants and in nonhuman primates therefore may contribute to understanding unique features of human knowledge. 2020 APA, all rights reserved)

My analysis

I think core knowledge raises a fundamental issue for AI because human cognition does not appear to begin as a blank slate. We seem to arrive with cognitive mechanisms that make some kinds of learning dramatically easier, and those mechanisms themselves are products of a long evolutionary history. Their presence also suggests continuity with other mammals that share portions of this inherited cognitive machinery.

This is related to Chomsky's argument that humans possess an innate capacity for language rather than learning linguistic structure entirely through unconstrained induction. Spelke's work broadens the point: some representations or processing biases may be present by default and then combined into more flexible cognition.

For LLMs, the relevant question is therefore not simply whether a sufficiently large statistical learner can fit the data. It is whether the learner contains—or can acquire—the kinds of structured priors that biological cognition starts with. A statistical model does not automatically inherit those priors merely by being exposed to more text.

14b. Iris Berent: Language universals at birth

Abstract:

The evolution of human languages is driven both by primitive biases present in the human sensorimotor systems and by cultural transmission among speakers. However, whether the design of the language faculty is further shaped by linguistic biological biases remains controversial. To address this question, we used near-infrared spectroscopy to examine whether the brain activity of neonates is sensitive to a putatively universal phonological constraint. Across languages, syllables like blif are preferred to both lbif and bdif. Newborn infants (2–5 d old) listening to these three types of syllables displayed distinct hemodynamic responses in temporal-perisylvian areas of their left hemisphere.

Moreover, the oxyhemoglobin concentration changes elicited by a syllable type mirrored both the degree of its preference across languages and behavioral linguistic preferences documented experimentally in adulthood. These findings suggest that humans possess early, experience-independent, linguistic biases concerning syllable structure that shape language perception and acquisition.

My analysis

This is another example of the same issue. If newborns exhibit experience-independent linguistic biases, then human language acquisition cannot be described as brute-force statistical induction from an initially structureless learner. The relevance to AI is not that engineered systems must copy those particular biological biases, but that a theory of general learning has to explain what plays the corresponding role.

Grow Smart and Die Young: Why Did Cephalopods Evolve Intelligence?

Abstract:

The most influential views on the evolution of intelligence suggest that intelligence coevolves with slow life history in response to socioecological challenges; however, these conclusions are primarily focused on large-brained vertebrates. Cephalopod mollusks strongly challenge the most accepted hypotheses on the evolution of intelligence: cephalopods evolved complex brains and high behavioral flexibility together with fast life histories and in simple social environments. Surprisingly, the evolution of intelligence in cephalopods has been largely overlooked, thus leaving this evolutionary conundrum unsolved. Discussing differences and similarities between cephalopods and large-brained vertebrates, may shed light on fundamental aspects of the evolution of intelligence.

My analysis

AGI research often uses intelligence in a highly operational sense, partly because an engineering project needs a formal description that can be implemented on a digital computer. There are important exceptions: biologically inspired computing and concepts such as swarm intelligence already recognize that intelligence need not take a single form. My concern is with treating cognition as a Platonic algorithm that can be cleanly separated from the evolutionary history that produced it.

The human case can also become too dominant. There are many forms of intelligence across the animal world, and cephalopods are especially interesting because their neural organization is radically different from ours and is distributed throughout the body. Evidence that cephalopod intelligence evolved under pressures such as predation suggests that intelligence is shaped by the problems an organism repeatedly has to solve.

I suspect human intelligence is similarly path-dependent. If so, “the algorithm” cannot simply be discussed as though its history were irrelevant; the path dependence of a cognitive system may help determine the representations, priors, and behavioral capacities that the mature system possesses.

My analysis

This line of research has been especially motivating to me because of the NIH Human Microbiome Project and related work on the gut–brain relationship. That matters because treating AGI or consciousness as something separable from developmental and evolutionary history risks turning cognition into a Platonic abstraction. Understanding human cognition may therefore require understanding the biological systems that make the organism viable in the first place.

My claim is not merely that “the gut affects the brain.” It is that the organism exists in a symbiotic relationship with microbial life, so cognitive function and cognitive degradation may depend in part on maintaining that relationship. How Organisms Come to Know the World: Fundamental Limits on Artificial General Intelligence

Abstract:

Artificial intelligence has made tremendous advances since its inception about seventy years ago. Self-driving cars, programs beating experts at complex games, and smart robots capable of assisting people that need care are just some among the successful examples of machine intelligence. This kind of progress might entice us to envision a society populated by autonomous robots capable of performing the same tasks humans do in the near future. This prospect seems limited only by the power and complexity of current computational devices, which is improving fast. However, there are several significant obstacles on this path.

General intelligence involves situational reasoning, taking perspectives, choosing goals, and an ability to deal with ambiguous information. We observe that all of these characteristics are connected to the ability of identifying and exploiting new affordances—opportunities (or impediments) on the path of an agent to achieve its goals. A general example of an affordance is the use of an object in the hands of an agent. We show that it is impossible to predefine a list of such uses. Therefore, they cannot be treated algorithmically.

This means that “AI agents” and organisms differ in their ability to leverage new affordances. Only organisms can do this. This implies that true AGI is not achievable in the current algorithmic frame of AI research. It also has important consequences for the theory of evolution. We argue that organismic agency is strictly required for truly open-ended evolution through radical emergence.

We discuss the diverse ramifications of this argument, not only in AI research and evolution, but also for the philosophy of science.

Editorial connection

This source sharpens the embodiment argument by focusing on affordances: the open-ended ways an organism can discover opportunities for action in a changing environment. The relevance to AGI is not that biology automatically proves silicon intelligence impossible, but that a general-intelligence theory must explain how an artificial system would acquire an equivalent capacity for open-ended, context-sensitive discovery.

The inferential bridge

The biological evidence in this section establishes something directly about biological cognition: humans and other animals develop through evolved, embodied, and environmentally coupled mechanisms. It does not, by itself, prove that every possible general intelligence must be biological.

The stronger argument is comparative. If evolved organisms begin with rich priors, specialized subsystems, bodily feedback, social learning, and context-sensitive mechanisms for relevance, then an artificial system that lacks those mechanisms must either reproduce their functional equivalents or explain why they are unnecessary. In that form, embodiment and evolution become constraints on a theory of AGI rather than an appeal to biology by analogy.

The same point applies to innateness. Spelke and the language material challenge a blank-slate picture of human learning, but an engineer could respond by building explicit priors into an artificial architecture. The real question is therefore whether current LLM pretraining already supplies the right kinds of priors and representations, or whether a qualitatively richer architecture is required.

Agency and Cognitive Architecture

Distinguish operational software agency from evolved psychological agency, then ask what architecture a more general system would need.

The word “agent” can conceal an important ambiguity. Software can be called agentic because it acts toward goals, while evolutionary accounts use agency to describe a much richer organization of perception, valuation, learning, planning, and self-maintaining behavior.

Free agents: how evolution gave us free will : Kevin Mitchell, Trinity College Dublin and The Evolution of Agency

Abstract:

An evolutionary case for the existence of free will. Scientists are learning more and more about how brain activity controls behavior and how neural circuits weigh alternatives and initiate actions. As we probe ever deeper into the mechanics of decision making, many conclude that agency-or free will-is an illusion. In Free Agents, leading neuroscientist Kevin Mitchell presents a wealth of evidence to the contrary, arguing that we are not mere machines responding to physical forces but agents acting with purpose. Traversing billions of years of evolution, Mitchell tells the remarkable story of how living beings capable of choice emerged from lifeless matter.

He explains how the emergence of nervous systems provided a means to learn about the world, granting sentient animals the capacity to model, predict, and simulate. Mitchell reveals how these faculties reached their peak in humans with our abilities to imagine and to introspect, to reason in the moment, and to shape our possible futures through the exercise of our individual agency. Mitchell's argument has important implications-for how we understand decision making, for how our individual agency can be enhanced or infringed, for how we think about collective agency in the face of global crises, and for how we consider the limitations and future of artificial intelligence. An astonishing journey of discovery, Free Agents offers a new framework for understanding how, across a billion years of Earth history, life evolved the power to choose and why this matters.

My analysis

One of the first concepts that appears in AGI discussions is “autonomous agency.” My contention is that we should be careful about what that phrase means. We can call a software system an agent in an operational sense because it selects actions toward an objective, but that is not necessarily the same phenomenon as the agency that emerges in a biological organism over evolutionary and developmental time.

Kevin Mitchell is not writing primarily to undermine AGI research. I include his work because it challenges the assumption that agency is simply another computational function that can be named at a high level and then treated as solved. If agency depends on the organization of self-maintaining living systems, that difference has to be addressed rather than hidden by using the same word for both.

The Evolution of Agency: Behavioral Organization from Lizards to Humans

Abstract:

A leading developmental psychologist proposes an evolutionary pathway to human psychological agency. Nature cannot build organisms biologically prepared for every contingency they might possibly encounter. Instead, Nature builds some organisms to function as feedback control systems that pursue goals, make informed behavioral decisions about how best to pursue those goals in the current situation, and then monitor behavioral execution for effectiveness. Nature builds psychological agents. In a bold new theoretical proposal, Michael Tomasello advances a typology of the main forms of psychological agency that emerged on the evolutionary pathway to human beings.

Tomasello outlines four main types of psychological agency and describes them in evolutionary order of emergence. First was the goal-directed agency of ancient vertebrates, then came the intentional agency of ancient mammals, followed by the rational agency of ancient great apes, ending finally in the socially normative agency of ancient humans. Each new form of psychological organization represented increased complexity in the planning, decision-making, and executive control of behavior. Each also led to new types of experience of the environment and, in some cases, of the organism's own psychological functioning, leading ultimately to humans' experience of an objective and normative world that governs all of their thoughts and actions. Together, these proposals constitute a new theoretical framework that both broadens and deepens current approaches in evolutionary psychology.

My analysis

Tomasello's account is similar to Mitchell's in treating agency as something that develops through evolutionary pressures. Tomasello emphasizes social cooperation and coordination: selection favors feedback systems that can interact intelligently with their environment and eventually pursue goals whose organization goes beyond immediate survival. Mitchell likewise describes self-preserving feedback systems as part of the evolutionary pathway toward richer agency.

Neither book is a direct attack on AGI research, but together they sharpen the question raised by the microbiome and cephalopod examples: can intelligence and agency emerge in a vacuum? Much human learning is social. If the capacities we associate with general intelligence depend heavily on interaction with other agents, then it is not obvious that those capacities can simply appear through isolated statistical learning over a fixed corpus.

“What's wrong with LLMs and what we should be building instead” - Tom Dietterich

Summary:

In this discussion Thomas G. Dietterich discusses the fundamental problems with LLMs and potential solutions. He essentially recognizes the need for Cognitive Architecture and Knowledge bases.

My analysis

I think artificial intelligence will require a cognitive architecture richer than pure connectionism. LLMs are extraordinarily large statistical models that extract patterns from vast data sets and use those patterns to predict likely tokens. My concern is that neither cognition nor consciousness can simply be identified with that statistical objective.

Memory is one example. Looking at human intelligence, what we call memory is not equivalent to database storage or to the ephemeral retention of tokens in a context window. Human memory is reconstructed, organized by experience, and embedded in a broader cognitive system. The work of Elizabeth Loftus, C. R. Gallistel, other memory researchers, and models such as Atkinson–Shiffrin makes it difficult for me to regard an LLM context window as genuine memory in the same cognitive sense.

That is only one missing function. The brain also contains specialized subsystems whose behavior depends on their coupling to other subsystems. Artificial deep neural networks abstract away many of these ontological differences between kinds of nodes and connections; they do not directly model functional integration in the biological sense. This is why Dietterich's emphasis on cognitive architecture and knowledge bases is important to me: the question is not simply how to scale one statistical mechanism, but how to organize heterogeneous cognitive functions into a coherent system.

Operational agency versus biological agency

A proponent of AI agents can reasonably object that “agency” in software engineering is an operational term and need not reproduce the evolutionary history described by Mitchell or Tomasello. I think that objection should be granted rather than argued past.

The substantive question is whether operational agency is enough for general intelligence. If the richer capacities associated with biological agency—self-maintenance, endogenous goal formation, adaptive valuation, social learning, perspective taking, and persistent world modeling—are necessary for general intelligence, then calling a tool-using language model an “agent” does not establish that those capacities are present. It changes the vocabulary, not the architecture.

Limits, Risk, and What Would Change My Mind

The final section separates the strongest impossibility claims from the more modest thesis of the article. Some sources argue that computational AGI is impossible in principle; my own conclusion does not need to go that far to reject the inference from current LLM progress to imminent AGI.

Why Machines will Never Rule the World: Artificial Intelligence without Fear

Abstract:

The book’s core argument is that an artificial intelligence that could equal or exceed human intelligence—sometimes called artificial general intelligence (AGI)—is for mathematical reasons impossible. It offers two specific reasons for this claim: Human intelligence is a capability of a complex dynamic system—the human brain and central nervous system. Systems of this sort cannot be modelled mathematically in a way that allows them to operate inside a computer. In supporting their claim, the authors, Jobst Landgrebe and Barry Smith, marshal evidence from mathematics, physics, computer science, philosophy, linguistics, and biology, setting up their book around three central questions: What are the essential marks of human intelligence? What is it that researchers try to do when they attempt to achieve "artificial intelligence" (AI)?

And why, after more than 50 years, are our most common interactions with AI, for example with our bank’s computers, still so unsatisfactory?

My analysis

Barry Smith and Jobst Landgrebe make the strongest impossibility claim among the sources collected here: they argue that the mathematics needed to model the relevant complexity of human intelligence cannot be captured in the way AGI research requires. This is an entire book, so I cannot summarize the case responsibly in a few sentences.

What matters for my purposes is that their argument ranges across stochastic complex-systems modeling, language, and social interaction rather than treating intelligence as a single benchmark capability. Smith's discussions of dispositions and capabilities are also useful because those concepts force us to ask what an autonomous system can actually do across changing circumstances, rather than what outputs it can produce in a fixed evaluation.

Three claims that should remain separate

  1. LLMs are insufficient for operational AGI. This is the main thesis developed by the reasoning, relevance, learning, agency, and architecture sections.
  2. Computation is insufficient for genuine semantic understanding. This is a stronger philosophical claim associated with the Chinese Room, symbol grounding, and related critiques.
  3. Computation is insufficient for consciousness or mind. This is stronger still. It is relevant to Strong AI, but it is not necessary to establish the narrower claim that scaling LLMs does not automatically yield AGI.

What would change my mind?

A useful thesis should identify evidence that would force it to update. I would take the case for an LLM-centered path to AGI much more seriously if systems demonstrated several of the following properties robustly and outside narrow benchmark conditions:

  • Rapid out-of-distribution learning without massive retraining.
  • Persistent, revisable world models that remain coherent across long time horizons.
  • Systematic causal, analogical, abductive, and defeasible reasoning in genuinely novel contexts.
  • Grounded acquisition of new concepts through interaction rather than text correlation alone.
  • Open-ended discovery of relevant affordances in changing environments.
  • Autonomous formation, evaluation, and revision of goals rather than merely executing externally supplied objectives.
  • Memory that functions as an integrated part of cognition rather than as a larger context window or external database.
  • Reliable composition of specialized cognitive functions into a coherent architecture.

Conclusion

I think this is enough for now, although I expect to add to the post—especially when I encounter work that directly addresses these objections. My main thesis is not that GPT-class systems lack value. There are many impressive and useful applications for them, including applications I use myself. The narrower point is that the jump from those capabilities to “we are close to AGI” remains, in my view, poorly justified.

The sources collected here point to several different gaps: statistical generalization versus structured reasoning; syntax versus semantics; learned correlations versus grounded concepts; externally specified objectives versus richer agency; context windows versus human memory; and disembodied pretraining versus cognition that develops through bodies, environments, and social interaction. No single one of these observations has to carry the entire argument. Their cumulative force is that “scale the language model” is not yet a theory of general intelligence.

I could be wrong. There may be a general learning algorithm or architectural development that resolves many of these problems at once, and there may be arguments I have not encountered that substantially weaken the objections above. That is why the update criteria matter: if systems begin to demonstrate those capacities robustly, the thesis should change with the evidence. Until then, I think the burden remains on claims that current LLM progress is evidence of an imminent transition to AGI.
