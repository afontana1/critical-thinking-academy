Michael Levin's Platonic Space, Mathematical Explanation, and Physicalism

Michael Levin is a professor of biology at Tufts University and a prolific, unusually interdisciplinary thinker. I have followed his work for a while because his biological research overlaps with artificial life, bioengineering, computer science, behavioral science, and cognitive science. That kind of cross-domain thinking is something I think modern academia is seriously lacking.

This is especially relevant to me because I think economics—my own discipline—ought to incorporate more of the concepts and methods his lab takes seriously: collectives, swarming behavior, emergence, scaling laws, evolutionary development, and process-oriented thinking. I think he is modeling complex systems in roughly the right way. A mere mortal like myself could not possibly make the intellectual strides he has made, and I see him as a thinker I would like to emulate. But admiration does not remove the need for criticism; I cannot help critically analyzing an argument when I think a key inference is doing more work than it first appears to be doing.

I recently watched Levin's “Platonic Space: brief argument and research agenda”. As I understand it, he argues that physicalism is false and that there is some kind of Platonic Space in which “intelligence,” along with other abstractions and patterns, can exist in an uninstantiated form while nevertheless constraining or determining the physical forms and patterns we observe. He further suggests that such patterns can instantiate even in simple systems, such as finite-state machines—not to the degree that they instantiate in complex organisms, but to a nonzero degree.

It is difficult to pin down exactly what he means, however, because he explicitly says that his view is not “Platonic in the philosophical sense of timeless forms,” while the brief presentation does not fully specify what replaces that classical conception. There is also some ambiguity about the direction and kind of dependence involved. Levin says that these patterns “supervene on the world in a one-directional way,” but also says things such as “biology is very good at exploiting these patterns.” If biology is genuinely using or exploiting something, that sounds more interactive than a strictly one-directional relation. Even the phrase “biology is good at” assigns capacities to an entity called “biology” in a way that I find conceptually unfamiliar.

My central contention: even on a charitable reconstruction, the argument does not establish the falsity of physicalism unless a controversial realist interpretation of mathematics—and a bridge from mathematical explanation to nonphysical ontology—is independently defended. On a strong reading, this risks begging the question; on a more cautious reading, the crucial premises are at least dialectically loaded against the physicalist.

How I will proceed. First I reproduce Levin's argument as presented. Then I compress its core logic, reconstruct it as an argument from explanation, make its hidden assumptions explicit, and stress-test those assumptions with critical questions. Finally, I explain why I think the anti-physicalist conclusion remains under-supported while also separating that philosophical criticism from the potential scientific value of Levin's research program.

Part I Framing the Dispute The first task is interpretive: separate Levin's own formulation from my reconstruction of what the anti-physicalist argument needs to be.

Levin's argument as presented

The following is the argument as presented in the source material. I am leaving its numbering and wording intact here because this section functions as the object of analysis rather than as my reconstruction.

1. There are specific facts of mathematics, let’s call them “patterns” (a.k.a., forms). Examples: value of e, Feigenbaum’s constant, facts of number theory and topology, symmetry of SU(2), amplituhedron, etc.

2. There are many specifics which are surprising, and forced on you, once you choose some basic assumptions (very few – just logic, apparently) → you “get more out than you put in”. Start with set theory and get the specific value of e.

3. For some such patterns P:

  • there are aspects of physics and biology that are explained by recourse to the specifics of P. If you ask “why” long enough, you end up in the Mathematics department.
  • in contrast, there is no aspect of the physical world (physical events/laws), and no amount of history (biological selection), that explain/set the properties of P
  • if P’s facts were different, biology and physics would be different.
  • it doesn’t work in the reverse: there is nothing you can change in the physical world to make P be different.
    • therefore, causality flows from these forms to the physical world (not in the temporal sense).
    • therefore, these facts play important instructive roles. They cannot be ignored if you want to understand and tame evolution, bioengineering, etc.

4. Therefore:

  • physicalism is a non-viable theory: there are facts that are simply not “in” the physical world in any useful sense of “physics”. Pythagoras knew this already. Let’s call the space of possible properties of P’s “the Platonic Space”.

5. Optional hypotheses: (optimistic metaphysical claim)

  • P is drawn from a distribution that’s not a random collection but a structured space
  • therefore, we have a research program: map the space, understand relationship between interface and which P it channels.

5. Skeptical position: we cannot assume that low-agency models of math encompass all the residents of this Space. Some may be better described by behavioral science tools.

  • therefore, some of the patterns that ingress into physics and biology may be “kinds of minds”.
  • therefore, Dualism is viable. We already knew it was true in physics and biology; this suggests it’s also relevant in cognitive science.

7. Skeptical position: we cannot assume that biological materials, evolutionary search, etc. have any monopoly on hosting those patterns.

  • therefore, perhaps algorithms/robots should be searched for surprising ingressions that are not just complexity or unpredictability, but well-understood cognitive competencies.

The larger research agenda is interesting, but the claim I want to isolate first is narrower: what, exactly, is the argument from mathematical patterns to the conclusion that physicalism is non-viable?

The core anti-physicalist inference

Stripping down the argument against physicalism to its core logical sequence, I take it to be:

  1. Certain mathematical “patterns” P explain aspects of physics and biology.
  2. Physics, biology, and history do not explain or determine those mathematical patterns.
  3. The explanatory direction is one-way: from mathematics → world, not world → mathematics.
  4. If there are facts that explain the physical world but are not themselves explicable in physical terms, then physicalism is false.
  5. Therefore, physicalism is false.
Why this compression matters: the later claims about minds, robots, evolutionary search, and the structure of Platonic Space depend on a prior step. Before asking what else inhabits the space, we need to know whether the explanatory asymmetry of mathematics establishes a nonphysical ontology in the first place.
Part II Reconstructing the Argument The same argument can be represented at several levels. Each representation below has a different job: the generic scheme identifies the inferential form; the P1–P4 reconstruction makes the commitments explicit.

An argument from mathematical explanation

At least at a high level, this resembles an abductive or argument-from-best-explanation structure of the sort described by Walton. That is useful because an argumentation scheme gives us not only a conclusion but a standard way to ask where the inference is vulnerable.

Generic explanatory scheme
  • Premise 1 (Data): Phenomenon F is observed.
  • Premise 2 (Explanatory link): Hypothesis H would explain F.
  • Premise 3 (Comparative): No other available hypothesis explains F as well as H.
  • Conclusion: Therefore, H is provisionally acceptable.

Before reconstructing Levin's argument in this form, my own starting point should be explicit. I am sympathetic to a broadly formalist view of mathematics. Levin's presentation acknowledges that we begin with assumptions—presumably axioms—and that surprising consequences can then be forced on us. But the fact that consequences follow once a formal system is specified does not by itself show that those consequences exist as mind- and world-independent entities. If we identify formal systems that map extraordinarily well onto empirical reality, that is important; I am simply not convinced that the facts derived within those systems therefore exist “outside physics.” They may instead be features of the representational apparatus—model artifacts, to use a deliberately computational analogy—rather than metaphysical objects with causal powers.

Charitable reconstruction

With that caveat in view, I would reconstruct Levin's “Argument from Mathematical Explanation against Physicalism” as follows:

  • P1 (Data): There are features of physics and biology—call them the “targets”—that can be explained by appealing to specific mathematical facts or patterns P. In Levin's formulation, “there are aspects of physics and biology that are explained by recourse to the specifics of P.”
  • P2 (Explanatory link): If mathematical facts or patterns P are mind- and world-independent—that is, if they belong to a nonphysical “Platonic Space”—then their explanatory role in physics and biology is accounted for.
  • P3 (Asymmetry / negative claim): No purely physical facts, and no historical or biological story, explain or determine the mathematical facts or patterns P. In the original formulation, “there is no aspect of the physical world … and no amount of history … that explain/set the properties of P.”
  • P4 (Bridge principle): If something explains the physical but is not itself explainable in physical terms, then physicalism is false—or at least non-viable.
  • C (Conclusion): Therefore, physicalism is false or non-viable.

This reconstruction is deliberately charitable: it tries to make the anti-physicalist inference as explicit as possible rather than dismissing it at the level of rhetoric. Once the argument is stated this way, however, the key philosophical work migrates into P2 and P4. P2 asks us to move from mathematical explanation to a realist ontology; P4 asks us to move from that ontology to the falsity of physicalism.

Part III What the Inference Must Assume The argument becomes easier to evaluate once its background commitments are separated from the premises Levin states explicitly.

Hidden assumptions and missing bridge premises

There are several assumptions underlying the argument that are not trivial. Some concern ontology, some concern what counts as scientific explanation, and others concern how strong a doctrine “physicalism” is supposed to be. I originally separated these into several lists; putting them together makes the inferential dependencies easier to see.

AssumptionCommitmentWhy it matters
A1 — Robust mathematical realismMathematical facts or patterns P exist independently of human activity and of the physical world.Without this, mathematical necessity does not automatically supply a nonphysical ontology.
A2 — Explanation licenses ontological commitmentIf an entity or fact genuinely explains something, we are entitled to count it in our ontology. This is an indispensability-style move.It connects explanatory usefulness to what we should say exists.
A3 — Explanatory priority ↔ non-reducibilityIf X explains Y and Y cannot in principle explain X, then X is not reducible to Y.It turns explanatory asymmetry into an ontological hierarchy. I find this less controversial than A1 or A2, although much depends on what kind of explanation and reduction are at issue.
A4 — Mathematics itself explainsExplanations in physics and biology really do appeal to particular mathematical facts, not merely to our current best formalism or model.If the mathematics is only representational apparatus, the ontological inference weakens substantially.
A5 — Mathematical truth is not hostage to pragmatic axiom choiceThe choice of axioms is not merely pragmatic or human, or at least the relevant truths that follow are not hostage to that choice.Levin's slide acknowledges assumptions or axioms, but then treats what “falls out” as fixed independently of us.
A6 — Strong requirement on physicalismPhysicalism requires all explanatorily relevant facts to be ultimately physical or derivable from the physical.This defines the target strongly enough for mathematical abstracta to threaten it.
A7 — Mathematical abstracta refute physicalismMathematical facts not derivable from the physical count as a counterexample to physicalism.This is stronger than many physicalists would grant; a physicalist may allow abstracta while denying that they are concrete causal constituents of the world.
M1 — Explanatory-to-ontological bridgeIf an explanans E for a physical phenomenon is itself not physically explainable, then E is not physical.It converts a limitation on physical explanation into a claim about what kind of thing E is.
M2 — Anti-physicalist bridgeIf there exists at least one nonphysical but explanatorily indispensable fact, physicalism is false.This is the final bridge from mathematical ontology to the global conclusion.

These assumptions are not minor details. A1 and A2 are plainly contested, and A6–A7 and M1–M2 determine whether the conclusion is even aimed at a version of physicalism that a physicalist needs to accept. Perhaps Levin defends these commitments elsewhere; in the brief formulation I am analyzing, however, they are not independently established.

Four pressure points

Rather than treating every objection as independent, I think the dispute can be organized around four recurring pressure points. The 17 critical questions below are best read as more granular tests within these four families.

Pressure pointCentral issue
1. Representation vs. explanationDoes a mathematical structure genuinely explain a physical or biological phenomenon, or does it represent an explanation whose operative content remains physical?
2. Explanation vs. ontologyEven if mathematics genuinely explains, why should explanatory indispensability commit us to independently existing mathematical entities or patterns?
3. Asymmetry and directionWhat exactly is one-directional: causation, explanation, logical dependence, structural constraint, or some other relation?
4. The bridge to physicalismEven if mathematical facts are nonphysical, which version of physicalism is thereby falsified, and why?

Causation, explanation, and constraint are not the same relation

One point deserves special treatment because it may resolve some of the apparent ambiguity in Levin's language. The claim that mathematical patterns are prior to physical systems can mean several different things, and the philosophical consequences differ depending on which relation is intended.

RelationQuestion to ask
CausalDoes P actually produce, alter, or otherwise enter into the causal history of physical events?
ExplanatoryDoes citing P answer why a physical phenomenon occurs, even if P is not itself a cause?
Structural / constrainingDoes P characterize the space of possible organizations or behaviors without causing any particular physical event?

If “Platonic Space” primarily names a space of structural constraints or possibilities, then saying that biology “exploits” those constraints need not imply that abstract patterns push matter around. But that interpretation also weakens the direct path from explanatory priority to a dualistic ontology. Clarifying which relation Levin intends is therefore not semantic housekeeping; it changes the argument.

Critical questions

The following questions preserve the full diagnostic inventory. My own criticism concentrates most heavily on CQ1, CQ3, CQ7, and CQ9, but the other questions help show exactly where alternative versions of the argument could be strengthened or weakened.

CQ group 1: About the data/explanations (P1)

  • CQ1: Are there really scientific explanations that essentially appeal to specific mathematical facts (and not just to the use of a mathematical model)?
  • CQ2: Could the same physical/biological phenomena be explained equally well with an empirically equivalent but mathematically different formalism?
  • CQ3: Are we mistaking representation for explanation – i.e. is math merely the language in which the explanation is stated?

CQ group 2: About the asymmetry (P3)

  • CQ4: Is it actually true that the physical/historical cannot explain the choice, adoption, or success of mathematical formalisms? (E.g. cognitive science of mathematics, evolutionary stories about why we have certain concepts.)
  • CQ5: Even if the physical cannot explain the truth of mathematical statements, can it explain why those truths are the ones that get to play an explanatory role in science?
  • CQ6: Is the direction “math → world” being construed as causal when it is only conceptual or structural?

CQ group 3: About the platonist step (P2, A1)

  • CQ7: Why should we interpret mathematical success as evidence for the existence of a nonphysical realm, rather than as evidence for the usefulness of certain formalisms?
  • CQ8: If mathematics is a human-selected formal system, and the world happens to be amenable to being modeled that way, does that require extra ontology?
  • CQ9: Can a nominalist or formalist account for all the same explanatory practices without positing nonphysical objects?

CQ group 4: About the bridge to anti-physicalism (P4, M1, M2)

  • CQ10: Does admitting noncausal, abstract, or representational entities automatically refute physicalism, or can a physicalist treat them as “ontologically lightweight”?
  • CQ11: Does physicalism require that every explanatorily relevant fact be physical, or only that concrete facts are physical?
  • CQ12: Is the inference from “indispensable abstracta” to “physicalism is non-viable” valid, or is that just importing a strong form of naturalism?

CQ group 5: About competing explanations (Walton’s comparative step)

  • CQ13: Are there rival hypotheses – e.g. mathematical fictionalism, structuralism, instrumentalism about models – that explain the same data (math’s role in science) as well as platonism?
  • CQ14: If there are rival explanations of equal or better fit, why should we prefer the platonist one?
  • CQ15: Is the appeal to a “Platonic Space” really explanatory, or just restating the phenomenon (“math seems prior”) in ontological language?

CQ group 6: About overreach

  • CQ16: Even if mathematics is nonphysical, why does that show physicalism about mind/biology/physics is false, rather than only physicalism about abstracta?
  • CQ17: Is the conclusion too strong for the premises – i.e. should the conclusion be “physicalism is incomplete” rather than “non-viable”?
Part IV Verdict and Limitations My objection is not that mathematical realism has been disproved. It is that realism cannot simply be inserted into the argument as though it were a neutral premise and then used to announce that physicalism is obviously false.

My main objection

Essentially, I am pressing the argument with CQ1, CQ3, CQ7, and CQ9. I do not think the scientific use of mathematics by itself commits us to mathematical objects or patterns as a separate realm. I see modeling first as a practice: we construct or select formal systems, derive consequences within them, and retain the systems that capture empirical regularities especially well. On that picture, the explanatory success of mathematics may tell us something profound about the structure of the world without yet telling us that mathematical objects constitute an additional nonphysical domain. If that alternative remains viable, P2 loses much of its force and the bridge premises are undercut.

The strongest way to state my original objection was that the argument is fundamentally question-begging. I would now formulate that point more carefully. To beg the question is to rely on a premise that already assumes, in the relevant respect, what the argument is supposed to establish. Here, the suspicious step is the move from “mathematical facts are not explained by anything physical” to “therefore there are explanatory facts outside the physical.” That move looks natural if one already treats mathematics as mind- and world-independent reality. A formalist, nominalist, or some kinds of structuralist can deny that interpretation without denying that mathematical reasoning is objective, constrained, or scientifically indispensable.

So the key premise is not philosophically neutral. At minimum, the argument is dialectically loaded: it asks a physicalist to grant a robust ontology of mathematical facts before using that ontology as evidence that physicalism is false. On a sufficiently strong reading, that risks genuine question-begging. Put colloquially, “physicalism is false because Platonism is true” can collapse into something like “you are wrong because I am right” unless an independent case for the Platonist step is supplied.

The burden of argument lies at the bridge. The important question is not whether mathematical reasoning produces necessary consequences once assumptions are fixed; of course it does. The question is why the success, necessity, or explanatory indispensability of those consequences entitles us to reify them as inhabitants of a nonphysical realm—and then why the existence of that realm falsifies the relevant form of physicalism.

A formalist response can therefore remain straightforward: humans specify axioms and rules; those assumptions generate consequences; some of those formal consequences model the world extraordinarily well. That fact may itself demand explanation, but it does not automatically follow that the consequences are additional nonphysical objects with independent causal powers. On this view, the explanatoriness of mathematics could remain a model-level, structural, or representational feature rather than an ontological one.

What would change the significance of the objection?

This entire response could turn out to be aimed at the wrong target. As noted earlier, Levin explicitly says that his “Platonic Space” is not simply Platonic in the classical sense, and the brief video does not provide a complete metaphysical account of what the term means. If his intended view differs substantially from the realist picture I am reconstructing, then the argument may need to be reformulated before my criticism can properly engage it. To be fair, he has written about Platonic Space on his website, which I have not yet read, and I am in the process of watching the material from his Symposium on the Platonic Space. That may change my interpretation.

There is also a second and importantly different question: whether this framework is scientifically fruitful. If Platonic Space guides experimental design and hypothesis generation in a way that produces novel discoveries in bioengineering, artificial life, or cognition, then the framework will have demonstrated genuine methodological value. Experiments ultimately arbitrate empirical claims in a way that philosophical argument alone cannot. Sometimes progress requires radical perturbations to the assumptions organizing a research program, and Levin may be attempting exactly that.

But scientific fertility and metaphysical truth should still be distinguished. A framework could be extraordinarily successful as a research heuristic while leaving its ontological interpretation open. If the programme begins generating novel, otherwise unexpected empirical successes, that would make the philosophical questions more urgent rather than automatically settling them. In Kuhn's vocabulary, a sufficiently deep conceptual reorganization might eventually look like a change of paradigm; in Lakatos's vocabulary, the more precise question would be whether the research programme is progressive—whether its theoretical innovations generate novel empirical success rather than merely accommodating what is already known.

That is ultimately why I find Levin's proposal worth taking seriously even though I am unconvinced by the abbreviated anti-physicalist argument. The experimental programme may succeed, the metaphysics may succeed, both may succeed, or one may succeed without the other. Keeping those possibilities separate makes it easier to criticize the inference without dismissing the research agenda.

Part V Conceptual Glossary The dispute turns on several positions that are easy to blur together. This glossary keeps the terminology explicit and makes the target of the critique easier to track.

Philosophy of mathematics

At its core, philosophy of mathematics is trying to answer a small cluster of stubborn questions: What are mathematical objects—numbers, sets, functions—supposed to be? Do they exist, and if so, where and how? How can we have knowledge of them if they are not in space and time? Why is mathematics so effective in science? And what makes a mathematical statement true—does it report a fact about something, or is it true because of rules we made up? Different views in philosophy of mathematics are basically different ways of answering those questions while trying not to create new problems worse than the originals.

Physicalism

Physicalism is the family of views according to which concrete reality is, in some relevant sense, wholly physical. The exact strength of the thesis matters enormously here. One version says that every concrete entity and event is physical or physically realized. A stronger version might require every genuine fact, property, or explanatorily indispensable posit to be physical or reducible to the physical. Levin's argument needs something closer to the stronger interpretation if the mere existence of mathematical abstracta is supposed to make physicalism non-viable. A physicalist can otherwise try to say that abstract mathematical discourse is harmless so long as no nonphysical concrete cause is added to the world.

Platonism

Platonism says that mathematical objects are real, mind-independent, and abstract. The number 2, the set of real numbers, group structures—these are not inventions; they are discoveries. They do not live in space or time, they do not depend on human practices, and mathematical statements are true because they describe how these abstract things actually are. On this view, “2 + 2 = 4” is true in roughly the way “water is H₂O” is true: it lines up with the structure of reality, just a nonphysical slice of it. The major attraction of Platonism is that it gives a direct account of the objectivity of mathematics and its uncanny usefulness: mathematics works because the world instantiates or mirrors real structures. The major cost is epistemic and metaphysical: how do we, as spatiotemporal creatures, acquire knowledge of non-spatiotemporal objects, and how do such objects fit into a broadly naturalistic picture?

Formalism

Formalism tries to lower the metaphysical temperature. On a formalist picture, mathematics is not primarily about a realm of abstract objects; it is about manipulating symbols according to rules inside formal systems. We lay down axioms and inference rules, and the theorems are what can be derived by following those rules. “Truth” within a system can then be understood in terms of the formal relations generated by that system rather than correspondence with a Platonic reality. This makes mathematical practice look more like playing a very elaborate, highly constrained game. But that does not mean it is arbitrary: not all formal systems are equally useful, and some map onto empirical reality with remarkable elegance. The contrast with Platonism is sharp here: the Platonist says mathematics is true because the relevant structure exists; the formalist emphasizes what follows from specified axioms and rules. A standard pressure on formalism is that mathematicians often talk and act as though they are discovering something, not merely tracing the consequences of stipulations, and we still need an account of why certain formal systems are so perfectly suited to physics.

Nominalism

Nominalism's starting impulse is “no abstract objects, please.” A nominalist wants to make sense of mathematics without committing to numbers, sets, or other entities that are not concrete. There are different strategies, but the shared idea is that mathematical talk can be paraphrased, regimented, fictionalized, or otherwise understood so that it does not require a Platonic ontology. Sometimes this means treating mathematical statements as shorthand for claims about concrete things; sometimes it means treating them as part of a useful fiction that carries no literal ontological commitment. In contrast with Platonism, nominalism denies that mathematical statements must literally be about a mind-independent abstract realm. Its appeal is that it fits comfortably with a physicalist or naturalist worldview. Its challenge is to recover the apparent objectivity, necessity, and scientific power of mathematics without the clean Platonist explanation that these features reflect an independently existing abstract reality.

Structuralism

Structuralism occupies an interesting middle space. It says that mathematics is about structures, not primarily about individual abstract objects taken in isolation. What matters are positions in a structure and the relations among them, not some mysterious intrinsic nature of “the number 2.” Systems with the same relevant structure are mathematically equivalent for the purposes at issue. Arithmetic, for example, can be understood as concerning the structure common to appropriate sequences rather than one privileged sequence living in Plato's heaven. Compared with Platonism, structuralism can thin out what we need to believe in: we need not posit robust objects with their own intrinsic identities; we focus on structures and relations. Compared with formalism, many structuralists insist that mathematics is not merely symbol-pushing because the symbols capture objective structural possibilities. The pressure on structuralism is then to explain what it is for a structure to exist and whether that answer pushes the view back toward a gentler form of Platonism.

Ontological commitment

Ontological commitment is a commitment to what a theory says exists, or to what must exist for the theory's claims to be literally true or explanatorily adequate. In this essay the concept matters because there is a difference between using mathematics in an explanation and being committed to mathematical entities as constituents of reality. A2 is the principle that tries to bridge that gap.

Mathematical explanation

A mathematical explanation is an explanation in which a mathematical fact, structure, theorem, or constraint appears to do more than merely provide notation. The central dispute is whether the mathematics itself explains the phenomenon or whether it represents, compresses, or reveals a physical explanation. That distinction is exactly what CQ1 and CQ3 are designed to probe.

Supervenience

Supervenience is a dependence relation often expressed by saying that there cannot be a difference at one level without some difference at another. It does not by itself imply ordinary temporal causation. Because Levin uses language of one-directional supervenience while also speaking of biology “exploiting” patterns, it is important to ask whether the intended relation is supervenience, structural constraint, explanation, causal influence, or some combination of these.

Dualism

Dualism, in the broad sense relevant here, is the view that reality cannot be exhaustively captured by one physical category alone. Different dualisms divide reality in different ways, so simply showing that abstract mathematical facts are nonphysical would not by itself establish every familiar kind of mind–body dualism. This is one reason CQ16 asks how far the conclusion is supposed to reach.

One way to keep the landscape straight

Platonism (robust abstract reality), formalism (rule-governed formal systems), nominalism (no abstracta), and structuralism (relations and structures over intrinsically individuated objects) are four different responses to the same puzzle: how can mathematics be necessary, objective, and scientifically indispensable without needlessly bloating our ontology? Each trades off explanatory directness, fidelity to mathematical practice, and metaphysical economy in a different way.
