Objective and Subjective: A Useless Distinction?

Why are people so obsessed with the objective/subjective distinction? The terms appear in debates about morality, religion, science, politics, and social construction, and calling an opponent “subjective” often functions as if it settles the argument. Atheism is said to lead to radical relativism; religious morality is described as “objective”; social construction is sometimes treated as if it implied that there are no facts. These debates often turn less on a carefully specified philosophical distinction than on a shifting and ambiguous use of the words objective and subjective.

I do not think social construction implies the radical claim that there are no facts, nor do I think that acknowledging the role of subjects in inquiry commits us to “anything goes.” The deeper problem is that we routinely use a single binary distinction to answer several different questions: What exists independently of us? What can be true or false? How do we know something? How do we measure it? What standards are we using? How certain are we? Which parts of a practice depend on social convention? Those are not the same questions.

Core claim: the ordinary objective/subjective binary is often useless because it collapses several independent distinctions into one word. The useful task is not to ask whether something is simply “objective” or “subjective,” but to ask in what sense it depends on subjects, standards, institutions, evidence, or information.

Part I One Word, Several Distinctions Before testing the distinction with examples, it helps to separate ontology, epistemology, social dependence, standards, uncertainty, and method.

John Searle is useful here because he distinguishes two different senses of the objective/subjective contrast. Ontology concerns what exists and the mode in which it exists. Epistemology concerns what we know, how we justify claims, and whether a proposition can be assessed independently of a particular person’s preference or attitude.

Experiences are ontologically subjective in the sense that they exist only as experienced by a subject. Physical structures such as mountains and molecules are ontologically objective in the sense that they do not depend on being experienced by a particular subject in order to exist. But epistemic objectivity is a different matter. It can be an epistemically objective fact that a person is in pain even though the pain itself has an ontologically subjective mode of existence.

My pains have a subjective mode of existence in that they only exist as experienced by me, the subject. But mountains and molecules have an objective mode of existence because they exist whether or not they are experienced by any subject. It can be an epistemically objective matter of fact that I have a pain even though the mode of existence of the pain is ontologically subjective. (Searle, Philosophy in a New Century, Cambridge University Press, 2008, p. 167)

This matters because people regularly equivocate between these senses. “Objective” may mean mind-independent in one sentence, evidence-based in the next, unbiased in another, universally true in another, and finally morally binding. “Subjective” may mean personal, emotional, socially conditioned, uncertain, relative to a standard, biased, value-dependent, or simply wrong. Those meanings overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

Question More precise distinction Example
Does it exist independently of human minds? Ontological objectivity / subjectivity A mountain versus an experienced pain
Can the claim be evaluated independently of my preference? Epistemic objectivity / subjectivity An accident rate versus “I dislike this driver”
Does the fact depend on conventions or institutions? Natural / socially constructed or institutional A molecule versus money or a traffic law
Does the judgment depend on a stated goal or criterion? Absolute / relative to a standard A “good driver” relative to safety criteria
How sure are we that a proposition is true? Certainty, confidence, probability, and uncertainty Belief about an unknown event given available evidence
How was the judgment produced? Procedural or methodological objectivity Public methods, explicit assumptions, replication, and error correction
The important point is that these dimensions can cross. A socially constructed fact can be epistemically objective. A proposition can be objectively true while I remain uncertain about it. A standard can be chosen by people while empirical claims about what satisfies that standard are nevertheless open to evidence.

With those distinctions in view, the examples that initially look paradoxical become easier to analyze.

Part II Intelligence: Reality, Concepts, and Measurement A latent concept illustrates the difference between what exists, how we define it, how we measure it, and how we interpret evidence.

Consider “intelligence.” Is it objective or subjective? If objective means “true or existent independently of human opinion,” then one immediately has to ask what kind of thing intelligence is. A cat is relatively straightforward: there is an animal here that I can see, touch, smell, and generally perceive. Intelligence is different. I do not perceive intelligence directly in the same way. I infer it from behavior and performance.

Psychologists therefore use theories, measurements, factor analysis, structural equation models, and related methods to infer the presence and level of a latent construct called “intelligence.” That process requires several distinct steps. Researchers conceptualize the construct, decide how to operationalize it, choose what counts as evidence, collect observations, analyze the data, and interpret the results. Every one of those activities is performed by subjects.

But the fact that subjects design the measurements does not tell us that the measurements are arbitrary or that the underlying proposition has no truth value. The useful question is instead: what constrains the inquiry? Are the definitions clear? Do the indicators behave as predicted? Do different measurements converge? Are the results reproducible? Does the construct predict anything outside the test itself? These are methodological questions, not merely labels of “objective” or “subjective.”

The same problem appears when we ask whether someone is intelligent. I have to observe the person, decide what evidence is relevant, and interpret that evidence. Suppose a group of scientists agrees that a person is intelligent. Does consensus itself create objectivity? Probably not. Consensus can be wrong. What we care about is whether the conceptual framework and the evidence correspond well enough to the phenomena we are trying to understand.

Ordinary language complicates matters further. Someone can score highly on an intelligence test yet be unable to replace a spare tire. Does that make the person unintelligent? Or does it show that “intelligence” is a category defined for particular explanatory purposes and that practical competence is a different construct? Once again, asking simply whether intelligence is “objective” does almost no analytical work.

“Scientifically objective” is not the same thing as “untouched by human concepts.” Scientific objectivity is better understood as a disciplined way of making human judgments answerable to evidence, explicit methods, criticism, and correction.
Part III Social Facts: The Blinker Example The meaning of a turn signal is socially constructed, but that does not make every claim about it subjective or arbitrary.

Now consider an obvious social fact. When I change lanes, the law generally requires me to use a turn signal. I activate a blinking light, other drivers interpret it as a signal that I intend to turn or change lanes, and they respond accordingly. Several different kinds of fact are already layered together.

1. The physical event

The light is blinking. That is a straightforward physical observation. Whatever social meaning the signal has, the light itself either is or is not flashing.

2. The institutional rule

It may also be a fact that the law requires me to signal. But this fact is contingent on a legal system, place, and era. We could live in a society without this rule; other societies could adopt a different rule. Its dependence on human institutions makes it socially constructed, but not therefore unreal. Given the relevant jurisdiction and law, the proposition can be true or false.

3. The conventional meaning

A blinking right-hand signal ordinarily means that the driver intends to move right. That meaning is not contained in the photons themselves. It exists because people share a convention. Yet once the convention exists, it is not merely my private opinion that the signal has that meaning. If I die tomorrow, the convention will probably continue.

4. The inference

Suppose a driver leaves the blinker on for several miles without turning. The signal now supports a new inference: perhaps the driver forgot to cancel it. That conclusion may be true or false, and it depends on evidence and background knowledge. Calling the inference “subjective” simply because I, a subject, made it would make nearly all scientific inference subjective in the same unhelpful sense.

The better question is whether the inference is reasonable given the evidence. I know that turn signals are normally used temporarily, that drivers sometimes forget to cancel them, and that a signal left on for several miles often has that explanation. My conclusion is fallible, but fallibility is not the same thing as arbitrariness.

Socially constructed does not mean arbitrary, fictional, or false. A socially created rule can generate facts once the convention exists. Money, laws, contracts, traffic signals, offices, rankings, and many other institutions work this way.
Part IV Standards, Values, and the “Good Driver” Normative standards may be chosen by people while empirical claims about performance relative to those standards remain evidence-sensitive.

The example becomes more interesting when I move from describing the driver to evaluating the driver. If I say, “That person left the blinker on and is not turning, therefore they are careless,” or “They are not a good driver,” the statement sounds more obviously like a judgment. But what exactly makes it subjective?

Insurance companies routinely classify drivers by measurable indicators: collisions, traffic violations, claims, mileage, and other variables associated with risk. The categories and thresholds were designed by people, and they could have been designed differently. If human beings rode horses rather than cars, our present rating standards would not exist. In that sense the system is socially constructed.

Yet it does not follow that every judgment inside that system is arbitrary. We can compare current behavior with historical outcomes and estimate whether some behaviors are associated with higher collision risk. A standard may be selected by subjects while the evidence about performance relative to that standard remains empirically constrained.

Suppose the normative starting point is that we should preserve human life and avoid unnecessary risk. That starting point is a value judgment; it is not a mountain or molecule. But once we stipulate the goal, we can investigate propositions about which actions are conducive to it. A person driving 100 miles per hour through a school zone creates a substantially different risk environment from a person driving cautiously under the same conditions. Saying that the first driver is unsafe is not merely equivalent to saying, “I personally dislike this style of driving.”

Chosen standard + empirical evidence ≠ arbitrary conclusion

This is also how money and traffic rules work. Money is a socially agreed-upon convention that persists across generations; very few people reject an offer of $100 on the ground that money is “only a social construction.” Likewise, people generally do not drive through a stop sign at 100 miles per hour because the stop sign is socially constructed. Once the context and rules of engagement exist, there are facts about whether an action conforms to them and whether it serves or undermines the goals those rules were designed to advance.

The remaining philosophical question is harder: are there axiomatic starting points that all rational beings could accept, allowing normative reasoning to proceed from a shared framework? I do not pretend that question is easy. My point is narrower: value-dependence at the level of goals does not automatically infect every downstream empirical claim with radical subjectivity.

Part V Relative to Something Is Not Radical Relativism Context-dependence, standard-dependence, and social dependence do not entail that there are no facts.

If a fact is socially dependent, is it also relative? In one sense, often yes. A legal obligation is relative to a jurisdiction. A judgment that someone is a “good driver” is relative to standards of good driving. A claim about intelligence may be relative to a particular construct definition and measurement framework. But being relative to something is not the same thing as radical relativism about truth.

We should distinguish the proposition from our conditions for applying it. “This driver satisfies safety criterion X” can have a determinate truth value once criterion X and the evidence are specified. “This law exists in jurisdiction Y at time Z” can also be true or false even though the law is historically contingent and socially constructed. Context determines what proposition we are evaluating; it does not necessarily erase the difference between truth and falsity.

The same applies to experience. It may be epistemically objective that a cat is sitting on the couch with me, while my experience of the cat—whether it comforts, annoys, or delights me—is ontologically and phenomenologically subjective. Even the category “cat” has a history and a human classificatory role, but that does not mean there is no animal there.

Radical relativism is a much stronger claim than any of these observations require. There are philosophers and theorists who defend various forms of relativism, and the term itself covers multiple positions. The target here is the crude inference that if standards, concepts, or social practices are historically contingent, then facts disappear. That inference simply does not follow.

Part VI Uncertainty Is Not Subjectivism: Bayesian Epistemology Our degree of belief can depend on information without making truth itself depend on the believer.

Bayesian probability provides another useful case. Bayesian methods are now used throughout science, although statisticians disagree about how probabilities and priors should be interpreted. Some critics have objected that Bayesian approaches are “too subjective,” while subjective Bayesians answer that explicitly modeling prior information can make assumptions visible and test how conclusions change under different assumptions. Frequentist procedures also involve choices—models, estimators, significance levels, sampling plans, and diagnostics—even though those choices are usually described in a different vocabulary.

I do not need to settle the Bayesian-versus-frequentist debate here. The useful philosophical point is that a degree of belief in a proposition can depend on the information available to an agent without implying that the proposition itself has a different truth value for each person. A Bayesian can say: the proposition is either true or false, but my rational confidence in it changes as evidence changes.

This is where words such as confidence, reliability, probability, and uncertainty become more useful than the crude objective/subjective binary. I do not “know objectively” whether I will be in a collision tomorrow. I can nevertheless estimate risk using information about my driving, road conditions, vehicle condition, population data, and other relevant evidence. My information is incomplete, so my confidence is limited. That does not mean the eventual event lacks a fact of the matter.

A frequentist confidence interval should not be read simply as a personal degree of belief in a parameter after observing the data; its standard interpretation concerns the long-run performance of the interval-generating procedure. A Bayesian credible interval, by contrast, is explicitly tied to a probability distribution over the parameter conditional on the model, prior, and observed data. The technical details differ, but both traditions remind us that statistical conclusions depend on assumptions, models, and information.

Consider two probability questions. With a fair six-sided die, the probability of any specified face is one-sixth under the usual model. Given that the observed face is odd, the conditional probability that it is a three is one-third. Now consider the probability that a burglar is in my house given that the door is unlocked even though I normally remember to lock it. The mathematical form is still conditional reasoning—what should I believe about an unknown given some information?—but the second case contains much more uncertainty about the data-generating process and the relevant background rates.

That difference helps explain why the first probability feels “objective” and the second feels “subjective.” In the die example, the model is highly specified and the outcome space is explicit. In the burglar example, my model is incomplete, my priors may be poorly calibrated, and relevant information may be missing. The distinction we really care about is not whether a subject is involved; it is how well specified the problem is and how strongly the available evidence constrains reasonable belief.

“Today’s posterior is tomorrow’s prior” captures the dynamic element. My assessment should change when I receive new information. That is not the claim that truth itself changes with my mood. It is a claim about rational belief under uncertainty. A subjectivist Bayesian can therefore reject radical relativism: propositions still have truth values, while our degrees of belief depend on evidence, prior assumptions, and background knowledge.

Return to the driver

Return to the driver who left the blinker on. Given my ordinary knowledge of driving, I infer that the person probably forgot to turn it off. That is a reasonable explanation, but it is defeasible. Suppose I learn that people in this particular group sometimes leave blinkers on deliberately as a joke, or that I know the driver personally and know he is a jokester. New information changes the relative plausibility of competing explanations.

My inference depends on my knowledge and on the reliability of that knowledge. I can be wrong while still reasoning responsibly. Dismissing the conclusion with “that is just subjective” tells us almost nothing. The right questions are: What evidence supports the inference? What alternative explanations are available? How reliable are the assumptions? How strongly should the evidence update our confidence?

This also clarifies why people reach for the word “objective” as a form of timeless authority. Boolean logic gives us examples that feel permanent and unconditional: FALSE OR FALSE is FALSE; TRUE AND FALSE is FALSE. We often want empirical claims about the physical and social world to possess that same transparent certainty. But empirical inquiry is messier. Information is incomplete, measurement is fallible, and conclusions remain open to revision.

None of this implies that “anything goes.” Subjective Bayesian approaches, for example, are constrained by coherence requirements; Dutch book arguments are one classical way of making that point. Decision theory extends the issue further. Can there be objectively good decisions, or only decisions that are optimal relative to some goal or utility function? In many cases “best” is shorthand for “best with respect to a specified objective.” Again, the specification of the objective and the empirical question of how well an option achieves it are different matters.

Part VII Objectivity as Method, Impartiality, and Error Correction If inquiry is always performed by subjects, objectivity may be better understood as disciplined procedures for discovering and correcting bias.

Impartiality and neutrality are closely related to this discussion, especially under law. Can a mediator stand unbiased between two parties with diverging interests? Does random jury selection guarantee an impartial assessment of the facts? What someone deems relevant information is itself affected by background assumptions, incentives, experience, and the design of the selection process.

Search neutrality provides a modern version of the same problem. How should a search engine prioritize results without privileging some sources over others while also minimizing the user’s search costs? Any ranking system must use criteria. Even alphabetical ordering privileges some positions over others. Algorithmic bias matters because people frequently reaffirm what they take to be objective truths through search results and newsfeeds that are themselves generated by partial selection processes.

News, for example, is not a transparent window onto reality. Editorial selection, ranking, repetition, incentives, and sensationalism influence what information reaches us. Recognizing that does not require the conclusion that there is no truth. It requires us to examine the mechanisms through which partiality arises.

Adam Smith’s “impartial spectator” and Rawls’s “veil of ignorance” both invoke attempts to reason from a standpoint less dominated by immediate self-interest. Whether anyone can literally stand outside every social and economic condition is doubtful. But the failure to achieve a perfect “view from nowhere” does not make the project pointless. The more practical question is whether we can identify recurrent sources of error and design institutions and habits that correct for them.

Objectivity need not mean the absence of subjects. It can mean an error-correcting relationship among subjects, evidence, methods, criticism, and revision that makes individual bias easier to detect and harder to preserve.

I think of the problem as something like local optimization and error correction. Given known mechanisms by which bias and partiality arise, try to correct them. Because we do not know every way in which we can fool ourselves, maintain feedback loops capable of filtering false positives and exposing mistakes while keeping inquiry open-ended. Finding truth requires being honest about our self-affirming tendencies. It should be acceptable to be wrong and uncertain; otherwise correction becomes impossible.

Application: relativism is not ideologically unique

Radical relativism is not uniquely a “politically left” phenomenon. It can appear across the political spectrum whenever a group treats truth as belonging to a civilization, identity, party, or standpoint rather than as something that competing claims must answer to. Aleksandr Dugin, for example, has explicitly used postmodern and civilizational language to argue for a distinctively Russian truth and to reject universal Western claims. Whatever one thinks of that political example, the broader point is that relativistic rhetoric is not owned by one political faction.

The same intellectual standards should therefore be applied to propositions regardless of political orientation, including one’s own. Appeals to “my truth,” crude standpoint claims in matters of fact, or civilizational truth claims can all function as ways to insulate propositions from criticism. The problem is not left versus right; it is whether claims remain open to evidence, coherence, consistency, and correction.

Part VIII When “Objective” and “Subjective” Become Rhetorical Weapons The words often communicate status, authority, or dismissal rather than adding analytical precision.

This returns to the problem that motivated the essay. “Subjective” often carries the connotation that anything goes: emotional, incoherent, illogical, relativistic, and unconcerned with truth. “Objective” carries the opposite prestige: rational, scientific, neutral, authoritative, and final. That is why calling an opponent subjective can feel like winning the argument before any evidence has been discussed.

But those pragmatic connotations should not be confused with description. A person can make a subjective preference with excellent self-knowledge; an epistemically objective claim can be badly measured; a socially constructed institution can generate rigid facts; an uncertain inference can be rational; and a person loudly claiming “objectivity” can be biased, incoherent, or simply wrong.

In that sense, the objective/subjective labels often operate as argumentative tactics. There is a difference between what is literally stated and what is meant. When these words are used carelessly, they carry a Gricean implicature: “my position is disciplined and truth-directed; yours is personal and arbitrary.” The words function pragmatically in a speech act rather than advancing the discussion toward the underlying issue.

Instead of asking…Ask…
Is this objective or subjective?Is the entity mind-independent, socially dependent, or experience-dependent?
Is this just an opinion?What evidence, assumptions, methods, and standards constrain the claim?
Is it relative?Relative to which context, criterion, model, jurisdiction, or goal?
Is the conclusion certain?How confident should we be, and what evidence would change that confidence?
Is the institution socially constructed?What facts follow once the relevant convention or institution exists?
Is the researcher biased?What procedures make bias detectable and correctable?
Conclusion Ask Better Questions The distinction is useful only after we specify what kind of objectivity or subjectivity we mean.

I began by asking why people are so attached to the objective/subjective distinction. My conclusion is not that examples of objectivity and subjectivity do not exist. It is quite subjective for someone simply to assert a preference without offering a reason, and it is highly objective in an epistemic sense to inspect a valid mathematical proof of the binomial theorem. The terms have clear uses in clear domains.

The problem begins when we throw them around outside those domains as if they were self-explanatory. Even an “objective measurement” is embedded in a process of instrument design, data collection, categorization, model choice, and interpretation. Binary tests generate false positives and false negatives. Observers make decisions about what to measure and how to classify results. None of this destroys knowledge; it simply means that knowledge is harder and more procedural than the fantasy of a perfectly detached observer suggests.

The same lesson applies to social construction, normative standards, relativism, probability, and political disagreement. The world does not become unreal because human beings conceptualize it. A proposition does not become false because our confidence in it is imperfect. A social fact does not cease to be a fact because people created the institution that sustains it. A judgment relative to a stated goal does not become arbitrary merely because the goal was selected.

The useful replacement for “Is it objective or subjective?” is a sequence of more precise questions: What exists? What proposition is being asserted? What does it depend on? Which standard is being used? What evidence bears on it? How reliable is that evidence? How uncertain are we? And what procedures would expose us if we were wrong?

That vocabulary gives us more information than the all-purpose binary. It also makes it harder to use “objective” as a badge of authority or “subjective” as a dismissal. If the goal is to move a discussion toward truth, precision about the kind of claim being made is more useful than taking the rhetorical high ground.

Appendix Epistemic Vocabulary: Possible, Plausible, Probable, and Proven These terms often communicate what people are actually trying to express when they reach too quickly for “objective” or “subjective.”
TermWorking meaning
Possible Something that could occur: it is not internally inconsistent or logically impossible. In some contexts, speakers use “possible” more narrowly for what is also consistent with the relevant physical laws.
Plausible A possibility supported by enough background knowledge, explanatory fit, or common experience to merit serious consideration, even when the evidence is insufficient for a strong probability estimate. Plausibility is often associated with inference to the best explanation and is defeasible because it depends heavily on assumptions. Both confirming and disconfirming evidence should be considered.
Probable A proposition or outcome assigned substantial likelihood on the basis of a statistical model, frequency data, calibrated judgment, or other structured evidence. Something can be probable even when it feels implausible relative to a person’s prior worldview; the evidence and the intuition need not agree.
Proof / very strong support The word proof should be used carefully. In mathematics and formal logic, proof can be deductive given explicit axioms and inference rules. In empirical inquiry, we more often have degrees of evidential support rather than proof in the same deductive sense. In legal and ordinary contexts, “burden of proof” refers to a specified standard of justification rather than literal elimination of every conceivable doubt.
