Part I

Foundations

Define the problem, clarify the concept of expertise, and state the scope before introducing evaluation tools.

1. The Expert-Identification Problem

The central difficulty is epistemic asymmetry: non-experts must decide whom to trust without already possessing the expertise that would make the decision easy.

Path: problem statement → decision context → operational task

Recently I've been reading C. Thi Nguyen, along with social epistemology more broadly, and have come across what's referred to as the expert identification problem. Here is the main idea: there is a difficulty of determining who genuinely qualifies as an expert in a given domain, especially when non-experts lack the knowledge or skills to make such a determination. This creates an epistemic asymmetry, there is an inherent epistemic gap between experts and non-experts. If non-experts had the expertise needed to evaluate an expert, they would, in a sense, be experts themselves. This creates a circular problem: non-experts are asked to identify who the experts are, but they lack the knowledge to do so effectively.

I see this problem as threefold. We must first define critical aspects of "expertise", second we must identify the entities to which that label could possibly refer, and third we must establish criteria/heuristics/rules of thumb that allow us to minimize our error rate. This is essentially a search and decision problem with the added complexity that there might not be a unique solution and we must satisfice. You could also think of it as a constraint satisfaction problem; we must identify a set of candidates that satisfy multiple criteria. 

I think so far I am getting ahead of myself. We should first loosely define the problem statement.  Suppose we have a situation in which an expert is required for some inferential task including, but not limited to: diagnosis, prediction, evaluation, recommendation, explanation, or guidance. This is normally in service of achieving some pragmatic goal in relation to some decision. The non-expert needs to establish whether some candidate expert satisfies minimal criteria to some degree of certainty. The candidate could be presented to the non-expert through some intermediary or the candidate could have been discovered through their own line of inquiry. There comes a point where the non-expert must:

  1. Have a working conceptualization of "expertise" that has been critically analyzed
  2. Decide whether the predicate "is an expert" is applicable to this candidate
  3. Consult a collection of criteria that can help establish whether the candidate is an "expert"
This process should take into consideration the problem of Evolving Knowledge: Expertise is not static; knowledge in most fields evolves over time. An individual who was an expert a decade ago may not be up-to-date on the latest developments. In other words, expert identification is a continual process that must be reapplied after some interval of time. 
Decision thresholds should be stake-sensitive. The amount of evidence required before treating someone as an expert should rise when the cost of a false positive is high. Conversely, rejecting a genuine expert can itself be costly. This makes expert identification a classification problem with asymmetric error costs, not merely a search for a perfectly accurate label.

With the decision problem stated, the next question is what kind of category “expert” is supposed to be.

2. What Do We Mean by Expertise?

The draft’s central conceptual move is to treat expertise as graded, context-sensitive, and defeasible rather than as a sharp binary property.

Path: graded category → ranking → trust → independence of sources

The first and third dimensions of the problem seem related; after all, shouldn't the methodology just be establishing whether the definition has been satisfied for any given example? I do not think we should think of "expertise" as a binary category. Rather, we should think of it as a graded concept that denotes a family resemblance consistent with the notion of categorization illustrated with prototype theory. This is why I qualified the first dimension; I do not wish to conclusively define "expertise" with sharp boundaries. Rather, I think there are various attributes of a candidate "expert" that should be identified with a sufficient degree of certainty, using some methodology, before tentatively applying the label. This is a defeasible process subject to revision in light of new information. This also implies the existence of a relative ranking system; for any two candidates A and B, A might jointly satisfy more properties with a higher degree of certainty than B. There will be a minimal threshold that must be crossed before adding some new entity C to the set of potential candidates. 

In the above scenario, why say that one candidate is preferred to another rather than simply classifying the weaker candidate as a non-expert? Because the notion of “expertise” is intimately connected to trust. By rank-ordering potential experts, we can weight our credence toward conclusions held by more preferable candidates. This gives us a way to reason when experts disagree. Suppose we have some proposition P. Expert A and Expert B disagree about whether P is more probably true or false. If we have ranked A above B, we would initially give A’s conclusion more weight. Now suppose a third candidate, C, enters with standing equivalent to B and also thinks P is more probably false than true. If the combined evidential force of B and C exceeds A’s, we can update our beliefs.

However, the number of experts cannot be treated as a simple vote count. B and C may rely on the same evidence, method, institution, or upstream source, in which case their agreement is not fully independent. A useful ranking system therefore has to distinguish source credibility, number of sources, and independence of sources. Consensus matters most when it reflects multiple substantially independent routes to the same conclusion.

Two credences should remain distinct. The question “Is E an appropriate expert for this task?” is not identical to the question “How likely is proposition P given E’s testimony and the rest of the evidence?” A genuine expert can be wrong about a particular claim, and a non-expert can occasionally be right. Expert identification therefore changes the weight we give testimony; it does not replace evaluation of the claim itself.

This conceptual model is intentionally modest. It helps with localized decisions, but it does not capture every social mechanism that creates or distributes epistemic authority.

3. Scope, Constraints, and What This Essay Does Not Solve

I think some tools from argumentation theory can help. These obviously don't completely solve the problem. For example, they have nothing to say about the social processes, norms, and power structures that shape who is seen as credible and how epistemic authority is maintained or challenged. This is key to the problem of expert identification because the credibility of experts is not just based on their knowledge, but also on how society perceives and legitimizes them. It also does not address collective knowledge. In many cases, we do not just rely on individual experts but on institutions or groups (such as scientific organizations or universities) to provide reliable knowledge. Social epistemology analyzes how these institutions function and how they can either promote or hinder collective knowledge production. This helps address how non-experts can assess not just individual experts but the credibility of institutions as a whole. I am not going to tackle this issue here. I am also not going to touch on the role of  media and communication. In contemporary society, the media play a huge role in shaping public understanding of who is an expert. Social epistemology analyzes the ways in which media representations of expertise and authority can influence public perception, often leading to the amplification of certain voices over others, regardless of their true level of expertise. These are massive endeavors. All I wish to do is relay some concepts from argumentation theory that I think could help localized instances of expert identification, under tractable conditions.

Within those limits, Argumentation Theory provides a useful set of practical tools for Stage One: deciding whether a candidate deserves the status and weight of an expert source.

Part II

Stage One — Identifying a Candidate Expert

Use authority distinctions, argumentation schemes, legal admissibility criteria, and critical questions to make a provisional expert classification.

The goal is not certainty. It is to decide whether a candidate is sufficiently credible, relevant, and trustworthy for the decision at hand.

Path: epistemic authority → argument from expert opinion → critical questions → admissibility → comparison of frameworks

Authority and Expert Opinion

I’ll begin by distinguishing two senses of authority that are easy to conflate. An expert normally exercises a degree of epistemic authority within a domain of inquiry: their influence comes from being plausibly in a position to know. This differs from institutional or deontic authority, where decision-making power follows from a role, office, or rule. By authority in the epistemic sense, I mean an individual who can influence decision making, resolve disagreement, or reduce ambiguity by reference to a body of knowledge relevant to a particular question. Authorities in this sense have access to relevant knowledge and are distinguishable from non-members of the domain.

In other words, expertise can generate epistemic authority, but authority by itself does not establish expertise. Many authority figures hold decision-making power by virtue of institutional legitimacy rather than superior domain knowledge. The diagram below visualizes this hierarchy of authority and helps distinguish the kind of authority relevant to arguments from expert opinion (Arguments from authority and expert opinion in computational argumentation systems).



We are obviously concerned with the left side of the tree. The right side of the tree concerns authority within the domain of reasoning that involves obligation, duty, forbidden, or what is permissible. This concerns the type of authority capable of explicitly invoking or enabling action based on a set of agreed upon societal regularities such as law. On the other hand, epistemic arguments involve propositions capable of being more or less probable, provable, supportable, demonstrable, or knowable. According to this classification, arguments from expert opinion will inherit the methods for assessing arguments from authority more broadly. 

The above classification is provided by scholars from the field of Argumentation Theory. This is a rich discipline that studies patterns of reasoning. One particular concept, that we will be invoking here to assess expertise, is the argumentation schematic. From Argumentation Schemes: History, Classifications, and Computational Applications, it is defined as:
... stereotypical patterns of inference, combining semantic-ontological relations with types of reasoning and logical axioms and representing the abstract structure of the most common types of natural arguments [Macagno and Walton, 2015]. The argumentation schemes provided in [Walton et al., 2008] describe the patterns of the most typical arguments, without drawing distinctions between material relations (namely relations between concepts expressed by the warrant of an argument), types of reasoning (such as induction, deduction, abduction), and logical rules of inference characterizing the various types of reasoning (such as modus ponens, modus tollens, etc.). For this reason, argumentation schemes fall into distinct patterns of reasoning such as abductive, analogical, or inductive ones, and ones from classification or cause to effect.
In other words, the schemes are like patterns or archetypes, that consistently occur within many domains of inquiry or dialogue. One particular pattern is the "Argument from Authority" or "Argument from Expert Opinion". These occur when someone needs to offload the process of justification onto a third party who presumably has done the work. In premise form, the argument looks like this (Walton, Appeal to Expert Opinion):
  • Conditional Premise: If Source E is an expert in subject domain S containing proposition A, and E asserts that proposition A is true (false) then A is true (false).
  • Major Premise: Source E is an expert in subject domain S containing proposition A.
  • Minor Premise: E asserts that proposition A is true (false).
  • Conclusion: A is true (false).

You might notice that this scheme assumes an expert has already been identified. You would be correct. Once identified, you can form a conclusion about whether some claim relevant to your situation is true or false. The question remains: is this person an expert? This is where the critical questions play a role. Argumentation theorists provide us with a set of critical questions associated with the specific scheme that probe an arguments relative strength. I think these can be used to assess whether a person is an expert or not. You can think of them as criteria that assess whether the major premise is justifiable. These serve as heuristics that allow us to determine if some candidate is an expert. The reason I've set up the problem this way, is due to the fact that determining "expertise" tends to take place under pragmatic considerations related to decision problems under lack of knowledge. We hardly ever sit idly and carve out an exhaustive set of experts from the domain of all possible experts. On the contrary, we are almost always asking this question, in a relevant context within some dialogue. Argumentation theory provides us with a categorization of common dialogues, see below (Meaning Negotiation in Multiple Agent System: an Automated Reasoning Approach):


You can imagine that argument from authority can occur at any stage within these types of dialogues. The crucial point is that "expert identification" occurs within a circumstance constrained by pragmatic considerations where our knowledge is defeasible. We typically hear an argument from authority, and then must determine whether that person is indeed an authority, before accepting the conclusion of the interlocutor. Below are six basic critical questions of arguments from authority:

Walton’s Six Basic Critical Questions

  • Expertise Question: How credible is E as an expert source?
  • Field Question: Is E an expert in the field F that A is in?
  • Opinion Question: What did E assert that implies A?
  • Trustworthiness Question: Is E personally reliable as a source?
  • Consistency Question: Is A consistent with what other experts assert?
  • Backup Evidence Question: Is E’s assertion based on evidence?
The first question concerns the depth of knowledge the expert supposedly has. Is the expert a master or only a beginner, even though he or she is qualified? This factor corresponds to the perceived expertise of the source, cited as one of the two most factors by Gilbert et al. (1998). The second question asks whether the field of the expert matches the field into which the proposition A falls. The third question probes into the exact wording of what the expert said (whether quoted or paraphrased). The fourth question raises doubts whether the expert is personally reliable as a source, for example, if she is biased, or has something to gain by making the statement. This factor corresponds to the trustworthiness of the source cited as the other of the two most important factors cited by Gilbert et al. (1998). The fifth question asks what kind of evidence the expert’s pronouncement is assumed to be based on. If asked what evidence the pronouncement is based on, an expert should be willing to  provide such evidence, and if she does not, the argument based on her expert opinion defaults.

These can be expanded upon in great detail. We can also add more questions to the list. Notice that, given pragmatic considerations, we might not be able to pose questions indefinitely. I suppose we could remain agnostic, but at some point we must reach a threshold, and decide whether "expertise" applies to the subject under consideration. 

Credibility, Rule 702, and the Daubert Analogy

Let's expound upon these one by one. The first question prompts us to assess credibility. But what is credibility? It's hardly a binary category. Once again, Argumentation Theory provides us with tools for conceptualizing the notion of credibility. In An Argumentation Interface for Expert Opinion Evidence, Walton references common legal practices to form a baseline, by referencing  Rule 702, Testimony by Expert Witnesses within the Federal Rules of Evidence:

A witness who is qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education may testify in the form of an opinion or otherwise if the proponent demonstrates to the court that it is more likely than not that:

(a) the expert’s scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge will help the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue;
(b) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data;
(c) the testimony is the product of reliable principles and methods; and
(d) the expert's opinion reflects a reliable application of the principles and methods to the facts of the case.

This is known as the Daubert standard, based on the ruling within that particular case referenced in the paper. More specifically, the standards are:

  1. Testability: “whether it [the evidence, theory or technique] can be (and has been) tested”
  2. Error Rate: “the known or potential rate of error”
  3. Peer Review: “whether the theory or technique has been subjected to peer review and publication”
  4. General Acceptance: the “explicit identification of a relevant scientific community and an express
  5. determination of a particular degree of acceptance within that community”. (Daubert at 594)
  6. Control Standards: whether its operation has been subjected to appropriate standards of control 

The notes also consider the following criteria:

  1. Whether experts are “proposing to testify about matters growing naturally and directly out of research they have conducted independent to the litigation, or whether they have developed their opinions expressly for the purposes of testifying” [Daubert at 1317].
  2. Whether the expert has unjustifiably extrapolated from an accepted premise to an unfounded conclusion [with reference to Joiner at 146].
  3. Whether the expert has adequately accounted for obvious alternative explanations [with reference to Claar v Burlington13].
  4. Whether the expert “is being as careful as he would be in his regular professional work outside his paid litigation consulting” [Sheehan v Daily Racing Form Inc. 14 at 942].
  5. Whether the field of expertise claimed by the expert is known to reach reliable results for the type of opinion the expert would give [with reference to Kumho at 1175]. (FRE, Rule 702, notes; see also Owen 2002, 361–2)
  6. “the technique’s relationship to other techniques established to be reliable”
  7. “the non-litigation-related uses to which the method has been put”
  8. “whether he [the expert] has sufficiently connected his testimony with the facts of the case.” (Ibid.)

The Daubert factors are not intended to be universal, exhaustive, or individually sufficient or necessarily decisive. Rather, they are to be thought of as general guidelines, that are to be used where appropriate, along with other relevant and useful criteria. Collectively, the criteria contribute to the assessment of the reliability and relevance of testimony offered as expert knowledge. They help to

determine the admissibility of the testimony in a court and analogously, whether an individual ought to "admit" an expert's testimony into their own personal decision making. Their application is also generally left to the discretion of the individual. 

Application of these principles is not that straightforward. Walton notes the problem with the application of these criteria within the paper. This is relevant to our understanding of "expert identification", since the criteria used by "lay people" must be actionable or useful, given pragmatic constraints. Later he mentions that there is a possibility the legal criteria might not even be the criteria used to determine admissibility, but rather they are used during the cross-examination period at the trial itself. Whether this is true, this is important, because it highlights the fact that expert identification is a defeasible process taking place within a dialogue, something Walton will expand upon with his general dialectical model for arguments involving expert opinion:

In short, the standard of reliability is not free of difficulties as a method for assessing expertise in real cases. Fact-finders are normally not experts in the scientific fields in which testimony is now so often offered as evidence in court. Then how are fact-finders to understand and assess expertise in the courtroom? An answer to this question will be given and defended in the following sections using an argumentation model of transmission of expert opinion evidence from a scientific inquiry to a different procedural setting, that of a legal trial.
I think his main point is to show that the critical questioning approach can subsume the Rule 702, because some of the critical questions are relatively similar to the legal criteria, and they both occur within a context of back-and-forth deliberation. Both critical questions and admissibility criteria resemble each other in terms of content and function. Walton asks us to consider how source credibility within the courtroom might work. The main idea being that competence and motives of the candidate might be insufficient for assessing source credibility. All of these additional factors and questions must be considered:
In answering this “trust” question, fact-finders have to consider more questions from internal and external aspects. The internal question concerns scientific validity and reliability of evidence itself. Scientific validity, which depends on scientific principles and methods, aims at answering the question: does the principle support what it purports to show? Reliability aims at answering the question: does application of the principle produce consistent results?

For internal validity of scientific research, we can assess the credibility of an expert when evaluating their claims, using the internal credibility criteria provided by Rule 702 and the critical questions (seen below) provided by Walton. For external credibility however, it is a bit more difficult. According to Walton, "Schum (2001, 103) identifies three factors that need to be taken into account in assessing the credibility of a witness: veracity, objectivity, and observational sensitivity", but the problem is that expert bias can still arise due to personal and financial interest. There tend to be strong benefits when affiliated with powerful interest groups, such that it might be impossible to disentangle these motivations from the credibility of their claims. Affiliation with organizations like these tends to enhance their expertise in the eyes of the public as well, making it even more challenging to differentiate between the institutional bias that might alter expert opinions and unfiltered opinions. The External Credibility branch is generally not addressed by Rule 702. There are no rules that explicitly account for this form of credibility. I don't think Walton's critical questions directly address this issue either. As I mentioned above, I don't want to address this problem here since it's way too broad to tackle. 

One of Walton's main conclusions is that admissibility factors are best seen as having the status of critical questions related to the argumentation scheme for expert opinion. The admissibility stage is seen as a sub-stage within the larger dialogue. I take this stage to be roughly equivalent to the problem of expert identification within social epistemology. Deciding whether someone is an expert is a presumptive or plausibilistic task, not something we can be certain of. The decision takes place within a dialogue, in changing circumstances, subject to new information characterized by defeasibility. The main idea is that, when the conditions of the dialogue are satisfied (when the critical questions are sufficiently answered), we plausibly admit candidate expert testimony.

Below is the full set of critical questions for assessing admissibility:

Expanded critical questions

Walton’s fuller checklist makes the six basic questions operational. I have retained the detailed questions below, but grouped them under explicit headings so the reader can see the hierarchy at a glance.

1. Expertise Question: How credible is E as an expert source?

  • 1.1 What is E’s name, job or official capacity, location, and employer?
  • 1.2 What degrees, professional qualifications or certification by licensing agencies does E hold?
  • 1.3 Can testimony of peer experts in the same field be given to support E’s competence?
  • 1.4 What is E’s record of experience, or other indications of practiced skill in S?
  • 1.5 What is E’s record 

2. Field Question: Is E an expert in the field that A is in?

  • 2.1 Is the field of expertise cited in the appeal a genuine area of knowledge, or area of technical skill that supports a claim to knowledge?
  • 2.2 If E is an expert in a field closely related to the field cited in the appeal, how close is the relationship between the expertise in the two fields?
  • 2.3 Is the issue one where expert knowledge in any field is directly relevant to deciding the issue?
  • 2.4 Is the field of expertise cited an area where there are changes in techniques or rapid developments in new knowledge, and if so, is the expert up-to-date in these developments?

3. Opinion Question: What did E assert that implies A?

  • 3.1 Was E quoted in asserting A? Was a reference to the source of the quote given, and can it be verified that E actually said A?
  • 3.2 If E did not say A exactly, then what did E assert, and how was A inferred?
  • 3.3 If the inference to A was based on more than one premise, could one premise have come from E and the other from a different expert? If so, is there evidence of disagreement between what the two experts (separately) asserted?
  • 3.4 Is what E asserted clear? If not, was the process of interpretation of what E said by the respondent who used E’s opinion justified? Are other interpretations plausible? Could important qualifications be left out?

4. Trustworthiness Question: Is E personally reliable as a source?

  • 4.1 Is E biased?
  • 4.2 Is E honest?
  • 4.3 Is E conscientious?

5. Consistency Question: Is A consistent with what other experts assert?

  • 5.1 Does A have general acceptance in S?
  • 5.2 If not, can E explain why not, and give reasons why there is good evidence for A?

6. Backup Evidence Question: Is E’s assertion based on evidence?

  • 6.1 What is the internal evidence the expert used herself to arrive at this opinion as her conclusion?
  • 6.2 If there is external evidence, e.g. physical evidence reported independently of the expert, can the expert deal with this adequately?
  • 6.3 Can it be shown that the opinion given is not one that is scientifically unverifiable?

Walton summarizes by saying:

"It might be said that these critical questions codify some of the background information that is assumed by (or implicit in) the argument from expert opinion. As questions, they function to request the background information on which the success of the argument depends. As objections, they challenge the acceptability of the original argument until the additional information is found to be favorable (to the initial argument). As such, the central feature of critical questions is that, when they are posed, they act as undercutting defeaters (Pollock 1995) for the original argumentation. Until the questions are satisfactorily answered, the original argument is defective and unacceptable. Moreover, there is no burden of proof associated with posing these critical questions. The questions themselves do not have to be supported with reasons, or evidence that their answers are unfavorable to the proponent of the argument from expert opinion. Instead, it is up to the proponent to demonstrate that they can be answered. In effect then, while the argumentative result of a presumptive argument is to shift the burden of proof to an objector, the argumentative result of posing a critical question is to shift the burden of proof back to the proponent of the argument."

Much of "expert identification" involves assessing a potential candidate using these sorts of critical questions. It also involves carefully explicating your own assumptions and reasoning process, along with the reasoning process of the candidate (which we will touch more on later). For example, you might ask yourself "why do I think this person is an expert?" and "is their reasoning aligned with established norms for credibility?" or "Which critical questions apply to this particular candidate? (in the situation where expertise is skill based)". If the candidate expert satisfies these critical questions to a sufficient degree based on contextual requirements, then someone can readily accept this person as an expert, much like how courts need to establish whether to admit expert testimony into the proceedings for a given case. Walton's Argumentation Theory approach prompts us to consider that arguments from expert opinion normally take place within some dialectical context in which a decision must be made. This contrasts with the open ended inquiry characterizing science. In addition, legal contexts are very frequently adversarial. This also contrasts with ideal scientific discourse. I see the critical questions as heuristics that can be used for practical use for individuals assessing experts in day-to-day life, without having to submerge themselves within the open ended inquiry characterized by scientific research. 

The legal criteria and critical questions can be seen as a checklist, where the person asking the questions determines which criteria are applicable and whether the candidate has satisfied them. Walton provides three possible responses when a criterion is applied:

  • (i) the proponent shows that the criterion has been satisfied (direct answer). 
  • (ii) the proponent shows that the criterion is not relevant (indirect answer). 
  • (iii) the proponent shows that the criterion is not significant (indirect answer).

I want to move away briefly, from Walton's approach, and consider alternative approaches. In Challenging Authority with Argumentation: The Pragmatics of Arguments from and to Authority, the author describes a paradox in argumentation theory, something I take to be very similar to the "expert identification problem":

The first of the paradoxes is well-recognized within argumentation theory (Bachman 1995; Goldman 2001; Goodwin 1998; Jackson 2008; Willard 1990). On the one hand, the argument from authority becomes the fallacious argumentum ad verecundiam, as it stifles the independent examination of reasons, thus endangering one of the basic principles of rationality. Indeed, if autonomous, unfettered, and unbiased weighing of reasons is what rationality consists of, then reliance on external authority with its prêt-à-porter judgements undercuts reason. Blind obedience to religious authority of any creed and epoch is a specimen of the grave offence to reason committed by appeals to authority.14 On the other hand, in the complex world we live in, there is only so much we can learn via direct, first-hand perceptual experience and individual reflection. A vast majority of our knowledge is second-hand knowledge, derived from the testimony of others in the position to know—i.e., eyewitnesses, experts, educators, public authorities, etc. (Lackey 2008). We simply cannot reason without arguments from epistemic authority, be them arguments from expert opinion, from testimony, or otherwise from the position to know. Hence the first paradox: authority both appears to be an unreasonable form of argumentation and its very condition of possibility.
Here he provides Goldman's list of critical questions that novices can use to evaluate experts:
  • (A) Arguments presented by the contending experts to support their own views and critique their rivals’ views. (Cf. consistency and backup evidence question)
  • (B) Agreement from additional putative experts on one side or the other of the subject in question. (Cf. consistency and expertise questions)
  • (C) Appraisals by “meta-experts” of the experts’ expertise (including appraisals reflected in formal credentials earned by the experts). (Cf. field and expertise questions)
  • (D) Evidence of the experts’ interests and biases vis-à-vis the question at issue. (Cf. trustworthiness question)
  • (E) Evidence of the experts’ past “track-records” (Cf. backup evidence and expertise question)
I think this is relatively similar to Walton's set of questions, so we don't need to linger. It's just important to note that they can be rephrased while retaining their content.

Another useful approach by David Schum (mentioned earlier), fuses witness and expert testimony:



If part of "expert identification" relies on individual judgment, this implies better or worse modes of reasoning underlying any given assessment. For example, if we find ourselves motivated to classify someone as an expert, solely motivated by political partisanship, we should be aware of this confirmation bias and reassess if feasible. If we are motivated by "winning" rather than exploration of truth, if we cannot possibly conceive of the option that we might be wrong, then identification of genuine expertise will be distorted. This obviously implies we consult our own motivations and reasons for classifying a candidate as an expert. Are we simply using this person as leverage in an argument to defeat an interlocutor? This requires self reflection, something I take to be a necessary condition for the efficient application of the criteria we have been discussing so far. It also implies that we don't just assume that our interlocutor is motivated by pernicious reasons. All too often, people explain away expert opinion by reference to a global conspiracy. This is antithetical to critical thinking and should be avoided at all costs. 

Passing Stage One does not make every subsequent statement by the candidate reliable. It only establishes a defeasible reason to give that source epistemic weight. The next stage tests the particular reasoning.

Part III

Stage Two — Evaluating the Expert’s Claim

Treat expert status as a reason to listen, not as a substitute for examining the evidence, explanation, and inferential structure of a particular claim.

Second-order verification asks whether a specific expert conclusion survives scrutiny even after the source has passed an initial credibility test.

Path: second-order verification → black-box analogy → explanatory virtues → abductive reasoning → detailed checklist

So far, we have identified a set of criteria used to assess a candidate if they are an expert, by means of critical questioning within a dialogue. Once this person is identified, we still need a method of generally assessing their conclusions. This can be thought of as second-order verification. Think about it this way; if a candidate passes all of the relevant criteria in the initial stage, we still need criteria to gauge whether it was a true positive. This is a multistage test. If the second stage is validated, we can form stronger convictions that this candidate is indeed an expert. 

Two credences should remain distinct. The question “Is E an appropriate expert for this task?” is not identical to the question “How likely is proposition P given E’s testimony and the rest of the evidence?” A genuine expert can be wrong about a particular claim, and a non-expert can occasionally be right. Expert identification therefore changes the weight we give testimony; it does not replace evaluation of the claim itself.
Many times, experts are providing an explanation of some occurrence and providing a subsequent recommendation based on their reasoning. More times than not, these explanations can be assessed without having to understand granular details about their particular domain knowledge. Think of an executive decision maker who oversees multiple international business segments. They cannot possibly know the details of every aspect of the business. They cannot possibly have the subject matter expertise of someone within some department they manage who has years of experience. They cannot possibly have a comprehensive model describing the entire enterprise that can answer all conceivable questions. This decision maker must rely on heuristics to assess and synthesize the absurd amount of information presented to them. Many times they are also dealing with people who are highly motivated to favor one conclusion over another. We see this many times within large organizations. Sub-domains might hyper-inflate the necessity of their domain in order to gain more funding. The decision maker must consider all of this. For example, an executive might have no idea how a particular business process works; they must rely on the expertise of a floor manager to communicate this information up the chain of decision makers. They must rely on the synthesis of information presented by someone who is an expert in a sub-domain, in order to steer the entire enterprise in the right direction. In this scenario, if the lower level person cannot synthesize information into a communicable form, the higher level person might conclude that the lower level person does not actually genuinely understand the segment of the business they are overseeing. This form of interaction occurs in many aspects of life. Someone needs to understand how something works, but can only interface with that thing at a very high level where details have been abstracted away. An expert is someone who is supposed to aid our decision making. But if we can't properly interface with them, our confidence in their reliability diminishes. This is a general problem of interfacing with a black box that you rely upon for some practical purpose. The expert is the black box you are relying upon for something. 

I find this situation to be highly analogous to the situation normal people find themselves in when trying to assess expert explanations. But like the executive decision maker, we have tools that can be used to gauge the validity of an expert explanation. I think there are general explanatory criteria that can be used to assess any explanation. If a candidate expert fails to satisfy these criteria, the second order verification test, then we can potentially retract our categorization of them as an expert, or lose confidence in the validity of their conclusions. Here are just a few explanatory criteria I think are relevant:

  • Logical Consistency: The explanation must be logically coherent and free from contradictions.
  • Causal Adequacy: The explanation should properly account for the causal relationships that produce the phenomenon in question.
  • Scope: The explanation should account for a wide range of phenomena, not just a single occurrence or a small set of cases.
  • Simplicity: Sometimes referred to as "parsimony," the explanation should not be more complex than necessary.
  • Unification: The explanation should unify different observations or facts under a single framework or theory.
  • Falsifiability: The explanation should be testable and potentially refutable by empirical evidence.

Some of these criteria do not require the evaluator to possess full subject-matter expertise. They can help non-experts examine the structure of an expert’s reasoning even when they cannot independently reproduce the underlying technical work. This is a form of second-order evaluation: we ask whether the explanation is coherent, responsive to evidence, appropriately qualified, and generated by recognizable reliable mechanisms.

That does not mean every criterion is equally accessible to a novice. Judging causal adequacy, the quality of a technical method, or whether evidence has been correctly interpreted may itself require substantial domain knowledge. The more defensible claim is that non-experts are not completely helpless: they can gain partial insight into an expert’s reasoning process, sometimes by learning the basic mechanisms of the discipline and sometimes by comparing the expert’s account with other credible sources. What I am suggesting therefore requires a generally educated population capable of understanding the basic forms of technical reasoning, but it does not require every decision maker to become a first-order expert.

In many cases, experts are providing an inference to the best explanation (abductive reasoning) about something. The argument generally has this form:

  • Observation: There is a set of observed facts or evidence (E).
  • Hypotheses: There are multiple competing hypotheses (H1, H2, H3, …, Hn) that could explain these facts.
  • Comparison: Each hypothesis is evaluated against a set of criteria (coherence, scope, simplicity, etc.).
  • Selection: The hypothesis that best meets these criteria is inferred to be the most likely explanation.

And can be assessed with the following criteria:

Is the explanation that is proposed genuinely explanatory?

  • Does the hypothesis offer a meaningful explanation for the observed data or evidence?
  • Does it merely describe the evidence, or does it provide an underlying mechanism or cause?

Is the explanation coherent and logically consistent?

  • Does the explanation avoid internal contradictions?
  • Does it align with established facts and other accepted theories?

Does the explanation adequately account for all the relevant evidence?

  • Does the explanation cover all the data or phenomena in question?
  • Are there any significant pieces of evidence that the explanation fails to address?

Is the explanation more plausible than its competitors?

  • Does the explanation make fewer assumptions than alternative explanations?
  • Does it rely on reasonable and likely premises?
  • How does it compare to other plausible explanations in terms of simplicity, scope, and power?

Is the explanation empirically testable or falsifiable?

  • Can the explanation be empirically tested or verified?
  • Are there predictions that could, in principle, disprove the explanation if they turn out to be false?

Is the explanation specific and detailed rather than vague or overly general?

  • Does the explanation provide a clear, specific account of the phenomenon?
  • Are the mechanisms it posits well-defined?

Does the explanation avoid ad hoc hypotheses?

  • Does the explanation introduce new, unsupported assumptions to save itself from being discredited?
  • Are there arbitrary additions to the explanation that make it less likely?

Does the explanation fit well with background knowledge?

  • Is the explanation consistent with what is already known about the world?
  • Does it contradict well-established scientific theories or empirical data?
  • Is the hypothesis consistent with well-established theories, principles, or facts?
  • Does it contradict what is already known, and if so, does it provide a compelling reason for such a contradiction?

Does the explanation have predictive power?

  • Does the explanation allow for accurate predictions of future events or phenomena?
  • Can it explain other related phenomena that were not originally considered?

Does the explanation have explanatory depth and breadth?

  • Does it not only explain the current evidence but also provide insight into other related phenomena?
  • Does it have the potential to unify disparate observations under a common framework?

Is the explanation conservative?

  • Does the explanation fit well with our prior knowledge and does it not require a radical revision of our understanding?

Is the explanation simple and parsimonious?

  • Does the explanation avoid unnecessary complexity and assumptions?
  • Is it the simplest explanation that accounts for all the evidence?

Is the explanation testable or falsifiable?

  • Can the hypothesis be subjected to empirical testing or verification?
  • Is it possible to identify evidence that could, in principle, falsify the hypothesis?

Is the explanation robust across different contexts or evidence?

  • Does the hypothesis hold up well under different conditions or when new evidence is presented?
  • Does it remain plausible when subjected to scrutiny from multiple perspectives?
Walton formulates the critical questions in a relatively similar way:

  • F is a set of facts or observations.
  • E is an explanation (or hypothesis) that, if true, would explain F.
  • No alternative explanation provides a better explanation of F than E.
  • Therefore, E is plausible (or is the best explanation).

    • CQ1: How satisfactory is E as an explanation of F?

    • This question evaluates how well the hypothesis explains the observed facts. It questions whether the hypothesis is actually a good fit for the evidence.

    CQ2: How much better an explanation is E than the alternatives?

    • This checks whether other possible explanations have been considered and whether the current explanation is truly superior.

    CQ3: How thorough is the search for alternative explanations?

    • This question challenges whether a wide enough range of potential explanations has been considered. If alternative hypotheses have been overlooked, the conclusion might be premature.

    CQ4: How likely is it that E is true, as opposed to other possible explanations?

    • This critical question deals with the probability or plausibility of the explanation being true, relative to other explanations.

    CQ5: Could F be explained by a combination of multiple factors?

    • This question examines whether the facts could be explained by more than one cause or factor rather than a single explanation. This would challenge the sufficiency of the current hypothesis.

    When an expert provides an abductive argument, evaluating it critically is crucial to ensure that their conclusion is reliable, well-founded, and not subject to cognitive biases or errors in reasoning. Although experts typically have specialized knowledge and experience, their arguments still need to be assessed using a systematic approach to prevent overreliance on authority. Here are some considerations:

    1. Clarify the Evidence and Observation

    • What is the evidence the expert is explaining?
      Start by identifying the specific observations or data that the expert is trying to explain. This helps to anchor the argument and makes it easier to assess whether the proposed explanation is truly the best.

    • Does the expert provide a clear account of the facts?
      Ensure that the evidence being discussed is presented accurately, clearly, and comprehensively. Are they using all the relevant evidence, or is some important data being overlooked or ignored?

    2. Check the Explanation’s Coherence and Plausibility

    • Is the expert’s explanation coherent and logically sound?
      A good abductive argument should be internally consistent. Does the expert's explanation make sense, or are there contradictions within their reasoning?

    • Does the explanation fit with what we already know?
      The explanation should be consistent with existing theories, knowledge, or data unless there’s strong evidence to challenge the established framework. Is the expert proposing something that goes against well-established facts without sufficient justification?

    3. Evaluate Competing Hypotheses

    • Has the expert considered alternative explanations?
      Even though an expert may be focused on one explanation, good abductive reasoning involves comparing multiple hypotheses. Did the expert consider alternative possibilities, and if so, why were those alternatives rejected? Is the reasoning for rejecting them sound?

    • Is the proposed explanation the best among alternatives?
      Just because an explanation fits the data does not mean it is the best explanation. Is the expert's explanation superior to other hypotheses in terms of simplicity, explanatory scope, or fit with the evidence?

    4. Check for Testability and Predictive Power

    • Can the expert’s explanation be empirically tested?
      One of the hallmarks of a strong explanation is its ability to be tested or falsified. Ask whether the expert's hypothesis allows for empirical verification or whether it makes predictions that can be tested against future evidence.

    • Does the explanation make new predictions?
      A strong abductive explanation will often predict new facts or phenomena. Does the expert’s explanation lead to any new insights or predictions that can be verified?

    5. Assess Simplicity and Parsimony

    • Is the expert’s explanation unnecessarily complex?
      According to Occam’s Razor, a simpler explanation that accounts for all the evidence is preferable to a more complicated one. Is the expert introducing unnecessary complexity, or is their explanation concise and to the point?

    • Are there ad hoc assumptions?
      Check whether the expert is introducing arbitrary or unsupported assumptions to make the explanation fit. An ad hoc adjustment can weaken an abductive argument, suggesting the hypothesis might not be as strong as it first appears.

    6. Consider the Expert’s Bias and Motivations

    • Could the expert be biased in their reasoning?
      Experts, like anyone else, can be subject to confirmation bias or other cognitive biases. Are there any indications that the expert is favoring a particular explanation due to personal biases, institutional pressures, or vested interests?

    • Is there over-reliance on authority?
      Just because an expert endorses a particular explanation doesn't mean it should be accepted without scrutiny. Are they appealing to their authority rather than making a well-reasoned argument supported by evidence?

    7. Review the Scope and Breadth of the Explanation

    • Does the explanation account for all the relevant data?
      Ensure that the expert’s argument covers all the known facts. Are there any key pieces of evidence that are being ignored or downplayed because they do not fit the proposed hypothesis?

    • Does the explanation unify disparate observations?
      A good abductive explanation often has the power to unify various observations or phenomena under a common framework. Does the expert’s explanation provide a broader understanding that could explain other related facts or situations?

    8. Examine the Explanatory Power

    • How well does the explanation clarify the evidence?
      An abductive argument should help clarify the evidence or make it more understandable. Does the expert's explanation provide a deeper understanding of the phenomenon, or does it leave key aspects unexplained?

    • Does the explanation resolve anomalies?
      If there are anomalies or unexpected findings, does the expert’s explanation satisfactorily address them? Strong abductive arguments often explain surprising or puzzling data better than alternatives.

    9. Consider the Domain-Specific Expertise

    • Is the expert working within their field of expertise?
      Ensure the expert’s abductive argument falls within their domain of expertise. Even experts can make errors when reasoning outside their field, and expertise in one area does not necessarily translate to others.

    • Do they use technical knowledge appropriately?
      Evaluate whether the expert is applying their technical knowledge correctly. Are the scientific or technical concepts being used accurately, or are there misunderstandings or misapplications?

    10. Evaluate the Strength of the Evidence

    • Is the evidence reliable and sufficient?
      A strong abductive argument depends on solid, well-supported evidence. Is the expert relying on high-quality data, or is the evidence weak, anecdotal, or incomplete?

    • Are there any unstated assumptions or missing evidence?
      Scrutinize whether the expert is making any unstated assumptions that could undermine the argument. Also, consider whether there is missing evidence that, if known, might alter the conclusion.

    A well-supported explanation may still fail to justify a particular action. Recommendations add another layer: context, values, feasibility, alternatives, and conflicts of interest.

    Part IV

    Stage Three — From Explanation to Recommendation

    Evaluate whether an expert’s proposed action actually follows from the explanation and fits the decision maker’s context.

    Recommendations are practical conclusions. They inherit the weaknesses of the explanation beneath them and add context-sensitive constraints of their own.

    Path: explanation → alternatives → context → feasibility → conflicts → action

    Recommendations are closely related to explanations because a recommendation often requires reasoning that explains why a particular choice or action is preferable in a given context. In essence, a recommendation typically involves an underlying explanation that justifies why the suggested course of action is better than alternatives. The Relationship Between Recommendations and Explanations between them can be understood as follows:

    1. Explanations Justify Recommendations: When someone makes a recommendation, they are often implicitly or explicitly providing reasons or explanations for their suggestion. For instance, a doctor might recommend a specific treatment because of its effectiveness, explaining that it has fewer side effects than alternatives. A strong recommendation is grounded in an explanation that makes clear why it is better suited to a person’s needs, preferences, or situation.
    2. Evaluating Recommendations Involves Examining Explanations: To assess the quality of a recommendation, one must examine the explanation behind it. This includes evaluating the logic, evidence, and assumptions that support the recommendation.
    To evaluate a recommendation effectively, we can focus on several key areas:

    1. Clarity and Logic of the Explanation: Does the recommendation come with a clear explanation? Is the reasoning behind the recommendation sound and logical? For instance, if someone recommends a particular investment strategy, does their explanation lay out the financial benefits, risks, and long-term expectations in a coherent way?

    2. Evidence and Supporting Information: Is there sufficient evidence or data to support the recommendation? Are the explanations based on reliable facts, studies, or expert opinions, or do they rely on anecdotal information? A doctor recommending a treatment should be able to cite clinical trials, medical research, or case studies to support their recommendation.

    3. Consideration of Alternatives: Does the recommendation consider alternatives and explain why they were not chosen? A strong recommendation should show an awareness of other options and explain why the proposed course of action is superior. If someone recommends a particular university, do they also discuss why other universities might be less suitable based on factors like location, cost, or academic offerings?

    4. Context Appropriateness: Does the recommendation fit the specific context and needs of the individual? Even a well-reasoned recommendation might not be appropriate if it doesn’t align with the person’s particular circumstances, goals, or values. For example, recommending a high-intensity workout routine to someone with a history of joint problems might not be appropriate, even if it's generally effective for fitness.

    5. Potential Bias or Conflicts of Interest: Is there any bias in the recommendation? Does the person making the recommendation have a vested interest in the outcome? For example, if a financial advisor recommends a particular stock but receives a commission from its sale, this potential conflict of interest could affect the credibility of their recommendation.

    6. Feasibility and Practicality: Is the recommendation realistic and practical to implement? Sometimes a recommendation might be theoretically sound but impractical given the available resources, time, or capabilities of the person receiving it. For instance, recommending an elaborate meal plan for someone with a busy schedule might not be feasible, even if it’s nutritionally ideal.

    7. Track Record and Reputation: What is the track record or expertise of the person making the recommendation? Evaluating their qualifications and previous successes or failures can provide insight into the reliability of the recommendation. An IT consultant with years of experience and a strong reputation might offer more trustworthy recommendations than someone less familiar with the field.

    So after an expert has been identified, we must evaluate the relationship between their recommendations and explanations before making a decision. If the recommendation is unaligned with the explanation provided, then we have reason to be skeptical. If "the experts are recommending this new diet to solve your problems", probe the reasoning behind the recommendation. You might find an incongruity between the proposed recommendation and reasons given. You might even find this person to be a false positive.

    Part V

    Synthesis — A Practical Model for Non-Experts

    Compress the preceding theory into a reusable decision procedure without pretending that a universal mechanical score can replace judgment.

    A compact practical worksheet

    The detailed questions in the article can be compressed into a reusable sequence. The point is not to score every expert mechanically; it is to make the relevant uncertainties explicit and to identify where additional inquiry would most improve the decision.

A missing credential may be decisive in one domain and irrelevant in another; consensus may be powerful when sources are independent and misleading when they all inherit the same upstream error. The framework is therefore a disciplined way to ask better questions, not a universal scoring algorithm.
    Part VI

    Limits, Updating, and Conclusion

    Expert identification remains defeasible, domain-sensitive, and stake-sensitive even after a structured method is applied.

    I think this is a good stopping point for now. My main contention is that “expert identification” can at least be partially alleviated by applying tools from Argumentation Theory. I do not think we are completely in the dark, but I also do not think this is a “solvable” problem in the sense of identifying a universal set of criteria that applies to all experts throughout space and time, or of finding a unique solution. To me, this is better understood as a constraint-satisfaction and error-rate-minimization problem. We want our constraints to be discriminating enough to maximize correct classifications while remaining usable under real decision constraints. The process is highly defeasible: knowledge evolves, contexts change, and the credibility of available information changes with them.

    The acceptable error rate should also depend on what is at stake. A false positive in a low-consequence judgment may be tolerable; a false positive in a high-stakes decision may justify a much higher threshold for treating someone as an expert. In that sense, expert identification is not merely a classification problem but a decision problem with asymmetric costs for false positives and false negatives.

    Part of the difficulty also comes from the way “expertise” is conceptualized. Expertise in computer engineering is not identical to expertise in coaching, command, craft, or other practical domains. In some cases we are primarily pointing to formal knowledge and technical know-how; in others we are pointing to practiced skill, strategic judgment, or accumulated experience. Rather than force all of these into a single sharp category, it is more useful to distinguish forms of expertise—such as propositional, technical, practical, strategic, and meta-expertise—and then ask which indicators are appropriate to the form at issue.

    This helps explain why expert identification is difficult: the referent of the definition is unstable when different people are using different operational concepts of expertise. Sometimes the problem is not that a person cannot recognize an expert; it is that the operational definition being used is too broad, too narrow, or mismatched to the task. A football coach, military commander, computer engineer, and scientific researcher may all possess genuine but differently structured expertise, and each will require a somewhat different evidential profile. The goal, then, is not to force every case through an identical checklist, but to choose and weight the relevant criteria for the kind of expertise the decision actually requires.
