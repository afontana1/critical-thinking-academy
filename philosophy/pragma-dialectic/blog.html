Pragma-Dialectics and Argument Evaluation

Pragma-dialectics is a theory of argumentation designed to evaluate argument in practice. Its distinctive move is to treat argument not merely as a relation among propositions, but as a communicative activity carried out by participants who have goals, roles, commitments, and rules of engagement.

Core claim. Evaluating an argument requires more than asking whether a conclusion follows from its premises. Because real argument occurs in dialogue, we also need to evaluate the communicative context, the shared starting points, the permitted forms of inference, and the procedures by which challenges are raised and answered.

Part IFoundations: What Pragma-Dialectics IsThe framework combines a dialectical view of argument as an interactive process with a pragmatic view of language as goal-directed action.

What pragma-dialectics is

The name itself signals the framework's two commitments: pragma refers to the pragmatic dimension of language use, while dialectic refers to argument as a process of interaction. Argumentation is therefore treated as a speech act whose function depends partly on the objectives of the conversation.

Referring to the Wikipedia page, the theoretical justification states:

In pragma-dialectics, argumentation is viewed as a communicative and interactional discourse phenomenon that is to be studied from a normative as well as a descriptive perspective. The dialectical dimension is inspired by normative insights from critical rationalism and formal dialectics, the pragmatic dimension by descriptive insights from speech act theory, Gricean language philosophy and discourse analysis.[2]

The dialectical foundation

The term dialectic has a long history ranging from Plato to Hegel. Without going deeply into that history, the relevant idea here is a process in which participants in dialogue subject propositions to challenge and gradually converge on what can be sustained. Argument is therefore context-bound and interactive rather than an isolated formal object. The Socratic Method is an obvious example, and the medieval quaestio disputata developed a related Aristotelian form of structured disputation.

Later uses of the term moved in a different direction. Hegelianism and Marxism treated dialectic as a way to understand the dynamics of history—how history “unfolds”—rather than primarily as an instrument of discussion (see historicism).

In modern argumentation theory, thinkers such as Stephen Toulmin, Frans H. van Eemeren, John Pollock, and Douglas Walton have attempted to formalize dialectical reasoning, including computational applications to AI and reasoning systems. This work intersects with defeasible reasoning, game-like models of dialogue, and dialogical logic. Because the relevant reasoning is often non-axiomatic and open to defeat, it also fits naturally with critical rationalism: a proposition that cannot be verified or deductively established can still be subjected to criticism and possible falsification. Someone advances an argument; interlocutors then probe it with critical questions.

The pragmatic foundation: speech acts

So far so good; argumentation can be seen as a dialectical process of back and forth critical questioning. It differs from traditional approaches to argument in that it sees argument as a natural language phenomenon conducted between multiple agents interacting. This is where the notion of speech act becomes important. When we communicate and argue with one another, we are not doing formal argumentation. We are typically expressing more than what is implied by the premises alone. Speech act theory helps us understand argument as a natural language process. An overview of speech act levels can be described here:

Speech acts can be analyzed on several levels:

  1. A locutionary act: the performance of an utterance: the actual utterance and its apparent meaning, comprising any and all of its verbal, social, and rhetorical meanings, all of which correspond to the verbal, syntactic and semantic aspects of any meaningful utterance;
  2. an illocutionary act: the active result of the implied request or meaning presented by the locutionary act. For example, if the locutionary act in an interaction is the question "Is there any salt?" the implied illocutionary request is "Please pass the salt to me." or at least "I wish to add salt to my meal.";
  3. and under certain conditions a further perlocutionary act: the actual effect of the locutionary and illocutionary acts, such as persuading, convincing, scaring, enlightening, inspiring, or otherwise getting someone to do or realize something, whether intended or not.[1]
  4. Additionally, a metalocutionary act categorizes speech acts that refer to the forms and functions of the discourse itself rather than continuing the substantive development of the discourse, or to the configurational functions of prosody and punctuation.[11]: 88–93 

Implicature and the Cooperative Principle

When we communicate or argue, we routinely convey more than the literal denotation or connotation of our words. This is the pragmatic side of pragma-dialectics. Paul Grice developed the closely related idea of implicature: meaning that is expressed or implied without being literally uttered. Such pragmatic compression is efficient because not everything has to be made explicit. The reverse problem also occurs: speakers can communicate implications they did not intend.

Consider the familiar example: Person A says, “I am out of gas,” and Person B replies, “There is a gas station around the corner.” Person A has not literally asked a question, yet Person B can infer the practical request because both speakers share a conversational context and background assumptions. If the response succeeds, it is relevant; if the connection is not immediately clear, additional reasoning may be needed to bridge the gap. This resembles the problem AI researchers face when trying to model “common sense.”

Conversational implicature should also be distinguished from conventional implicature, in which words such as “but” or “therefore” themselves signal conceptual relations.

Grice utilizes the notion of Cooperative Principle to describe how people come to mutual understanding:

Make your contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged.

MaximRequirementArgumentative function
QualityDo not say what you believe to be false; do not say what you lack adequate evidence for.Truthfulness and evidential responsibility.
QuantityMake your contribution as informative as required for the exchange, but no more informative than required.Provide enough information without obscuring the issue through unnecessary detail.
Relation / RelevanceMake your contributions relevant.Keep contributions connected to the purpose and standpoint under discussion.
MannerAvoid obscurity and ambiguity; be brief where possible; be orderly.Make the argument interpretable and reduce avoidable misunderstanding.

— Grice (1975:26–27), Levinson (1983:100–102)

From pragmatics to critical discussion

The connection should now be clearer. Argumentative discourse has both pragmatic and dialectical dimensions. If participants are trying to reach understanding or resolve a disagreement, some general procedures must govern what counts as a relevant move at each point in the exchange. Pragma-dialectics both describes and prescribes those structures, especially in critical discussion.

This is also why the surrounding literature connects so readily. Walton & Macagno’s Implicatures as Forms of Argument, for example, treats conversational implicature as a form of argument that can reduce doubt when information is incomplete. The same cluster of ideas—dialogue, implicit meaning, uncertainty, and defeasible inference—has important implications for AI.

The next step is to move from the foundations of the theory to the structure of a critical discussion itself.

Dialogue types and context

On to the discussion of the paper: A Pragma-dialectical Procedure for a Critical Discussion. The main idea here is that argument takes place in a dialogue. There are multiple stages within a dialogue in which specific argumentative strategies are or are not allowed. There are many different types of dialogues, and different stages within each dialogue. The figure below shows a few catalogued by Walton

At a basic level this is intuitive: different communicative contexts are governed by different expectations. The stages and acceptable moves in a persuasion dialogue are not identical to those of a negotiation, an inquiry, or an eristic quarrel. A fallacy can therefore be understood as a violation of a rule appropriate to a particular type of dialogue rather than merely as a context-free defect.

These dialogue types are ideal forms, not rigid boxes. Real conversations can shift rapidly from one to another. A presidential debate, for example, may move from persuasion into eristic exchange in seconds. The earlier gas-station example makes the same point from the pragmatic side: replying “prove it” to “I am out of gas” would be bizarre because it ignores the dialogue's practical goal and the implicature carried by the utterance.

The problem the procedure is solving

Enough background: now we can turn to the paper itself. The central question is whether a protagonist's standpoint can withstand an antagonist's criticism. A critical discussion consists of challenges, defenses, possible subdiscussions, and eventually either acceptance, retraction, or unresolved disagreement.

The procedural problem is therefore demanding. The rules must promote resolution while allowing enough flexibility for real argument. They must handle multiple agents and standpoints, define when particular speech acts contribute to resolution, and prevent participants from changing the game opportunistically. A courtroom provides a useful analogy: not every gesture, assertion, inference, or evidential move is admissible simply because a participant wants to make it.

"This makes it necessary to indicate for each discussion stage when exactly the parties are entitled to perform a particular kind of speech act, and if and when they are even obliged to do so." (Pg.3)

Part IIIRules 1–5: Setting the DiscussionThe first five rules establish the freedom to challenge, the burden of defense, the roles of the participants, and the standards by which the exchange will be judged.

These rules are mostly procedural. Before the merits of an argument can be tested, participants need to know who may challenge what, who bears the burden, and which standards govern the exchange.

Rule 1 — Freedom to challenge a standpoint

RULE 1 

a. Special conditions apply neither to the propositional content of the assertives by which a standpoint is expressed, nor to the propositional content of the negation of the commissive by means of which a standpoint is called into question; 

b. in the performance of these assertives and negative commissives, no special preparatory conditions apply to the position or status of the speaker or writer and listener or reader.

Participants in the dialogue will have the unconditional right to call into question every stance of every other discussant. This is the confrontation stage. If people agree, there is no need to embark to the next stage. There are no propositions that are off limits, and there is no status of a speaker which is off limits. You are not allowed to prevent others from voicing potential questions. Participants are not obligated to state anything, but once something is stated they can freely question its grounds and engage in critical discussion. At this point, everyone begins to converge on what some of the rules of engagement might be. 

Rule 2 — The right to demand a defense

RULE 2 

The discussant that has called the standpoint of the other discussant into question in the confrontation stage is always entitled to challenge this discussant to defend his or her standpoint.

A follow-up on this rule is that the person advancing a standpoint can simply retract. If they do choose to defend, then:

 A discussant that has successfully defended a standpoint is not subsequently obliged to defend the same standpoint again according to the same discussion rules and with the same premises against the same discussant. This principle does not apply to discussions either with a different challenger, or with the same challenger but with different premises, or different discussion rules. (p.6)

Discussion can be reopened, but only with new premises or if the initial response was subject to doubt after additional information became available. Note that the person advancing the claim is always obligated to defend or retract. 

Rule 3 — The burden to defend or retract

RULE 3 

The discussant who is challenged by the other discussant to defend the standpoint that he or she has put forward in the confrontation stage is always obliged to accept this challenge, unless the other discussant is not prepared to accept certain shared premises and discussion rules; the discussant remains obliged to defend the standpoint as long as he or she does not retract it and as long as he or she has not successfully defended it against the other discussant on the basis of the agreed premises and discussion rules.

If you cannot agree on definitions, preliminary conceptions, or shared rules of engagement you do not have to accept the challenge, but are still obligated to defend the standpoint assuming it is not retracted. If you already defended it in another place, you can refer the antagonist to that defense. "Agree to disagree" is not how an argument is resolved. This accounts for the burden of proof: whoever puts forth a position is obligated to defend it. Who has to demonstrate the weight of evidence? The protagonist.

Rule 4 — Assigning protagonist and antagonist roles

RULE 4 

The discussant that in the opening stage has accepted the other discussant’s challenge to defend his or her standpoint will fulfill the role of protagonist in the argumentation stage, and the other discussant will fulfill the role of antagonist, unless they agree otherwise; the distribution of roles is maintained until the end of the discussion.

During the argumentation stage, which rules should apply? In court of law, certain evidence is admissible. Certain inferences like "speculation" are not allowed. Character attacks are also frowned upon unless the person's character is somehow relevant to the goal of the discussion. There can be exceptional cases for this say if a psychologist deems it likely that person of type X will do such and such. The weight or force of this inference may be defined as less significant compared to something more demonstrable and evident. All of these rules can be determined during the argumentation stage and the key is to remain consistent with the rules and to clearly delineate their boundaries. In non-legal settings, any rule can be called into question. How strongly should the rule apply? What are the conditions of good argument? Discussion about the rules of discussion leads to a metadiscussion.

Rule 5 — Fixing the rules of engagement

RULE 5 

The discussants who will fulfill the roles of protagonist and antagonist in the argumentation stage agree before the start of the argumentation stage on the rules for the following: how the protagonist is to defend the initial standpoint and how the antagonist is to attack this standpoint, and in which case the protagonist has successfully defended the standpoint and in which case the antagonist has successfully attacked it; the rules in which this is laid down apply throughout the duration of the discussion, and may not be called into question during the discussion itself by either of the parties

Questions about ethical argument, relevance, evidential sufficiency, and burden of proof should therefore be addressed before participants know which standards will favor them. If someone begins to lose, the standards cannot simply be revised mid-discussion. The antagonist may probe whether a provisional defense has enough evidence, the right kind of evidence, an appropriately limited claim, or an obvious fallacy or cognitive bias.

The deeper point is procedural consistency. If a controversial issue is worth arguing about, the participants need some shared understanding of the discussion's scope and of the constraints on acceptable reasoning. Personal abuse—“you are dumb,” for example—does not test the propositional content or justificatory force of a standpoint and therefore does not perform the relevant argumentative work.

With the discussion established, the theory can turn to the central evaluative problem: what makes a defense or attack succeed?

Part IVRules 6–9: What Makes an Argument Defensible?This is the conceptual center of the procedure. The rules distinguish acceptable propositional content from the inferential force that connects reasons to a standpoint.

Rule 6 — Permissible forms of attack and defense

RULE 6 

a. The protagonist may always defend the standpoint that he or she adopts in the initial difference of opinion or in a sub-difference of opinion by performing a complex speech act of argumentation, which then counts as a provisional defense of this standpoint;

 b. the antagonist may always attack a standpoint by calling into question its propositional content or the force of the argumentation as a justification or refutation;

 c. the protagonist and the antagonist may not defend or attack standpoints in any other way.

How do we know if an argument is good? If the protagonist has put forth an argument in accordance with the rules established, and the antagonist has accepted the argument according to the rules. The protagonist "successfully defended" the position in this case, and if they do not do this, then the antagonist has "successfully attacked" the argument. But how do we know if a proposition is acceptable? 

Shared premises and propositional content

The propositions that are accepted by both parties may concern facts, truths, norms, values or value hierarchies. The discussants are completely free to draw up a list of propositions accepted by both parties. All of the propositions that they both accept may be included. The only restriction is that the list must be consistent. It may not contain any propositions that contradict other propositions. Otherwise it would always be possible to defend successfully any arbitrary standpoint against any attacker, which inevitably renders the resolution of a difference of opinion impossible. That a proposition is included in the list of accepted propositions means only that the discussants are agreed that the proposition in question may not be called into question during the discussion. In other words, they may treat this proposition in the discussion as though they accept it, whether or not this is actually the case. The list specifies which propositions have been accepted by the discussants for the duration of the discussion and can therefore be regarded as their shared premises.

This is the intersubjective problem of establishing common ground. The propositions accepted for the discussion function as its provisional “facts”: they are treated as beyond dispute for the duration of that exchange, and they cannot be mutually contradictory without making resolution arbitrary.

Not every premise, however, must already appear on a pre-agreed list. New information can be introduced. Once that possibility is admitted, the discussion also needs standards for acquiring and evaluating new information. In other words, the participants need not only shared premises, but some shared account of how a previously unsettled premise can become acceptable.

Rule 7 — Testing propositional content

RULE 7 

a. The protagonist has successfully defended a complex speech act of argumentation against an attack by the antagonist with regard to its propositional content if the application of the intersubjective identification procedure yields a positive result or if the propositional content is in the second instance accepted by both parties as a result of a subdiscussion in which the protagonist has succesfully defended a positive sub-standpoint with regard to this propositional content; 

b. the antagonist has successfully attacked the propositional content of the complex speech act of argumentation if the application of the intersubjective identification procedure yields a negative result and the protagonist has not succesfully defended a positive sub-standpoint with regard to this propositional content in a sub-discussion.

A recurring theme is that both the reasons for accepting a proposition and the force of the reasoning must be made explicit. Rule 7 focuses first on propositional content. The authors' “intersubjective identification” procedure asks whether the relevant proposition can be accepted under the shared standards or successfully defended in a subdiscussion.

This already introduces an important constraint: not every kind of evidence or argumentative move must be admissible in every context. A court may exclude speculative inference; a scientific inquiry may demand controlled evidence; another dialogue may permit weaker presumptive reasoning. The question is not merely what is asserted, but under what standards it can become an acceptable premise.

Argument schemes and defeasibility

There are many kinds of argument schemes. Reasoning Lab collects several major catalogues, and Douglas Walton's work provides a particularly influential treatment; a collection of materials is available here. In A Classification System for Argumentation Schemes, Walton groups schemes into broad families such as source-based arguments, analogy, cause and effect, expert opinion, practical reasoning, rule application, cases, signs, position to know, and consequences.

The larger point is that ordinary reasoning tends to instantiate recognizable patterns. Each pattern has a structure and a corresponding family of critical questions. A useful introduction to argument mining is available here.

Argumentation Schemes capture stereotypical, non-deductive, and non-monotonic forms of reasoning. 

The definite clause logic is monotonic in the sense that anything that could be concluded before a clause is added can still be concluded after it is added; adding knowledge does not reduce the set of propositions that can be derived.

A logic is non-monotonic if some conclusions can be invalidated by adding more knowledge. The logic of definite clauses with negation as failure is non-monotonic. Non-monotonic reasoning is useful for representing defaults. A default is a rule that can be used unless it overridden by an exception.  (Artificial Intelligence: Foundations for Computational Agents).

Non-monotonic logic captures the possibility that a conclusion can be defeated when new information arrives. This is closely connected to defeasibility, which characterizes much natural-language argumentation. The Stanford Encyclopedia article on Defeasibility develops the idea in detail. My own view is that a very large share of reasoning under uncertainty is best understood in defeasible terms because certainty is rarely available and the knowledge base remains open.

Argumentation schemes help make those defeasible forms explicit. They also connect naturally to pragma-dialectics: if reasoning occurs in a context, then both the scheme being used and the questions appropriate to that scheme become part of the critical discussion. Monotonic entailment provides the contrast: when a conclusion is strictly entailed, adding information does not invalidate the inference.

Notice the direction of entailment in the picture above. If you know that Mary is not a linguist, you know she cannot be a semanticist. But the reverse doe not hold true. Think about other intractable problems. Suppose you see a puddle where there once was ice. Is it possible to infer what the shape and structure of the ice was from the puddle?

Monotonicity means that learning new information does not reduce the set of what follows. Defeasible reasoning behaves differently. Consider: P1: all birds fly; P2: Tweety is a bird; therefore Tweety flies. The inference looks deductive only until we learn that Tweety is a penguin. In an open and changing knowledge base, the major premise is better qualified as “typically, birds fly.”

This connects directly to belief revision: rational agents must revise beliefs when relevant information changes. It also supplies a useful question for the pragma-dialectical discussion itself: is the protagonist inflating the scope of a claim that should have been defeasibly qualified?

Defeasible reasoning finds its fullest expression in jurisprudence, ethics and moral philosophy, epistemology, pragmatics and conversational conventions in linguistics, constructivist decision theories, and in knowledge representation and planning in artificial intelligence. It is also closely identified with prima facie (presumptive) reasoning (i.e., reasoning on the "face" of evidence), and ceteris paribus (default) reasoning (i.e., reasoning, all things "being equal").

Now return to Rule 7. A discussion might exclude an “Argument from Popular Opinion” because popularity is unreliable for establishing truth. An argument from analogy may be similarly weak for demonstrating scientific cause and effect. In one sense we cannot think entirely outside analogy—it is often indispensable as a heuristic—but heuristic usefulness is not the same thing as proof: “Drug Z is similar to Drug X, therefore Drug X will cure Y” is not enough to establish efficacy.

Yet analogy and abductive reasoning cannot simply be discarded. Medical practitioners often have to reason from similarities among cases when direct tests are unavailable. If one patient with symptoms X, Y, and B responded to a treatment, a clinician may defeasibly reason that another patient with X, Y, and Z could respond as well. The conclusion remains provisional. New information—for example, a side effect associated with attributes A, B, and C—can defeat or weaken it. That is precisely what an open knowledge base requires.

Critical questions and inferential force

Every acceptable argument scheme has corresponding critical questions. Suppose a standpoint rests on Expert A's testimony. We can ask whether A is genuinely an expert in the relevant domain, whether expertise is possible there, whether other experts broadly agree, whether A's statement is relevant, and whether A has enough information to support the inference.

Those questions test more than the bare presence of a premise. They test the force of the reason. An expert's statement may be relevant yet still weak on its own; combined with independent evidence, the same argument may become much stronger. Context matters as well. An analogy that is insufficient to establish guilt in court might still have heuristic value elsewhere, and an argument from authority may function differently in scriptural interpretation than in an empirical scientific dispute. Before reasoning, we therefore need to ask not only what reasons we have, but what kind of reasoning those reasons license.

Rule 8 — Testing the force of justification or refutation

RULE 8 

a. The protagonist has successfully defended a complex speech act of argumentation against an attack by the antagonist with regard to its force of justification or refutation if the application of the intersubjective inference procedure or (after application of the intersubjective explicitization procedure) the application of the intersubjective testing procedure, yields a positive result;

 b. if the application of the intersubjective inference procedure or (after application of the intersubjective explicitization procedure) the application of the intersubjective testing procedure yields a negative result, the antagonist has successfully attacked the force of justification or refutation of the argumentation.

For a conclusive defense of a standpoint, the protagonist must have defended both the propositional content of the argumentation (as prescribed in rule 7) and its force of justification or refutation with regard to the proposition on which the standpoint bears (as prescribed in rule 8). For a conclusive attack on a standpoint, the antagonist must have successfully attacked either the propositional content of the argumentation or its force of justification or refutation (as prescribed in rules 7 and 8). 

Rule 9 — Conclusive defense and attack

RULE 9 

a. The protagonist has conclusively defended an initial standpoint or substandpoint by means of a complex speech act of argumentation if he or she has successfully defended both the propositional content called into question by the antagonist and its force of justification or refutation called into question by the antagonist; 

b. the antagonist has conclusively attacked the standpoint of the protagonist if he or she has successfully attacked either the propositional content or the force of justification or refutation of the complex speech act of argumentation.

The word complex in “complex speech act” matters here. There is no single objective measure of how complex an argument must be, but “because I say so” is obviously near the minimal end of the spectrum. A serious defense may need breadth, depth, precision, multiple reasons, explicit qualifications, and responses to critical questions. This helps explain why the paper repeatedly emphasizes complex speech acts: a bare assertion or extremely simple reason may not yet amount to the kind of developed defense that critical discussion is designed to test.

Rule 9 also reveals an asymmetry between defense and attack. The protagonist must successfully defend the relevant components of the argument, whereas the antagonist can defeat the defense by successfully attacking a component that is necessary to it. If the protagonist repairs that point, the discussion can move to the next live issue.

Three dimensions of argument evaluation

A useful synthesis: the framework separates three kinds of failure that are often collapsed together. An argument can fail because the procedure is defective, because its propositional content is unacceptable, or because its inferential force is too weak.

DimensionWhat is being evaluatedTypical failure
ProceduralWhether participants follow the norms governing challenge, burden, roles, relevance, turn taking, clarification, and closure.Burden shifting, changing standards midstream, misrepresentation, refusing clarification, or disorderly conduct.
PropositionalWhether the premises or propositions can be accepted under the agreed identification procedures and shared starting points.Unsupported, inconsistent, contradictory, or inadmissible premises.
InferentialWhether the argument scheme and its application provide sufficient justificatory or refutatory force.A weak analogy, unreliable authority, causal overreach, or failure to answer critical questions.

Rules 10–13 now regulate what the participants may continue to challenge, defend, retract, and repeat while that evaluation is underway.

Part VRules 10–13: Rights and Conduct During the ExchangeOnce argumentation is underway, the procedure preserves reciprocal rights to challenge and defend while preventing repetition, burden shifting, interruption, and disorderly topic changes.

Rule 10 — The antagonist’s continuing right to challenge

RULE 10 

The antagonist retains throughout the entire discussion the right to call into question both the propositional content and the force of justification or refutation of every complex speech act of argumentation of the protagonist that the latter has not yet successfully defended. 

The antagonist may continue to attack any component that has not yet been successfully defended. The protagonist cannot simply declare that an objection concerns an “unimportant” part of the case when that part is necessary to the defense.

That does not mean every objection is substantial. Trivial objections can function as distractions. If I complain that waterfowl are annoying because the geese outside my apartment are loud, “but ducks are not loud” misses the thrust of the claim rather than answering it. That kind of move resembles a red herring. Still, the label “trivial” cannot be used arbitrarily to immunize a claim from criticism; the relevance of the challenged point itself has to be explained.

Rule 11 — The protagonist’s continuing right to defend

RULE 11 

The protagonist retains throughout the entire discussion the right to defend both the propositional content and the force of justification or refutation of every complex speech act of argumentation that he or she has performed and not yet successfully defended against every attack by the antagonist.

Rule 11 is the reciprocal counterpart of Rule 10: as long as an argumentative component remains under attack and has not yet been successfully defended, the protagonist retains the right to answer that attack.

Rule 12 — The right to retract argumentation

RULE 12 

The protagonist retains throughout the entire discussion the right to retract the complex speech act of argumentation that he or she has performed and thereby to remove the obligation to defend it.

A protagonist is not bound permanently to an initial argument. If objections expose a weakness, the argument can be retracted or revised rather than defended indefinitely. The procedure therefore permits belief and argument revision instead of rewarding stubborn consistency.

Rule 13 — Orderly conduct: repetition, turns, and issue control

RULE 13

 a. The protagonist and the antagonist may only once perform the same speech act or the same complex speech act with the same role in the discussion. 

b. The protagonist and the antagonist must perform speech acts or complex speech acts in turn. 

c. The protagonist and the antagonist may not perform more than one speech act or complex speech act at one time.

Rule 13 regulates the conduct of the exchange. First, participants may not endlessly repeat the same move in the same role or shift the burden with “well, you prove me wrong.” Second, speech acts are performed in turn rather than by talking over one another. Third, participants should make one argumentative move at a time rather than bouncing among unrelated topics.

These constraints may seem mundane, but procedures require norms of conduct in order to work. Without them, even good arguments can disappear inside interruption, repetition, and issue drift.

The final two rules explain how a discussion closes and how participants repair ambiguity before misunderstanding derails the exchange.

Part VIRules 14–15: Closure and Mutual UnderstandingA critical discussion needs both an endpoint and a method for repairing unclear formulations. Resolution is impossible if parties never retract defeated positions or cannot agree on what has actually been said.

Rule 14 — Closure: retracting a defeated standpoint or doubt

RULE 14 

a. The protagonist is obliged to retract the initial standpoint if the antagonist has conclusively attacked it (in the manner prescribed in rule 9) in the argumentation stage (and has also observed the other discussion rules); 

b. the antagonist is obliged to retract the calling into question of the initial standpoint if the protagonist has conclusively defended it (in the manner prescribed in rule 9) in the argumentation stage (and has also observed the other discussion rules); 

c. in all other cases the protagonist is not obliged to retract the initial standpoint, nor is the antagonist obliged to withdraw his or her calling into question the initial standpoint.

If the antagonist's attack is conclusively defeated, the doubt must be retracted; if the protagonist's standpoint is conclusively attacked, the standpoint must be retracted. When neither side achieves a conclusive result, both may retain their positions, and a new discussion can always be opened under new premises or rules.

The important point is that a critical discussion cannot resolve anything if a participant simply reintroduces a position that has already been conclusively dealt with under the agreed conditions.

Rule 15 — Usage declaratives, clarification, and charitable interpretation

RULE 15 

a. The discussants have the right at every stage of the discussion to request the other discussant to perform a usage declarative and to perform one themselves. 

b. the discussant who is requested to perform a usage declarative by the other discussant is obliged to do so.

The last rule concerns rights and obligations surrounding “usage declaratives”: participants must be able to clarify what they mean and must try to understand what the other participant means. If a standpoint is poorly formulated or misinterpreted, the parties will speak at cross-purposes rather than resolve a disagreement.

This is where ideas such as the Principle of Charity and steelmanning become relevant. Participants should formulate and interpret as well as they can, and they should be willing to replace inadequate formulations with better ones. Deliberate misinterpretation is worse than ordinary ambiguity because it turns the interlocutor into an enemy whose position is reconstructed in its least defensible form.

This becomes especially difficult in emotionally charged topics. A person who hears “I favor stronger border control” may immediately substitute a maximally hostile interpretation for whatever the speaker actually meant. At that point the exchange becomes less like critical discussion and more like existential defense. Critical thinking requires the ability to represent an argument as it is before attacking it.

Aiming for optimal formulations and optimal interpretations does not, unfortunately, automatically mean that these aims are attained. To be on the safe side, discussants who doubt the clarity of their formulation do well to replace it by a formulation that they consider to be clearer, and discussants who doubt their interpretation do well, to be on the safe side, to put it to the other discussant and to ask for an amplification, specification or other usage declarative.

So think carefully about both sides of formulation. How can you state your own position more clearly, and how can you represent your opponent's position more accurately? A phrase such as “So you are saying that…” can be a legitimate request for clarification, but it becomes destructive when it repeatedly introduces a distorted version of the original claim.

This does not mean clarification should be abandoned merely because disagreement is difficult. The antagonist has a right to ask what the protagonist means, and clarification may even dissolve an apparent disagreement before a substantive attack is necessary. But when a participant continues to misrepresent a position after repeated clarification, the conditions for a productive critical discussion may no longer be present.

Part VIIResolution, Truth, and Epistemic LimitsA procedure for resolving disagreement is not automatically a procedure for guaranteeing truth. Shared standards can themselves be weak, parochial, or epistemically defective.

Resolution is not the same thing as truth. A discussion can be procedurally well-formed and still reach a bad conclusion if the participants begin from poor shared premises or accept unreliable standards of evidence and inference.

The explicitization and testing procedures therefore have to look beyond an immediate social “bubble.” Suppose two participants agree that correlation is enough to establish causation and that anecdotal evidence is sufficient support. They might then reject a vaccination on the basis of anecdotal reports claiming that vaccines cause autism. The dialogue could be internally consistent while the epistemic standards remain badly deficient.

The problem is not merely that the participants disagree with someone else. Anecdotes were not collected under systematic or standardized conditions; there is no experimental control and no reference to randomized controlled trials. If those standards are never brought into view, almost anything can be made to look warranted; the same loose method could be used to attribute some other disease or outcome to the vaccine. A participant who does not understand statistics may not even know the extent of what is missing. Nor is it enough to dismiss someone who demands experimental evidence as merely “biased”; the dispute is then about the epistemic standards themselves.

This is why the conditions for a successful argument matter so much. Procedures for dialogue constrain how disagreement is managed, but following those procedures does not by itself guarantee a true conclusion. External epistemic standards—reliable methods, evidence quality, appropriate expertise, and well-tested forms of inference—remain necessary. A critical discussion is therefore best understood as a disciplined method for testing standpoints, not as a machine that turns agreement into truth.

Part VIIIWhy This Matters for Artificial IntelligenceThe framework suggests that artificial reasoning systems need more than formal inference. They also need models of dialogue state, commitments, implicit meaning, defeasible inference, and revision.

The AI connection follows from the structure developed throughout the article. A system that participates in argument must do more than calculate whether one proposition formally entails another. It must recognize the type and stage of the dialogue, identify who has made which commitment, infer pragmatic meaning, track shared premises, and decide which critical questions are appropriate to a particular argument scheme.

Defeasible and non-monotonic reasoning make this especially important. Real-world knowledge bases are open: new information can weaken or defeat previous conclusions. An artificial reasoner therefore needs some analogue of belief revision, together with procedures for distinguishing a challenge to propositional content from a challenge to inferential force. Argumentation schemes and their critical questions provide one possible vocabulary for organizing that evaluation.

The limitation identified above also carries over to AI. A system can follow a dialogue protocol flawlessly while reasoning from bad premises or bad epistemic standards. Procedural competence is not the same thing as truth-tracking. A robust reasoning system would therefore need both dialogue-level norms and independent mechanisms for evaluating evidence, sources, uncertainty, and the reliability of inferential patterns.

Conclusion

This is a lot of machinery, but it changes the way argument evaluation looks. Argument is not merely a sequence of premises ending in a conclusion. It is also a communicative process in which participants challenge standpoints, establish starting points, negotiate acceptable forms of reasoning, test both content and inferential force, repair ambiguity, and eventually retract either a standpoint or a doubt when the agreed conditions have been met.

The framework also clarifies why good procedure is necessary but insufficient. Critical discussion can make disagreement more disciplined and more resolvable, yet agreement does not guarantee truth. The quality of the premises, evidence, argument schemes, and critical questions still matters. That is why the procedural, propositional, and inferential dimensions of argument evaluation have to work together.

Now return to ordinary public argument. Do political debates follow anything resembling these norms? Do we? These rules can prevent difficult discussions from devolving into the spectacles we often see on television, but only if participants care more about testing their positions than preserving the illusion that they are right. A useful final question is therefore not simply “Did my side win?” but “Would I be convinced by an argument that ignored these standards if it came from someone I already disagreed with?”
