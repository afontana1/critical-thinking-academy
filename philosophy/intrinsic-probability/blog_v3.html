1. Introduction: The Target and the Burden of Proof

Philosophers sometimes speak of the “intrinsic probability” of a hypothesis: the probability a hypothesis possesses prior to the consideration of some body of empirical evidence. The idea is often connected to familiar theoretical virtues such as simplicity, elegance, explanatory scope, coherence, modesty, or unification. A hypothesis that is simpler, more elegant, or less ad hoc may be said to possess a greater intrinsic probability than a more complicated rival, even before the relevant evidence is considered.

The terminology is tempting because it appears to fit naturally into Bayesian reasoning. If \(H\) is a hypothesis, \(E\) is some body of evidence, and \(K\) represents background knowledge or assumptions, then Bayesian updating may be written as

\[ P(H\mid E,K) = \frac{P(E\mid H,K)P(H\mid K)} {P(E\mid K)}. \]

The likelihood term,

\[ P(E\mid H,K), \] represents how expected the evidence would be if the hypothesis were true, while the prior, \(P(H\mid K)\), represents the probability assigned to the hypothesis before the new evidence \(E\) is incorporated. It is therefore natural to ask what determines this prior probability. One possible answer is that at least part of it is determined by features of the hypothesis itself. Simpler, more coherent, or more elegant hypotheses are assigned greater prior probability because they are, in some sense, antecedently more plausible.

The difficulty is that this answer moves too quickly between several fundamentally different concepts. Simplicity is not probability. Elegance is not probability. Coherence is not probability. Nor does a preference for one theory over another, however rational that preference may be, by itself constitute a probability assignment. At most, such theoretical virtues may provide reasons for ranking or preferring hypotheses. A further argument is required before that ranking can be interpreted as a measure of the probability that a hypothesis is true. This distinction matters because probability is not simply a generic numerical expression of epistemic approval. A probability measure is a particular kind of mathematical structure. Given a probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\), the measure \(P\) assigns values to events in accordance with the axioms of probability. Those axioms constrain the behavior of the measure once it has been specified. They do not, by themselves, tell us which probability measure ought to be adopted, nor do they extract a probability from the semantic content of a proposition. A hypothesis \(H\) does not therefore appear to carry with it some determinate value \(P(H)\) merely in virtue of what it says. To arrive at such a value, some additional structure must be supplied. We need a relevant space of hypotheses, a way of distinguishing and representing them, and some rule governing the allocation of probability mass among them. If simplicity, elegance, coherence, or other theoretical virtues are supposed to determine that allocation, then we must additionally specify what those virtues mean, how they are measured, how conflicts among them are resolved, how they are weighted, and how the resulting evaluation is transformed into a probability measure. Once these commitments are made explicit, the supposed intrinsic probability begins to look considerably less intrinsic. What initially appears as \(P(H)\) is better represented as something like

\[ P_M(H), \] or more explicitly, \(P(H\mid M,K)\), where \(M\) represents a methodological or meta-theoretical framework governing how hypotheses are evaluated and how those evaluations are converted into prior probabilities. The probability assignment is therefore not generated by \(H\) alone. It is generated by \(H\) together with a substantial body of assumptions concerning theory choice, representation, and probabilistic modeling.

This essay argues that this dependence undermines the notion of intrinsic probability. The claim is deliberately narrower than the denial that priors can be assigned, justified, or even objectively constrained. The target is the stronger thesis that a hypothesis determines a probability of truth merely in virtue of its own content or theoretical virtues, independently of the representational, methodological, and probabilistic framework under which it is assessed. Theoretical virtues may provide a methodological basis for preferring one hypothesis over another, and in some circumstances they may even be formalized sufficiently to construct a prior distribution. But neither result establishes that a hypothesis possesses a probability intrinsically. At best, a catalogue of theoretical virtues induces a preference relation—perhaps only a partial one—over a space of hypotheses. Turning that relation into a probability distribution requires additional assumptions that are themselves contestable, theory-laden, and external to the hypotheses being evaluated. The central claim can therefore be expressed schematically as \(H \longrightarrow V(H) \longrightarrow \succeq \not\Rightarrow P(H)\), where \(V(H)\) represents the theoretical virtues attributed to a hypothesis and \(\succeq\) represents a methodological preference relation. The transition from such a relation to a probability measure is not supplied by the virtues themselves. Where a probability distribution is nevertheless constructed, the more accurate representation is \(M + H \longrightarrow P_M(H)\), not

\[ H \longrightarrow P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H). \]

The difference is not merely terminological. It marks the difference between a probability that belongs to a hypothesis in virtue of its own character and a probability assigned to that hypothesis by a particular methodological model. The argument developed here is that only the latter is intelligible. There is no probability contained within the proposition itself waiting to be recovered from its simplicity, elegance, or other alleged theoretical virtues.

What the Argument Does—and Does Not—Claim

The essay is easiest to follow if three claims are kept separate from the outset:

Claim Status in the argument
Priors may be assigned to hypotheses. Accepted.
Some priors may be better justified than others. Accepted.
A hypothesis possesses a determinate probability merely in virtue of its own content or theoretical virtues. Denied unless an additional grounding argument is supplied.

The burden on a defender of intrinsic probability is therefore not merely to produce a normalized set of numbers. A successful account must explain, at minimum, which features of a hypothesis are probability-relevant, how those features are identified and measured, how multiple virtues are aggregated, how an ordinal or partial ranking becomes a cardinal probability distribution, why the resulting numbers should be interpreted as probabilities of truth, and why the assignment is stable across changes of representation, background knowledge, and hypothesis-space construction. The sections that follow address these requirements in that order.

The first task is therefore deliberately modest: before asking whether simplicity or elegance can determine a prior, we must ask what probability theory itself supplies. The answer establishes the burden that every later proposal must meet.

2. Probability Requires a Measure; a Proposition Does Not Supply One

The first problem with intrinsic probability is that probability theory does not provide a mechanism by which a proposition generates its own probability. Probability theory begins with a measure; it does not derive that measure from the content of the propositions to which it is applied. In the standard Kolmogorov formulation, a probability space is given by \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\), where \(\Omega\) is a space of possible outcomes, \(\mathcal{F}\) is a collection of measurable events, and \(P\) is a probability measure satisfying the familiar axioms of non-negativity, normalization, and countable additivity. For any event \(A\in\mathcal{F}\),

\[ 0\leq P(A)\leq 1, \]

with

\[ P(\Omega)=1. \]

If \(A_1,A_2,\ldots\) are mutually exclusive events, then

\[ P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(A_i). \]

These conditions tell us what a probability measure must be like. They do not tell us which probability measure must be chosen.

This distinction is easy to overlook because, once a measure has been specified, probability calculations can proceed mechanically. But there may be many probability measures satisfying the axioms over the same space. The axioms constrain admissible measures; they do not uniquely select one. Nothing in the mathematical formalism alone determines whether a particular hypothesis should receive probability \(0.7\), \(0.2\), or \(10^{-6}\).

This becomes especially important when the object under consideration is not a repeatable physical event such as a coin toss, but a hypothesis about the world. Suppose we have a set of possible hypotheses

\[ \mathcal{H} = \{H_1,H_2,\ldots\}. \]

Assigning probabilities to these hypotheses requires a probability measure over \(\mathcal{H}\) or over an appropriate sigma-algebra constructed from it. The proposition \(H_i\) does not itself supply that measure. One must specify how probability mass is to be distributed across the competing hypotheses. In other words, the implication \(H \Longrightarrow P(H)\) does not exist merely as a consequence of probability theory. What is required is something more like \((H,\mathcal{H},M) \longmapsto P_M(H)\), where \(M\) represents whatever modeling assumptions determine the measure. These assumptions may concern the structure of the hypothesis space, the distribution from which hypotheses are imagined to arise, the coding language in which they are represented, prior empirical information, or methodological principles governing how competing hypotheses should be weighted.

This does not make probability arbitrary. In many applications there are excellent reasons for choosing one probability model rather than another. A physical symmetry may justify a uniform distribution. A known data-generating process may determine a sampling distribution. Previous observations may constrain a parameter distribution. In each case, however, the probability assignment is justified by something beyond the bare proposition whose probability is being evaluated. Consider an ordinary die. If we write \(P(X=4)=\frac{1}{6}\), that number does not arise from the proposition “the die lands on four.” It follows from a model in which the six outcomes are assigned equal probability. If the die is weighted, the model changes and so does the probability. The event has remained the same; what changed was the probabilistic structure imposed upon the outcome space. The same point applies more strongly to hypotheses. Suppose \(H\) is the claim that some physical theory correctly describes a particular domain. What feature of the proposition itself determines whether \(P(H)=0.1\) rather than

\[ P(H)=0.01? \]

The syntax of the sentence does not determine the number. Its truth conditions do not determine the number. Its logical form does not determine the number. To obtain a numerical assignment, one needs some further rule connecting properties of the hypothesis, or information concerning it, to a probability measure.

This is where the usual notation \(P(H)\) can obscure more than it reveals. In epistemic contexts, probabilities are ordinarily relative to some body of background knowledge, assumptions, or information. It is therefore often more revealing to write

\[ P(H\mid K), \]

where \(K\) represents that background. Likewise, posterior probability should more fully be represented as \(P(H\mid E,K)\), where \(E\) is the evidence under consideration and \(K\) contains the assumptions already in place. Bayes’ theorem then takes the form

\[ P(H\mid E,K) = \frac{P(E\mid H,K)P(H\mid K)} {P(E\mid K)}. \] This notation makes clear that a prior is not necessarily a probability assigned in a state of complete ignorance. It is simply prior relative to the new evidence \(E\). The term \(P(H\mid K)\) may already depend on substantial background commitments.

This matters directly for intrinsic probability. If a philosopher claims that a simpler or more elegant hypothesis should receive a greater prior probability, then the relevant probability is not being supplied by the hypothesis alone. It is being supplied by a methodological principle contained within \(K\), or within some additional framework \(M\). The real expression is therefore closer to

\[ P(H\mid K,M), \]

where \(M\) includes claims about which features of hypotheses ought to influence prior probability.

Once the conditioning structure is made explicit, the apparent intrinsic character of the probability begins to disappear. A hypothesis may receive one probability under one methodological framework and a different probability under another:

\[ P(H\mid M_1)\neq P(H\mid M_2). \]

Yet \(H\) itself has not changed. If one framework strongly rewards simplicity while another places greater weight on explanatory scope, the same proposition may receive different prior probabilities. This is not a contradiction within probability theory. It simply reflects the fact that different probability models have been specified. But it does create a problem for the claim that the probability belongs intrinsically to the proposition.

The issue is therefore not whether one may assign priors to hypotheses. Of course one may. Nor is the issue whether some priors are better justified than others. They may well be. The issue is what grounds the assignment.

Probability theory distinguishes sharply between the existence of a valid measure and the justification for choosing that measure. A proposed intrinsic probability must supply the latter. It must explain how features of a proposition determine a particular measure over the relevant hypothesis space. Without such an account, the phrase “intrinsic probability” risks confusing two questions: \(\text{Is }P\text{ a valid probability measure?}\) and

\[ \text{Why should this particular }P\text{ represent uncertainty about }H? \]

The first is a mathematical question. The second is an epistemological and methodological one.

The central difficulty is that nothing about \(H\) considered in isolation answers the second question. A proposition may be true or false. It may entail or contradict other propositions. It may admit shorter or longer representations. But none of these facts, without some additional probabilistic model, specifies a unique number in the interval \([0,1]\). For this reason, the appropriate starting point is not \(P_{\mathrm{intrinsic}}(H)\), but

\[ P_M(H). \]

The subscript is philosophically significant. It reminds us that the probability assignment is made relative to a model. If the alleged intrinsic probability depends upon that model, then the burden falls on the defender of intrinsic probability to explain in what meaningful sense the resulting probability remains intrinsic to the hypothesis itself.

The measure-theoretic point does not yet show that theoretical virtues are irrelevant. It shows only that an additional bridge is required. The next question is therefore what sort of structure those virtues naturally provide before any probabilistic interpretation is imposed.

3. Theoretical Virtues: Preference, Ranking, and the Cardinality Gap

3.1 Preference Is Not Probability

Suppose that hypotheses can be evaluated according to a catalogue of theoretical virtues. Let \(V=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\}\) represent criteria such as simplicity, coherence, explanatory scope, unification, elegance, conservatism, or absence of ad hoc assumptions. A philosopher might maintain that these features provide rational grounds for preferring one hypothesis to another even before the relevant empirical evidence is considered.

There is nothing obviously objectionable about this claim. Scientific and philosophical reasoning routinely involves judgments that are not reducible to direct empirical fit. Two hypotheses may accommodate the same observations while differing substantially in complexity, unification, or compatibility with established theory. It may therefore be perfectly reasonable to regard one as methodologically preferable to the other. The difficulty begins when this preference is redescribed as probability. Suppose that, according to some set of theoretical virtues, hypothesis \(H_1\) is preferable to hypothesis \(H_2\). We can represent this as \(H_1 \succeq_V H_2\), where \(\succeq_V\) denotes a preference relation induced by the virtue catalogue \(V\).

This relation might mean that \(H_1\) is simpler, more coherent, more theoretically economical, or otherwise more attractive according to the relevant methodological criteria. But nothing follows immediately about probability. In particular,

\[ H_1 \succeq_V H_2 \]

does not entail

\[ P(H_1)>P(H_2). \]

These statements belong to different conceptual categories. The first expresses a comparative evaluation of theories. The second expresses an ordering generated by a probability measure.

The distinction becomes clearer if we consider an analogy with decision theory. Suppose two possible actions, \(a_1\) and \(a_2\), are compared according to several criteria: cost, convenience, expected benefit, risk, and so forth. These criteria may support a preference

\[ a_1 \succ a_2. \]

We might even construct a numerical utility function satisfying

\[ U(a_1)>U(a_2). \]

But it would be a category mistake to infer from this that

\[ P(a_1)>P(a_2). \]

Utility and probability serve different roles. One represents evaluation or preference; the other represents uncertainty.

The same distinction should be preserved when evaluating hypotheses. If theoretical virtues tell us that we prefer \(H_1\) to \(H_2\), the natural interpretation is methodological:

\[ H_1 \succ_V H_2. \]

It does not follow that \(H_1\) is more probable in the probabilistic sense unless an additional principle connects methodological preference to probability of truth.

That additional principle is doing substantive work. To make this explicit, suppose each hypothesis is represented by a vector of theoretical attributes:

\[ v(H)= \begin{pmatrix} v_1(H)\\ v_2(H)\\ \vdots\\ v_n(H) \end{pmatrix}. \]

One might then assign weights

\[ w= \begin{pmatrix} w_1\\ w_2\\ \vdots\\ w_n \end{pmatrix} \]

and construct a scalar score

\[ S(H)=w^\top v(H). \]

This is already a substantial modeling choice. It assumes that the relevant virtues can be represented numerically, that they are sufficiently commensurable to be aggregated, and that the specified weights appropriately represent their relative importance.

Even granting all of this, however, \(S(H)\) is still only a score. If \(S(H_1)>S(H_2)\), we may have constructed a reason to rank \(H_1\) above \(H_2\). We have not yet established

\[ P(H_1)>P(H_2). \]

A further transformation would be required. For example, one might define

\[ P(H_i) = \frac{\exp(S(H_i))} {\sum_j \exp(S(H_j))}. \]

The resulting numbers are non-negative and sum to one. Formally, they can therefore constitute a probability distribution over the hypothesis set.

But this construction immediately reveals the problem. Nothing about normalization explains why the resulting numbers should be interpreted as probabilities that the hypotheses are true.

The same transformation could be applied to virtually any collection of numerical scores. Suppose restaurants are assigned scores according to taste, price, atmosphere, and convenience. We could define

\[ P(R_i) = \frac{\exp(S(R_i))} {\sum_j \exp(S(R_j))}. \]

These values would also satisfy the formal requirements of a probability distribution. Yet no one would infer merely from the normalization procedure that they represent the probabilities that the restaurants are “true.” They remain transformed preference scores.

The general lesson is that

\[ \boxed{ \text{having the mathematical form of a probability distribution} \neq \text{being justified as a probability of truth}. } \]

A normalization procedure can manufacture a probability-shaped object from almost any numerical ordering. What it cannot manufacture is the epistemic interpretation of that object.

The defender of intrinsic probability therefore needs more than a way of converting theoretical virtues into normalized numbers. They need a justification for the bridge principle

\[ \text{greater theoretical virtue} \Longrightarrow \text{greater probability of truth}. \]

That claim is neither a theorem of probability theory nor a logical consequence of the concept of a theoretical virtue. It is itself a substantive epistemological thesis.

This becomes especially important because methodological preference can be rational without being probabilistic. A scientist might prefer one model because it is easier to understand, easier to compute with, more robust under perturbation, or more readily integrated with existing theory. These can all be legitimate reasons for selecting one model over another for particular purposes. But none automatically implies that the selected model has a greater probability of being true.

Indeed, the criteria relevant to theory choice may serve several distinct purposes. Simplicity may aid tractability. Unification may reduce explanatory fragmentation. Conservatism may minimize costly revisions to an established theoretical framework. Elegance may reflect mathematical symmetry or conceptual economy. These features can make a theory preferable as an object of inquiry without determining its truth probability. This distinction is obscured when all rational considerations are forced into the prior term of Bayes’ theorem. Because Bayesian inference contains a quantity \(P(H\mid K)\), there is a temptation to treat every pre-evidential reason for preferring one hypothesis to another as something that must be represented numerically within that prior.

But it does not follow that every epistemic consideration is probabilistic in kind. Some may instead belong to a separate structure of methodological preference:

\[ \succeq_V. \]

Under this interpretation, the theoretical virtues help determine which hypotheses are worth pursuing, comparing, testing, or adopting provisionally. They need not determine a probability measure. The distinction can be represented schematically as \(V(H) \longrightarrow \succeq_V\), rather than

\[ V(H) \longrightarrow P(H). \]

The first mapping is conceptually natural. A set of evaluative criteria generates, or at least influences, a comparative judgment among hypotheses. The second requires another argument entirely.

3.2 Ordinal Preference Does Not Determine Cardinal Probability

There is also an important asymmetry between probability and preference. A probability measure imposes considerable structural constraints. If hypotheses are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, their probabilities must satisfy relations such as

\[ \sum_i P(H_i)=1. \]

A preference relation need not possess anything analogous to this structure. Knowing that

\[ H_1\succ H_2 \] and \(H_2\succ H_3\) does not tell us whether \(H_1\) is twice as preferable as \(H_2\), one hundred times as preferable, or only infinitesimally preferable. Nor does it tell us how the introduction of a fourth hypothesis should alter the numerical standing of the first three. Probability demands cardinal structure that an ordinal ranking does not provide. Even if theoretical virtues produced a complete ordering \(H_1\succ H_2\succ H_3\), this would still leave infinitely many probability distributions compatible with that ordering. For example, all of the following could preserve the same ranking: \[ (0.6,0.3,0.1), \] \((0.4,0.35,0.25)\), or

\[ (0.98,0.015,0.005). \]

The ordinal information alone cannot determine which distribution, if any, is correct.

This exposes an important gap in appeals to intrinsic probability. Theoretical virtues may plausibly tell us that one hypothesis is preferable to another. But a probability measure requires far more information than such a preference relation contains.

The problem becomes still more severe if the theoretical virtues fail to generate a complete ordering at all. If one hypothesis is simpler while another is more unifying, and a third is more coherent with established knowledge, there may be no principled way to rank them without introducing additional weights and tradeoffs. In that case, the appropriate structure may be only a partial order rather than a total ranking.

The attempt to derive a precise probability distribution from such an incomplete evaluative structure therefore introduces information that was not present in the original judgments.

The central point is not that theoretical virtues are irrational or useless. They may be indispensable to scientific reasoning. The point is that their natural role is evaluative rather than probabilistic. They can supply reasons for preferring, pursuing, or provisionally accepting one hypothesis over another without thereby supplying a numerical measure of how likely each hypothesis is to be true.

The burden therefore lies with the defender of intrinsic probability to justify the transition

\[ H_1\succeq_V H_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(H_1)>P(H_2). \]

Until that bridge is supplied, theoretical preference and probability should remain conceptually distinct.

3.3 The Partial-Order Problem

Even if theoretical virtues could be identified clearly, a further difficulty remains: they do not necessarily generate a complete ranking of hypotheses. The reason is that theory evaluation is typically multidimensional. A hypothesis may be simpler than a rival while being less unifying, more coherent with existing knowledge while being less elegant, or more conservative while having narrower explanatory scope. Once several virtues are in play, there is no guarantee that they will all point in the same direction.

Suppose a hypothesis is evaluated according to a vector of theoretical attributes,

\[ V(H)= \begin{pmatrix} S(H)\\ U(H)\\ C(H)\\ E(H) \end{pmatrix}, \]

where \(S\) represents simplicity, \(U\) unification, \(C\) coherence with existing knowledge, and \(E\) elegance. It is entirely possible that one hypothesis, \(H_1\), performs better on simplicity and coherence while another, \(H_2\), performs better on unification and elegance. In such a case, the virtues themselves do not obviously determine which hypothesis should be preferred overall. The natural result may not be a total ordering at all, but a relation of incomparability,

\[ H_1\parallel H_2. \]

This possibility is important because discussions of intrinsic probability often proceed as though every set of theoretical virtues can ultimately be collapsed into a single scale. But that assumption requires justification. Multidimensional evaluation does not automatically imply scalar evaluation. If the virtues are genuinely distinct, then tradeoffs among them may not have determinate resolutions. A theory can be better in one respect and worse in another without there being any fact of the matter about which is “better overall” prior to the introduction of some additional decision rule.

A useful way to capture this is through a Pareto-style relation. We might say that \(H_1\) dominates \(H_2\) when \(H_1\) is at least as good as \(H_2\) on every relevant theoretical virtue and strictly better on at least one. In that special case, there may be a relatively clear methodological basis for preferring \(H_1\). But many interesting comparisons will not have this structure. If

\[ v_i(H_1)>v_i(H_2) \]

for some virtues while

\[ v_j(H_1)<v_j(H_2) \]

for others, then neither hypothesis dominates the other. The result is a partial ordering rather than a complete ranking. That distinction matters because probability imposes much more structure than a partial order does. If one assigns \(P(H_1)=0.6\) and

\[ P(H_2)=0.3, \]

the hypotheses have been decisively ranked. More than that, a cardinal relationship has been imposed between them. Yet the theoretical-virtue structure may have supplied only the information that \(H_1\) is superior on some dimensions and \(H_2\) on others. Moving from that situation to a numerical probability ordering introduces information that was not contained in the original evaluation.

This cannot be solved simply by assigning weights to the virtues. Suppose one defines an overall score by

\[ S(H)= w_1v_1(H)+w_2v_2(H)+\cdots+w_nv_n(H). \]

A complete ranking may now be possible, but only because a new layer of structure has been added. The weights \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\) encode tradeoffs among the virtues. They determine how much simplicity is worth relative to unification, how much coherence compensates for reduced explanatory scope, or how much elegance offsets greater complexity. Nothing in the virtues themselves supplies these exchange rates. The weighting scheme is therefore not a discovery of latent ordering; it is a methodological decision about how competing considerations should be combined.

This point is closely analogous to multi-criteria decision analysis. When alternatives are evaluated along several dimensions, a complete ordering is often available only after one introduces a utility function, weighting system, or aggregation rule. That procedure may be entirely legitimate, but it should not be mistaken for uncovering an ordering that was already inherent in the alternatives. The same is true of hypotheses. If a scalar score emerges only after theoretical virtues have been weighted and aggregated, then the score is relative to the chosen aggregation model. The structure can be represented as \(V(H) \longrightarrow \text{partial order}\), followed, if desired, by

\[ V(H)+w \longrightarrow S(H), \]

and only later by some further transformation

\[ S(H)+f \longrightarrow P(H). \]

Each transition adds assumptions. The first stage gives us multidimensional evaluation. The second imposes tradeoffs among dimensions. The third converts the resulting scalar into something with the formal structure of a probability distribution. By the time a numerical prior has been produced, considerably more has entered the construction than the original theoretical virtues themselves. A simple example makes the underdetermination clear. Suppose the available methodological considerations establish only that \(H_1\succ H_3\) and

\[ H_2\succ H_3, \]

while leaving \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) incomparable:

\[ H_1\parallel H_2. \]

The theoretical virtues therefore tell us that both \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) are preferable to \(H_3\), but not how the first two compare. A probability distribution such as

\[ P(H_1)=0.5,\qquad P(H_2)=0.4,\qquad P(H_3)=0.1 \] adds a ranking between \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) that the original virtue structure did not contain. An alternative assignment, \(P(H_1)=0.4,\qquad P(H_2)=0.5,\qquad P(H_3)=0.1\), would be equally compatible with the partial ordering. So would

\[ P(H_1)=P(H_2)=0.45,\qquad P(H_3)=0.1. \]

The initial evaluative information cannot select among these distributions.

The problem remains even if the theoretical virtues happen to generate a complete ordinal ranking. Suppose one obtains

\[ H_1\succ H_2\succ H_3. \]

That ranking still fails to determine a probability distribution. The assignments \[ (0.6,0.3,0.1), \] \((0.4,0.35,0.25)\), and

\[ (0.98,0.015,0.005) \]

all preserve exactly the same order. The ordinal relation tells us which hypothesis is ranked above which, but not how much more probability one should receive than another. Probability requires cardinal structure that ordinary preference does not provide.

This distinction is especially important when precise numerical priors are presented as though they reflected equally precise epistemic judgments. If the underlying basis for preferring \(H_1\) to \(H_2\) is merely that the former appears somewhat simpler or more unified, then assigning

\[ P(H_1)=0.72 \]

can create an appearance of measurement that the original judgment does not support. The numerical precision may come entirely from the modeling convention used to transform rankings into a distribution rather than from any precise epistemic information about the hypotheses themselves.

The partial-order perspective therefore provides a more modest and, in many cases, more accurate account of what theoretical virtues can accomplish. They may establish clear preference in some cases and leave genuine incomparability in others. We may be justified in writing

\[ H_1\succ_V H_2 \]

when one hypothesis is superior according to the relevant criteria, while allowing

\[ H_1\parallel_V H_2 \]

when the virtues pull in conflicting directions. Nothing requires every theoretical comparison to terminate in a unique scalar value.

This is consequential for intrinsic probability because a probability measure cannot be recovered from a partial order without additional assumptions. If the original theoretical virtues fail even to produce a complete ranking, then a complete numerical distribution must obtain its additional structure from somewhere else. Weighting rules, aggregation procedures, tradeoff functions, and normalization conventions must be introduced to resolve what the virtue structure itself leaves unresolved.

The resulting probability is therefore not intrinsic to the hypothesis. It is the output of a broader evaluative model that determines how multidimensional theoretical considerations are to be compared and combined. The more seriously the partial-order problem is taken, the harder it becomes to maintain that theoretical virtues reveal a pre-existing probability of a hypothesis. At best, they provide incomplete methodological guidance; any further transition to a numerical prior depends on assumptions external to the hypotheses under evaluation.

The preceding argument grants, for the sake of analysis, that the relevant virtues can be identified and compared. That assumption now needs examination. Before theoretical virtues can be aggregated, ranked, or transformed into probabilities, we must know what the virtues themselves amount to.

4. Operationalizing Theoretical Virtues

4.1 What Does “Simplicity” Mean?

Even if one grants that theoretical virtues can legitimately guide the evaluation of hypotheses, a more basic problem arises before any attempt is made to translate those virtues into probabilities: the virtues themselves are often vague, heterogeneous, and context-dependent. Terms such as simplicity, elegance, coherence, explanatory scope, and unification are frequently invoked as though they referred to well-defined properties, but closer inspection shows that each can be understood in several importantly different ways. If these virtues are supposed to play a quantitative role in assigning prior probability, then one must first explain what exactly is being measured.

Consider simplicity. To say that one theory is “simpler” than another may mean that it contains fewer free parameters, invokes fewer entities, relies on fewer auxiliary assumptions, has a shorter mathematical formulation, possesses lower computational complexity, or can be encoded more compactly. It may also mean that the theory integrates more easily with an existing body of knowledge and requires less revision of the surrounding theoretical structure. These different notions of simplicity need not coincide. A theory with a very compact equation may be computationally intractable. Another may contain many explicit parameters but derive them from a highly unified mechanism. A third may be ontologically sparse but require a large number of auxiliary assumptions before it can generate predictions. Thus, even before asking whether simplicity should affect probability, one must ask which kind of simplicity is meant.

We might formalize the point by distinguishing several candidate measures:

\[ S_1(H)=\text{parameter simplicity}, \]

\[ S_2(H)=\text{syntactic simplicity}, \] \[ S_3(H)=\text{ontological simplicity}, \] \(S_4(H)=\text{computational simplicity}\), and perhaps

\[ S_5(H,K)=\text{simplicity relative to background knowledge}. \]

There is no reason to expect these measures to rank hypotheses in the same way. A hypothesis may score highly under one and poorly under another. If so, the claim that the hypothesis is “intrinsically simpler” is already ambiguous unless some prior decision has been made about which conception of simplicity is relevant. The supposed virtue therefore cannot simply be read off from the hypothesis. It depends upon a choice of metric.

The dependence becomes even clearer when simplicity is understood in terms of how easily a new hypothesis fits into an existing body of knowledge. Suppose one theory can be incorporated into the current theoretical framework with only minor adjustments, while another requires extensive revision of the surrounding system of beliefs. It is tempting to describe the first as simpler or more coherent. But this kind of simplicity is plainly relational. It is not merely a function of the hypothesis,

\[ S(H), \]

but of the hypothesis relative to some background structure,

\[ S(H\mid K). \]

A hypothesis might fit naturally within one body of knowledge and disrupt another:

\[ S(H\mid K_1)\neq S(H\mid K_2). \]

Nothing about the hypothesis itself has changed. What has changed is the theoretical environment in which it is being assessed. This matters directly for intrinsic probability, because any probability derived from such a virtue inherits the same dependence. If the simplicity of \(H\) depends on \(K\), then a simplicity-based prior cannot plausibly be regarded as intrinsic to \(H\) alone.

There is also a further methodological issue here. A preference for hypotheses that require minimal revision of existing knowledge may reflect a norm of epistemic conservatism rather than a truth-directed probabilistic principle. Conservatism can certainly be rational. It can prevent unnecessary theoretical upheaval and reduce the risk of abandoning well-supported structures too readily. But a theory’s compatibility with existing knowledge does not, by itself, show that it is more likely to be true. Existing knowledge may itself be incomplete or mistaken. A genuinely successful scientific theory may require radical revision precisely because the framework it replaces was deeply flawed. To assign greater probability to theories merely because they preserve current structure is therefore to build a substantive methodological commitment into the prior. It is not to discover an intrinsic probability already present in the hypothesis.

4.2 Elegance and Other Cluster Virtues

The problem is even more severe with a virtue such as elegance. Scientists and philosophers often use the term meaningfully, but it is difficult to specify what would count as a measurable degree of elegance. One theory may be called elegant because of its symmetry, another because of its economy of expression, another because it unifies previously separate phenomena, and another because it eliminates arbitrary constants or auxiliary assumptions. If elegance is really a cluster concept of this sort, then a formal representation might look something like

\[ E(H) = F\big( S(H), U(H), Y(H), N(H), \ldots \big), \]

where \(S\) represents simplicity, \(U\) unification, \(Y\) symmetry, and \(N\) non-arbitrariness. Yet this does not solve the problem. It merely relocates it. One must still determine what counts as symmetry or unification, whether these quantities are measurable on common scales, and how conflicts among them should be resolved.

Suppose one theory is highly symmetrical but requires several arbitrary constants, while another is less symmetrical but explains those constants through a deeper mechanism. Which theory is more elegant? There is no obvious answer unless a weighting scheme has already been imposed. One might try to define

\[ E(H) = w_SS(H) + w_UU(H) + w_YY(H) + w_NN(H), \]

but then the weights themselves become part of the theory. Why should symmetry receive one weight and unification another? Why should the elimination of arbitrary constants count more or less than mathematical compactness? The resulting elegance score is not simply observed; it is constructed from a set of methodological decisions. This makes the later move from elegance to probability even more difficult, because the quantity being mapped into probability is already model-dependent.

The same general problem applies to other theoretical virtues. Explanatory scope may conflict with precision: a very broad theory may explain many phenomena while making only weak predictions, whereas a narrower theory may be far more exact. Coherence may refer to logical consistency, compatibility with established theories, inferential integration with a wider network of beliefs, or conceptual unity. Unification may mean deriving many phenomena from a common mechanism, reducing the number of independent assumptions, or embedding several theories within a larger formal structure. These are not trivial distinctions. They show that many alleged virtues are not single variables at all, but families of related evaluative criteria whose meaning depends upon context.

4.3 From Operationalization to Quantification

If such virtues are to enter into a quantitative model, one eventually needs functions of the form \(v_i:H\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) or, where background information matters,

\[ v_i:(H,K)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}. \]

Constructing these functions requires decisions about representation, scale, comparison class, and measurement procedure. Different operationalizations can generate different rankings. Suppose one researcher measures simplicity by parameter count,

\[ S_A(H)=-k(H), \]

while another measures it by description length,

\[ S_B(H)=-L(H). \] There is no guarantee that \(S_A(H_1)>S_A(H_2)\) will imply

\[ S_B(H_1)>S_B(H_2). \]

The same pair of hypotheses can therefore be ranked differently depending on how the virtue is defined. If those rankings are then converted into priors, the resulting probability disagreement is inherited from the prior methodological choice rather than discovered through probability theory.

The full structure therefore looks less like a direct mapping from hypothesis to probability and more like a chain of modeling decisions:

\[ H \longrightarrow \text{representation} \longrightarrow v(H) \longrightarrow S(H) \longrightarrow P(H). \]

Each arrow introduces assumptions that require independent justification. First, one must decide what the relevant virtue is. Then one must operationalize it. If several virtues are involved, one must determine whether they are commensurable and how they should be aggregated. Finally, one must explain why the resulting evaluation should be interpreted probabilistically at all. The more ambiguous the original virtue, the more assumptions are required before a numerical probability can emerge.

This sequence can be summarized as

\[ \boxed{ \text{What is the virtue?} \rightarrow \text{How is it measured?} \rightarrow \text{How is it aggregated?} \rightarrow \text{Why should it determine probability?} } \]

The concept of intrinsic probability tends to obscure these intermediate steps by presenting the resulting number as though it were an antecedent property of the hypothesis. Once the full chain is made explicit, however, the probability looks less like something discovered in \(H\) and more like the output of an elaborate methodological procedure.

None of this implies that theoretical virtues are meaningless or useless. Simplicity, elegance, coherence, and unification may play indispensable roles in scientific reasoning. The point is that their usefulness does not require them to be numerical probabilities. They may function as methodological norms, heuristic guides, or dimensions along which competing theories are compared. A scientist may reasonably judge one theory to be more elegant or more unified than another without claiming to have measured a cardinal quantity or determined a corresponding probability of truth. The difficulty arises only when such judgments are treated as though they supplied a determinate prior probability. If the virtues themselves are representation-dependent, relational, multidimensional, and operationally underdetermined, then a probability constructed from them cannot plausibly be described as intrinsic to the hypothesis itself.

Operationalization introduces a further issue. Even a precisely defined virtue may fail to be intrinsic to the hypothesis if its value changes with the language, background theory, or set of alternatives used to represent the problem. The next section makes that dependence explicit.

5. Representation and Background Dependence

5.1 Language and Coding Dependence

The preceding argument can be sharpened by focusing directly on the word intrinsic. If a probability is genuinely intrinsic to a hypothesis, then it should in some important sense belong to that hypothesis independently of arbitrary choices about how the hypothesis is represented, encoded, or situated within a larger conceptual framework. Yet many of the considerations used to motivate intrinsic probability are plainly sensitive to precisely these factors.

Simplicity provides the clearest example. A theory may be concise in one formal language and cumbersome in another. Its apparent economy can depend on which concepts are treated as primitive, which operations are built into the language, and which background results are assumed rather than explicitly derived. A hypothesis that requires a long formulation under one representational system may become compact under another simply because the second system has been designed around the relevant structure. This means that what appears to be a property of the hypothesis may actually be a property of the pair consisting of the hypothesis and its representation. Instead of writing \(S(H)\), the more accurate notation may be

\[ S(H\mid L), \]

where \(L\) denotes a language, coding system, or representational framework. If \(S(H\mid L_1)\neq S(H\mid L_2)\), then a simplicity-based prior will generally inherit that dependence:

\[ P(H\mid L_1)\neq P(H\mid L_2). \]

The truth conditions of \(H\) may remain unchanged throughout. What changes is only the language in which \(H\) is encoded or assessed.

This creates an immediate tension with the claim that the resulting probability is intrinsic. An intrinsic property should not fluctuate merely because the same proposition is redescribed in a different vocabulary. If the alleged probability changes when the representational language changes, then the source of the probability cannot lie solely in the proposition itself.

5.2 Background Knowledge

The problem is not limited to syntactic representation. Background knowledge can have the same effect. A hypothesis that initially appears complex may become extremely simple once a relevant theoretical framework is already in place. Conversely, a hypothesis that looks compact in isolation may require extensive auxiliary machinery once its relation to established knowledge is taken seriously. This is naturally represented by a conditional measure such as \(S(H\mid K)\), or, in the algorithmic case,

\[ K_U(H\mid K). \]

The quantity now explicitly depends on background information \(K\). If the prior is derived from that quantity, then the prior depends on \(K\) as well.

The same hypothesis can therefore receive different prior assignments under different bodies of background knowledge:

\[ P(H\mid K_1)\neq P(H\mid K_2). \]

Again, the hypothesis has not changed. The surrounding informational structure has.

This suggests that many so-called intrinsic virtues are actually relational virtues. Coherence is coherence with something. Conservatism is conservatism relative to an existing body of belief. Explanatory integration is integration with an already structured body of theory. Even simplicity may be simplicity relative to a language, coding scheme, or stock of accepted primitives.

Once these dependencies are made explicit, the term intrinsic becomes increasingly difficult to defend.

5.3 Hypothesis-Space Dependence

There is another form of representation dependence at the level of the hypothesis space itself. A prior distribution is not assigned to isolated propositions in a vacuum. It is defined over some set or algebra of alternatives. How that set is constructed can affect the resulting probabilities.

Suppose we begin with a hypothesis space

\[ \mathcal{H}_1 = \{H_1,H_2\}. \]

Now suppose that \(H_2\) is decomposed into a family of more specific alternatives:

\[ \mathcal{H}_2 = \{H_1,H_{2,1},H_{2,2},\ldots,H_{2,n}\}. \]

Depending on the prior construction, the probability assigned to \(H_1\) may change because the surrounding space has changed. The proposition \(H_1\) itself is exactly the same in both cases. What differs is the way the alternatives have been represented and partitioned.

This matters because probability is inherently measure-theoretic and therefore relational to a space of events. A probability assignment is not ordinarily a unary property attached to a proposition independently of the other events in the model. It is part of a measure defined across a structured domain. That fact is unproblematic for ordinary probability theory, but it is problematic for the rhetoric of intrinsic probability. If the value of \(P(H_1)\) depends on how neighboring hypotheses are individuated, grouped, or refined, then there is little sense in which that number belongs to \(H_1\) alone.

5.4 Equivalent Formulations and the Unit of Evaluation

The same issue appears when one considers equivalent formulations of a theory. Suppose two formulations, \(H\) and

\[ H', \]

are logically or empirically equivalent but differ greatly in syntactic form. One may be highly compressed, while the other is verbose and cumbersome. If a prior rewards description length directly, then it may assign

\[ P(H)>P(H'). \]

Yet if the two formulations express the same underlying hypothesis, then this difference is hard to interpret as a difference in the probability of truth. At most, it is a difference in the probability assigned to two representations.

A defender might respond that the relevant unit of evaluation is not the sentence or formulation but the proposition or theory itself. But this simply relocates the problem. One must then identify a representation-independent measure of simplicity, elegance, or coherence at the level of propositions. That is precisely what has proven difficult. Any concrete formalization typically requires some language, coding convention, ontology, or set of primitives. The problem can be expressed schematically as \(H \longrightarrow R(H) \longrightarrow V(R(H)) \longrightarrow P(H)\), where \(R(H)\) is a chosen representation of the hypothesis and \(V\) evaluates that representation according to some theoretical virtue. If a different representation \(R'(H)\) yields a different evaluation, \(V(R(H))\neq V(R'(H))\), then the resulting probability assignment is representation-sensitive.

To call such a quantity intrinsic is to ignore the machinery through which the quantity was generated.

The point is not that representation dependence is always illegitimate. Many useful scientific quantities depend on conventions, coordinate systems, parameterizations, or modeling choices. Such dependence may be harmless when it is understood and controlled.

The problem is instead conceptual: a convention-dependent or model-relative quantity should be described as such. If a prior is defined relative to a language \(L\), a background theory \(K\), and a hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\), then the appropriate notation is something like

\[ P(H\mid L,K,\mathcal{H},M), \]

not

\[ P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H). \]

The former makes the dependencies explicit. The latter suppresses them.

This distinction becomes particularly important when intrinsic probability is used to suggest that a hypothesis has some antecedent probability merely by virtue of what it is. If that were the case, one would expect a stable assignment across representational frameworks that preserve the same substantive content. But the mechanisms typically invoked to generate such priors do not provide this stability. They depend on descriptions, coding choices, background assumptions, and the structure of the hypothesis space.

At this point, the term intrinsic risks doing rhetorical rather than analytical work. It gives the impression that the probability belongs to the hypothesis independently of external considerations, while the actual construction reveals extensive dependence on those very considerations.

The more accurate conclusion is that what is being called intrinsic probability is usually a prior generated under a particular representation and methodological framework. Such a prior may be perfectly coherent. It may even be useful or well motivated. But its dependence on external structure prevents it from being intrinsic in any strong sense.

The issue can therefore be stated simply:

\[ \boxed{ \text{If the probability changes when the representation or background changes, then the probability is not intrinsic to }H. } \]

This does not show that representation-sensitive priors are invalid. It shows only that they should be understood for what they are: model-relative probability assignments rather than inherent properties of propositions.

A defender might now object that these difficulties mainly afflict informal virtues such as elegance or explanatory simplicity. Algorithmic information theory supplies a much more precise notion of complexity, so it provides the strongest test of the argument developed so far.

6. Kolmogorov Complexity: The Strongest Case Still Falls Short

6.1 Why Algorithmic Complexity Is the Strongest Case

A defender of intrinsic probability might respond that the preceding objections rely too heavily on vague notions such as elegance or explanatory simplicity. Perhaps some theoretical virtues are indeed difficult to operationalize, but simplicity need not always remain informal. Algorithmic information theory provides a precise way of defining complexity, and therefore seems to offer a much stronger basis for assigning prior probabilities to hypotheses.

The most obvious candidate is Kolmogorov complexity. Relative to a universal Turing machine \(U\), the Kolmogorov complexity of an object \(x\) is the length of the shortest program that causes \(U\) to output \(x\):

\[ K_U(x) = \min_{p:U(p)=x}|p|. \]

If a hypothesis \(H\) can be represented as a finite object, then one can in principle compare hypotheses according to the length of their shortest descriptions. A hypothesis requiring a shorter program is, in this technical sense, simpler than one requiring a longer program. Unlike ordinary appeals to elegance or conceptual economy, this notion of simplicity is formally defined.

It is then natural to construct a prior that penalizes complexity. A familiar form is

\[ P(H)\propto 2^{-K_U(H)}. \]

Under such a rule, hypotheses with shorter minimal descriptions receive greater prior weight. If

\[ K_U(H_1)=K_U(H_2)+10, \]

then the prior odds satisfy approximately

\[ \frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_2)} = 2^{-10}. \]

The hypothesis requiring ten additional bits of description receives roughly one-thousandth the prior weight of its shorter rival. Here, unlike with vague talk of elegance, there is at least an explicit mapping from a formal property of the hypothesis to a numerical probability assignment.

This is a much stronger case than simply saying that simpler theories are more plausible. Yet it still does not establish the existence of intrinsic probability.

6.2 Reference-Machine Dependence

The first reason is that the complexity value itself is not intrinsic in the relevant sense. Kolmogorov complexity is defined relative to a reference machine. The correct notation is not simply \(K(H)\), but

\[ K_U(H). \] Different universal Turing machines can assign different description lengths to the same object. The invariance theorem guarantees that for two universal machines \(U\) and \(V\), \(|K_U(H)-K_V(H)|\leq c_{UV}\), where \(c_{UV}\) is a constant independent of \(H\). This result is important because it shows that differences in complexity become less significant asymptotically for sufficiently large objects. But it does not make the complexity of an individual finite hypothesis uniquely defined. For concrete hypotheses, the additive constant can matter, and under an exponential prior such differences can generate substantial changes in probability. If the prior is \(P_U(H)\propto 2^{-K_U(H)}\), then changing the reference machine can change the relative prior weight assigned to the same hypothesis. The proposition itself has not changed, but the quantity supposedly representing its intrinsic probability has. This already suggests that the more accurate notation is \(P(H\mid U)\), or more generally

\[ P(H\mid U,M), \]

where \(M\) includes the assumptions connecting algorithmic complexity to prior probability. Once again, what initially appears as a property of \(H\) turns out to depend on an external representational framework.

6.3 From Complexity to Prior Weight

There is also a deeper issue. Even if one fixes the reference machine and accepts \(K_U(H)\) as a legitimate measure of complexity, it does not follow that complexity should determine probability according to the rule

\[ P(H)\propto 2^{-K_U(H)}. \]

The transformation from complexity to probability is itself an additional assumption. One could instead define

\[ P(H)\propto e^{-\lambda K_U(H)} \]

for some parameter \(\lambda>0\), or choose a weaker penalty such as

\[ P(H)\propto \frac{1}{(1+K_U(H))^\alpha}. \]

Each construction favors simpler hypotheses, but they produce different quantitative distributions. Probability theory does not decide among them merely from the existence of the complexity measure.

6.4 Algorithmic Probability and the Generative Model

The standard exponential form has a stronger motivation than an arbitrary weighting rule. For prefix-free binary programs, a program of length \(n\) can naturally be assigned weight \(2^{-n}\), and Kraft’s inequality ensures that the total weight assigned to a prefix-free set of programs does not exceed one:

\[ \sum_p 2^{-|p|} \leq 1. \]

This makes possible a genuine algorithmic probability construction. Instead of considering only the shortest program for an output, one can sum over all programs that generate it. Schematically,

\[ M(x) = \sum_{p:U(p)=x*} 2^{-|p|}, \]

where the sum ranges over programs whose output begins with \(x\).

This construction is important because it shows that short descriptions can arise naturally from an explicitly specified probabilistic process. If programs are generated by random binary input, shorter programs receive more probability mass simply because fewer bits must be specified.

But this changes the character of the argument. The probability is no longer emerging from simplicity alone. It emerges from a stochastic generative model. The relevant structure is not \(\text{simplicity} \longrightarrow \text{probability}\), but rather

\[ \text{random program-generation process} \longrightarrow \text{probability measure} \longrightarrow \text{greater mass on short descriptions}. \]

That is a legitimate probabilistic construction. Yet it immediately raises a philosophical question that probability theory itself cannot answer: why should a process in which random bits are fed into a particular universal machine represent our uncertainty about which hypotheses are true of the world?

The generative process defines a measure, but defining a measure is not the same as justifying its interpretation as a probability of truth.

This distinction is central. Suppose a universal machine and random-bit process generate a well-defined distribution over programs and hence over hypotheses. One has then shown that, conditional on that generative setup, certain hypotheses receive greater probability mass. What has been established is something like

\[ P(H\mid U,G), \]

where \(G\) denotes the chosen generative process.

What has not been established is

\[ P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H). \]

The former is explicitly conditional on a model. The latter suggests that the probability belongs to the hypothesis independently of such a model.

The difference can be made clearer with an analogy. Suppose a random procedure generates English sentences by selecting words according to a particular distribution. Some sentences will be far more probable than others under that process. But the probability assigned by the generator is a property of the sentence relative to the generator, not an intrinsic probability possessed by the sentence itself. A different generative mechanism would produce a different distribution over the same sentences.

Likewise, algorithmic probability tells us how much measure a hypothesis receives under a particular coding and generation scheme. It does not show that the hypothesis carries that probability independently of the scheme.

6.5 Conditional Complexity and Background Knowledge

There is an additional representational difficulty. A hypothesis can often be encoded in many different ways. The apparent complexity of a theory may change depending on which primitives are built into the description language, which background information is assumed, and which structures are treated as basic rather than derived. In some cases, a more appropriate quantity may therefore be conditional Kolmogorov complexity,

\[ K_U(H\mid K), \]

which measures the length of the shortest program required to generate \(H\) given background information \(K\).

This is conceptually interesting because it captures one notion of how naturally a hypothesis fits with what is already known. A hypothesis may be complex in isolation but simple relative to a rich background theory. Conversely, a hypothesis that looks concise on its own may require substantial additional machinery once its relation to established knowledge is made explicit. But this only reinforces the central criticism. If the relevant complexity is \(K_U(H\mid K)\), then the simplicity of the hypothesis is explicitly conditional on both a reference machine and a body of background information. Any prior constructed from that complexity is correspondingly conditional:

\[ P(H\mid U,K,M). \]

The resulting measure may be useful, rigorous, and even rationally defensible. What it is not is intrinsic to the proposition \(H\).

Kolmogorov complexity is therefore an important limiting case for the present argument. It shows that at least one notion of simplicity can be made precise enough to support a formally defined prior. This should be conceded. The objection to intrinsic probability is not that theoretical virtues can never be formalized or that they can never be used in prior construction.

The objection is instead that successful formalization reveals exactly why the resulting probability is not intrinsic.

Once the machinery is written out, the probability depends on the choice of reference machine, encoding conventions, background information, and a rule connecting description length to probability mass. The structure is therefore better represented as

\[ (U,K,M,H) \longrightarrow P_{U,K,M}(H) \]

than as

\[ H \longrightarrow P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H). \]

In this sense, Kolmogorov complexity does not rescue intrinsic probability. It clarifies the distinction between constructing a probability measure from a formal notion of complexity and discovering a probability already contained within the hypothesis. The former is possible. The latter has still not been established.

The preceding sections have identified the dependencies one at a time: a measure must be chosen; virtues must be defined; multidimensional judgments must be aggregated; representations and backgrounds matter; and even algorithmic simplicity depends on a coding and generative framework. We can now collect those dependencies into a single meta-theoretical picture.

7. The Hidden Meta-Theory Behind Intrinsic Probability

Dependency Map

The framework can be read as a list of distinct questions that are often compressed into the notation \(P(H)\):

Symbol / component Question it answers
\(K\) What background information or assumptions are already in place?
\(L\) In what language, coding scheme, or representation is the hypothesis expressed?
\(V\) Which theoretical virtues count as relevant?
\(w\) How are conflicts or tradeoffs among those virtues weighted?
\(f\) How are evaluations transformed into numerical weights or probabilities?
\(\mathcal H\) Over what hypothesis space is probability mass distributed?
\(M\) What methodological theory licenses the construction as a whole?

This table does not add a new objection; it makes explicit the structure accumulated in the preceding sections.

7.1 The Full Dependency Structure

The preceding discussion suggests that the notion of intrinsic probability conceals a second-order theory about how hypotheses are to be evaluated. Once that theory is made explicit, the probability assigned to a hypothesis no longer appears to arise from the hypothesis itself. It arises from a methodological framework that specifies which features matter, how those features are measured, and how they are converted into prior probability.

Suppose a hypothesis \(H\) is evaluated according to a set of theoretical virtues. A general prior construction might take the form

\[ P(H\mid M) = \frac{\exp[-C_M(H)]} {\sum_{H'}\exp[-C_M(H')]}, \]

where \(C_M(H)\) is some methodological cost assigned to \(H\) under a framework \(M\). That cost might incorporate simplicity, lack of ad hoc assumptions, explanatory scope, coherence, elegance, or any other property thought to be epistemically relevant.

The notation \(M\) is doing substantial work. It represents a collection of assumptions about theory choice. Among other things, \(M\) must determine which virtues are relevant, what each virtue means, how each is operationalized, how conflicts among virtues are resolved, how the virtues are weighted, how the hypothesis space is partitioned, and how the resulting evaluation is transformed into probability mass. The resulting prior is therefore not simply a function of \(H\). It is more accurately represented as something like \(P(H\mid M,K,L,V,w,f,\mathcal{H})\), where \(K\) denotes background knowledge, \(L\) a representational language or coding scheme, \(V\) the selected catalogue of virtues, \(w\) a set of weights, \(f\) an aggregation or transformation rule, and \(\mathcal{H}\) the relevant hypothesis space. This expression is intentionally cumbersome. Its purpose is to expose the structure hidden by the simpler notation

\[ P(H). \]

Once those dependencies are visible, the language of intrinsic probability becomes difficult to sustain.

The crucial point is that the probability assignment is being generated by a meta-theory. The object-level hypothesis \(H\) makes a claim about the world. The methodological framework \(M\) makes claims about how hypotheses like \(H\) should be assessed. These are distinct levels of analysis. A hypothesis might state a physical law, a historical claim, or an explanation of some phenomenon. The meta-theory specifies whether simplicity should count in its favor, whether compatibility with existing knowledge should be rewarded, whether complexity should be penalized exponentially or linearly, and how much weight should be placed on explanatory scope relative to elegance or coherence. The structure is therefore better represented as \(M \longrightarrow P_M \longrightarrow P_M(H)\), rather than

\[ H \longrightarrow P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H). \]

This difference is conceptually significant. Under the first representation, the probability depends on a prior methodological framework. Under the second, the probability appears to belong to the hypothesis in virtue of its own character. The former is intelligible. The latter is what requires defense.

The problem becomes especially clear when two methodological frameworks assign different priors to the same hypothesis:

\[ P(H\mid M_1)\neq P(H\mid M_2). \]

Suppose \(M_1\) strongly rewards simplicity while \(M_2\) places greater weight on explanatory scope. A hypothesis that is simple but narrow may receive a high prior under \(M_1\) and a lower prior under \(M_2\). The hypothesis itself has not changed. What changed was the evaluative framework.

If probability were genuinely intrinsic to \(H\), such dependence would be difficult to explain. A property intrinsic to the hypothesis should not vary merely because an external methodological framework changes. Yet virtue-based priors do exactly that.

7.2 Objectivity Is Not Intrinsicness

The defender of intrinsic probability might respond that one of these meta-theories is objectively correct. Perhaps there really is a uniquely rational way to weight simplicity, elegance, unification, and other virtues. If so, then the resulting prior might be objective even if it is mediated by a methodological framework.

But this response does not restore intrinsicness. Even if there were a uniquely correct \(M\), the probability would still be conditional on that framework. It would be objective relative to a true methodological theory, not intrinsic to the proposition in isolation.

This distinction is important because objectivity and intrinsicness are not the same thing. A quantity can be objectively determined by a relation without being intrinsic to either of the relata. Distance, for example, may be objectively determined between two points while not being an intrinsic property of either point alone. Likewise, even if there were an objectively correct method for assigning priors, the resulting probability could remain relational:

\[ P(H\mid M^\ast). \]

The claim that \(M^\ast\) is uniquely rational would therefore need to be defended independently.

7.3 The Regress of Methodological Justification

This generates a regress of justification, or at least a hierarchy of commitments. If the prior assigned to \(H\) depends on \(M\), then one may reasonably ask why \(M\) should be accepted. Perhaps \(M\) is itself defended because it is simple, coherent, elegant, or successful. But if those same virtues are used to justify the framework that assigns virtue-based probabilities, the reasoning risks becoming circular.

Suppose simplicity is used to justify a simplicity-weighted prior:

\[ \text{simplicity is epistemically privileged} \Longrightarrow P(H)\propto e^{-\lambda S(H)}. \]

The obvious question is why simplicity should be epistemically privileged. If the answer is that simpler methodological principles are themselves more plausible, then the original assumption has simply been reapplied at a higher level. One has not independently justified the role of simplicity; one has presupposed it in the justification.

This does not necessarily produce an infinite regress. A theorist may simply treat some methodological principles as primitive. But if that is the position, then the resulting probabilities should be described accordingly. They are priors generated under a set of primitive methodological commitments. They are not probabilities intrinsic to the hypotheses themselves.

7.4 Prior to Evidence Is Not Prior to Assumptions

The meta-theoretical dependence also reveals an important sense in which intrinsic probability merely shifts conditioning rather than eliminating it. Ordinary Bayesian updating is represented as \(P(H\mid E,K)\), where \(E\) is evidence and \(K\) is background knowledge. The appeal to intrinsic probability attempts to identify a prior that exists before \(E\) is incorporated. But once the methodological framework is made explicit, the prior becomes

\[ P(H\mid K,M). \]

The probability is prior to \(E\), but it is not prior to assumptions. This distinction is crucial. “Prior probability” means prior relative to some specified evidence. It does not mean probability free from all conditioning structure. The error of intrinsic-probability language is to slide from

\[ \text{prior to }E \]

to

\[ \text{intrinsic to }H. \]

Those claims are not equivalent. A hypothesis can have a prior probability under a model without possessing a probability independently of that model. Once this is recognized, the need for the adjective “intrinsic” becomes unclear. The quantity in question is simply a prior defined under a set of background assumptions.

7.5 Dependence on the Hypothesis Space

The dependence on the hypothesis space makes the same point from another direction. Suppose a prior is defined over a set

\[ \mathcal{H}_1 = \{H_1,H_2\}. \]

Now suppose that \(H_2\) is refined into many sub-hypotheses:

\[ \mathcal{H}_2 = \{H_1,H_{2,1},H_{2,2},\ldots,H_{2,n}\}. \]

The way prior mass is distributed may change substantially depending on how the hypothesis space is partitioned. Yet the proposition \(H_1\) may remain exactly the same.

If the probability assigned to \(H_1\) changes because the surrounding hypothesis space changes, then the probability cannot plausibly be intrinsic to \(H_1\) alone. It depends on how alternatives are represented and grouped.

This dependence is familiar in probabilistic modeling, but it sits uneasily with intrinsic-probability language. A model-relative prior is expected to depend on the space over which it is defined. An intrinsic property is not.

The hidden meta-theory argument therefore brings together the earlier objections. Theoretical virtues do not directly yield probability. At most they provide evaluative structure. To transform that structure into a prior, one must supply a methodology that specifies the relevant virtues, their interpretation, their aggregation, their weighting, and the mapping from evaluation to probability. The resulting quantity is not \(P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H)\), but

\[ P_M(H). \]

This does not make the quantity illegitimate. A methodological prior may be useful, coherent, and even well justified. But its legitimacy depends on the defensibility of \(M\). Once that dependence is acknowledged, there is no reason to treat the probability as something intrinsic to the hypothesis.

The distinction is therefore not merely semantic. It marks the difference between a probability that is generated by an evaluative framework and a probability that supposedly belongs to a proposition in virtue of its own content. The former is a familiar feature of probabilistic modeling. The latter remains unsupported.

Once the hidden methodological structure is visible, the most natural objection is Bayesian: whatever its source, inference still requires priors. The issue is therefore whether the necessity or rational defensibility of priors restores the stronger claim of intrinsicness.

8. Objection: Bayesian Reasoning Requires Priors

8.1 Requiring a Prior Does Not Make It Intrinsic

A natural objection is that Bayesian reasoning cannot proceed without prior probabilities. If inference requires a prior over hypotheses, and if theoretical virtues are among the considerations used to construct that prior, then perhaps the notion of intrinsic probability is simply a name for a necessary feature of rational inference. On this view, the criticism developed so far may seem to demand an impossible neutrality: if evidence alone cannot determine posterior probabilities, then some pre-evidential structure must enter the analysis, and theoretical virtues may be precisely what supply it.

There is an important truth in this objection, but it does not establish intrinsic probability. The need for priors does not imply that hypotheses possess probabilities independently of the models under which those priors are assigned. A Bayesian framework may require a prior distribution, but this is a claim about the structure of the inferential model, not about an intrinsic property of the hypotheses themselves. The distinction can be stated directly. From \(\text{Bayesian inference requires a prior}\) it does not follow that

\[ \text{theoretical virtues uniquely determine that prior}, \]

and even if that were true, it would still not follow that

\[ \text{the resulting prior is intrinsic to }H. \]

These are three separate claims, and the validity of one does not entail the others.

Within a Bayesian framework, the posterior is determined by both likelihood and prior:

\[ P(H\mid E,K) \propto P(E\mid H,K)P(H\mid K). \]

The fact that \(P(H\mid K)\) must be specified does not tell us how it should be specified. One may use symmetry arguments, previous data, hierarchical models, domain knowledge, regularization considerations, reference priors, or other methods. Theoretical virtues may enter among these considerations, but if they do, they do so as part of the model-building procedure.

The most that follows is that some methodological framework \(M\) helps determine the prior:

\[ M \longrightarrow P_M(H). \]

This is entirely compatible with the criticism of intrinsic probability. Indeed, it reinforces it. The prior is now explicitly relative to a model of rational or methodological judgment.

8.2 Truth-Conduciveness and Meta-Evidence

The defender might insist that some priors are more rational than others and that theoretical virtues provide the rational constraints. Perhaps simpler hypotheses should receive greater initial probability because simplicity is truth-conducive; perhaps more unified theories should receive greater weight because unification has historically correlated with successful scientific theories. If such claims can be defended, then they may indeed justify a structured prior.

But even here, the relevant justification would itself rely on further premises. If simplicity is regarded as truth-conducive because simpler theories have historically generalized better, then that historical pattern is evidence. It becomes part of the background knowledge \(K\). The prior is then informed by empirical facts about the performance of certain classes of hypotheses:

\[ P(H\mid K). \]

This is no longer an evidence-independent probability intrinsic to \(H\). It is a prior conditional on meta-level evidence about the reliability of a methodological criterion.

Likewise, if a simplicity preference is justified through statistical learning theory, predictive performance, coding efficiency, or some other formal consideration, the justification depends on assumptions about the data-generating process, the loss function, the hypothesis class, or the goals of inference. These assumptions may be entirely legitimate, but they remain part of the surrounding model.

The distinction is therefore not between “having a prior” and “having no prior.” It is between recognizing that a prior is an explicitly modeled component of inference and reifying that prior into a property of the hypothesis itself.

This can be seen by comparing two formulations. The first says:

Under methodological framework \(M\), hypothesis \(H_1\) receives greater prior weight than hypothesis \(H_2\) because \(M\) penalizes complexity.

The second says:

Hypothesis \(H_1\) is intrinsically more probable than hypothesis \(H_2\) because it is simpler.

The first statement is transparent. It identifies the rule under which the probability assignment is made. The second suppresses that rule and makes the conclusion appear to follow from the hypothesis itself.

The objection from Bayesian necessity therefore misses the target. The criticism of intrinsic probability is not a criticism of prior distributions as such. A prior may be indispensable within a given model. It may also be well justified. What is denied is the stronger claim that the prior is somehow read directly from the hypothesis independently of the modeling commitments that generate it.

8.3 Probabilistic Rationality and Theory Choice

This distinction also prevents a common conflation between epistemic rationality and probabilistic representation. Even if one accepts the Bayesian view that rational degrees of belief should satisfy the probability axioms, it does not follow that every consideration relevant to theory choice must be encoded directly into a prior. Some considerations may guide the construction of the hypothesis space, determine which models are worth investigating, influence the loss function, or operate as external methodological constraints.

A researcher might, for example, prefer a simpler model because it is easier to interpret or less vulnerable to overfitting. That preference may affect which models are investigated or retained. It need not be interpreted as a direct statement that the simpler model has a greater probability of being true.

This is especially important because Bayesian inference and decision theory are often combined. In a decision-theoretic setting, probability and utility remain conceptually distinct:

\[ EU(a) = \sum_s P(s\mid K)U(a,s). \]

The framework does not require utility to be absorbed into probability. On the contrary, the usefulness of the formalism depends on keeping the two quantities separate. Probability represents uncertainty, while utility represents preference or value.

The same conceptual discipline should apply to theoretical virtues. If simplicity, elegance, or coherence function as methodological preferences, there is no requirement that they be converted into probabilities merely because Bayesian inference contains a prior term. They may remain external criteria of model evaluation.

The defender of intrinsic probability might reply that this creates an incomplete epistemology. If theoretical virtues influence rational belief, then they must somehow affect rational credence. But even if one grants this, the conclusion would still be only that theoretical virtues constrain or influence prior assignment under some epistemic theory. It would not show that the resulting probability is intrinsic to the hypothesis.

At most, one would have

\[ V(H),M,K \longrightarrow P(H\mid M,K). \]

The theoretical virtues matter because a methodological theory says they matter. The distinction becomes even clearer if different reasonable meta-theories generate different priors. Suppose one framework uses a strong complexity penalty while another uses a weak one: \(P_{M_1}(H)\propto e^{-\lambda_1 C(H)}\) and

\[ P_{M_2}(H)\propto e^{-\lambda_2 C(H)}, \]

with

\[ \lambda_1\neq\lambda_2. \]

Both are valid probability distributions. Both may be defensible under different modeling assumptions. The existence of these alternatives shows that the mere requirement for a prior does not uniquely determine its form.

What would be needed to establish intrinsic probability is a much stronger result: one would have to show that there exists a privileged prior that is determined by the content of the hypotheses themselves, rather than by an external methodological framework. The fact that Bayesian inference requires a prior does nothing to establish such a result.

8.4 Objective Priors

The same point applies to so-called objective priors. An objective prior may be constructed to satisfy principles such as invariance, maximum entropy, or reference behavior. But these methods still rely on modeling choices about parameterization, constraints, information structure, and inferential goals. Their objectivity, insofar as they possess it, is methodological rather than intrinsic.

The relevant distinction can therefore be summarized as

\[ \boxed{ \text{necessity of a prior} \neq \text{uniqueness of a prior} \neq \text{intrinsicness of a prior}. } \]

Recognizing this allows the critique of intrinsic probability to remain compatible with Bayesian inference. There is no need to deny that priors are useful or necessary, nor to deny that some priors are better justified than others. The point is simply that a justified prior is still a prior under a model. Theoretical virtues may constrain that model, and empirical or meta-empirical considerations may help justify those constraints. But once the dependence is acknowledged, the result is properly expressed as \(P(H\mid M,K)\), not

\[ P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H). \]

Bayesian reasoning therefore does not rescue intrinsic probability. It merely provides a formal location in which prior assumptions must be represented. The burden remains to justify those assumptions and to avoid mistaking their output for a property that belongs to the hypothesis independently of them.

Rejecting intrinsic probability does not leave us without a theory of scientific reasoning. It instead suggests a cleaner division of labor among probability, methodological evaluation, and decision theory.

9. A Better Alternative: Keep Probability and Methodological Preference Distinct

9.1 Keep Distinct Structures Distinct

If the notion of intrinsic probability is rejected, it does not follow that theoretical virtues become irrelevant. The more plausible alternative is to preserve a distinction between different kinds of epistemic structure. Probability can represent uncertainty, theoretical virtues can guide methodological preference, and decision-theoretic quantities can represent the value of actions or choices. These structures may interact, but they need not be collapsed into one another. For probabilistic uncertainty, the appropriate object remains something like \(P(H\mid E,K)\), where \(E\) represents evidence and \(K\) represents background assumptions or information. For methodological comparison, one may instead use a relation such as

\[ H_1\succeq_V H_2, \]

where \(V\) is a catalogue of theoretical virtues. This relation expresses the claim that \(H_1\) is at least as attractive as \(H_2\) according to the chosen methodological criteria. It does not imply any specific probabilistic relation between them.

Decision theory introduces yet another structure. If an agent must choose some action \(a\) under uncertainty, one may represent the desirability of outcomes with a utility function and combine that function with probability:

\[ EU(a) = \sum_s P(s\mid K)U(a,s). \]

The importance of this expression is not merely technical. It illustrates how distinct quantities can interact without being identified. Probability represents uncertainty concerning possible states; utility represents the value assigned to outcomes. The formalism works precisely because these roles remain separate.

The same conceptual discipline can be applied to theoretical virtues. Simplicity, coherence, elegance, explanatory scope, or unification may influence which hypotheses scientists prefer to investigate, which models they regard as explanatorily satisfactory, or which theories they provisionally adopt. None of this requires those virtues to be reinterpreted as probabilities. A researcher might therefore say that two hypotheses fit the available evidence similarly, but that one is preferable because it is simpler and more unified. Such a judgment can be represented as \(P(E\mid H_1,K)\approx P(E\mid H_2,K)\) while

\[ H_1\succ_V H_2. \]

There is no inconsistency in maintaining both statements. The first concerns empirical fit under a probabilistic model. The second concerns methodological preference. A theory can be methodologically preferable without there being a determinate claim that it is numerically more probable.

9.2 Preserve Multidimensional Evaluation

This separation also allows for richer forms of theoretical evaluation. Instead of collapsing all virtues into a single scalar, one can retain a multidimensional structure such as

\[ V(H) = \begin{pmatrix} S(H)\\ U(H)\\ C(H)\\ E(H) \end{pmatrix}. \]

The comparison of hypotheses may then yield a partial order rather than a complete ranking. In some cases one hypothesis may dominate another; in others the virtues may conflict and no unique ordering may be justified. This is not necessarily a limitation. It may be a more accurate representation of the actual epistemic situation.

The advantage of this framework is that it preserves distinctions that the notion of intrinsic probability tends to obscure. A hypothesis may be attractive for one reason and probable for another. A model may be preferred because it is computationally tractable, conceptually unified, or easy to interpret even when those features do not directly determine its probability of truth. Conversely, a hypothesis may receive substantial probability under a well-specified model while being methodologically unattractive for practical purposes.

9.3 Use Explicit Methodological Priors When Appropriate

This does not mean that theoretical virtues can never enter into probabilistic modeling. In some contexts, one may have good reasons to construct a prior that penalizes complexity or rewards certain structural features. If so, the proper procedure is to make that construction explicit. For example, one may define \(P_M(H) \propto e^{-\lambda C(H)}\), where \(C(H)\) is a specified complexity measure and \(\lambda\) controls the strength of the penalty. Such a prior may be useful for regularization, model selection, or predictive performance. But it should be described as what it is: a prior under a methodological model \(M\). The notation \(P_M(H)\) is preferable to

\[ P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H) \]

because it keeps the source of the probability visible. The probability is generated by a rule. It does not emerge directly from the hypothesis.

This distinction also clarifies the status of formal methods such as minimum description length, information criteria, or algorithmic priors. These approaches may encode principled preferences for simpler models, but their justification lies in the properties of the procedures themselves. They may improve prediction, control overfitting, optimize compression, or satisfy particular asymptotic criteria. Such results can provide strong reasons to use the methods. They do not imply that the favored hypotheses possess greater intrinsic probabilities independent of the procedures that favor them.

The positive alternative is therefore not hostile to formalization. On the contrary, it requires greater transparency about what is being formalized. If a complexity penalty is used, its form should be stated. If multiple theoretical virtues are aggregated, the weighting scheme should be explicit. If a prior depends on a representation language or background theory, that dependence should be acknowledged.

9.4 A Cleaner Inferential Architecture

This produces a cleaner inferential architecture:

\[ \text{evidence} \longrightarrow \text{likelihood structure}, \]

\[ \text{background assumptions} \longrightarrow \text{prior model}, \]

\[ \text{theoretical virtues} \longrightarrow \text{methodological preference}, \]

and

\[ \text{preferences and consequences} \longrightarrow \text{decision criteria}. \]

These components can interact without being identified. Under this framework, theoretical virtues retain an important role. They can affect which hypotheses are formulated, which are taken seriously, which are tested first, how competing explanations are compared, and which models are judged scientifically satisfying. They may also motivate explicit choices in probabilistic model construction. What they do not automatically provide is a probability of truth.

This distinction avoids the pressure to force every form of rational evaluation into the language of probability. It allows epistemic life to contain more than one kind of ordering or measure. Some judgments may be probabilistic; others may be ordinal, comparative, heuristic, or decision-theoretic. There is no obvious reason to assume in advance that all of them must be reducible to a single probabilistic scale.

The conceptual picture is therefore better represented as

\[ \boxed{ P(H\mid E,K) \quad\text{and}\quad H_1\succeq_V H_2 } \]

rather than by trying to absorb the second structure into the first.

This alternative preserves what is valuable in appeals to theoretical virtue while avoiding the stronger and more problematic claim that those virtues reveal an intrinsic probability. It allows us to say that one hypothesis is simpler, more coherent, or more elegant than another without pretending that these judgments determine a unique numerical probability of truth.

Where a probability is required, it should be supplied by an explicit probabilistic model. Where a methodological preference is being expressed, it should be represented as such. Keeping these categories distinct produces a clearer account of both probabilistic inference and scientific theory choice, and it removes the need to posit a mysterious probability residing within the hypothesis itself.

10. Conclusion: There Is No Probability Inside the Hypothesis

The concept of intrinsic probability initially appears to offer a natural way to think about the prior plausibility of competing hypotheses. Some theories seem simpler, more elegant, more coherent, or more unified than others, and it is tempting to interpret these differences as differences in probability prior to the consideration of evidence. The problem is that this interpretation imports far more structure than the theoretical virtues themselves provide.

A hypothesis does not generate its own probability measure. Probability theory can tell us how a measure must behave once specified, but it does not tell us which measure should be assigned to a space of hypotheses. To obtain a prior, one must introduce additional structure: a hypothesis space, a representation scheme, background assumptions, a set of evaluative criteria, and some rule for transforming those criteria into numerical weights. None of this is supplied by the proposition alone.

Theoretical virtues therefore do not naturally yield probabilities. At most, they provide a basis for comparative evaluation. One hypothesis may be methodologically preferable to another because it is simpler, more unified, or more coherent with existing knowledge. Such judgments can be represented by a preference relation,

\[ H_1\succeq_V H_2, \]

but this relation does not entail

\[ P(H_1)>P(H_2). \]

The move from preference to probability requires an additional theory about how methodological virtues track truth and how strongly they should influence prior weight.

In many cases, the virtues do not even generate a complete ranking. Different hypotheses may perform better along different dimensions, leaving only a partial order. One theory may be simpler but less unifying; another may be more elegant but less coherent with established knowledge. To collapse these multidimensional comparisons into a single scalar requires weights, tradeoffs, and aggregation rules. These are further methodological commitments, not properties discovered within the hypotheses.

The operationalization problem deepens the difficulty. Simplicity can mean parameter economy, short description length, low computational complexity, ontological parsimony, or ease of integration with existing theory. Elegance may refer to symmetry, compactness, unification, or the elimination of arbitrary structure. Different definitions can rank the same theories differently. Once again, the resulting evaluations depend upon choices made by the theorist rather than on some uniquely determined quantity intrinsic to the proposition. Even the strongest formal case, Kolmogorov complexity, does not escape this problem. A complexity measure such as \(K_U(H)\) is defined relative to a reference machine, and a prior such as

\[ P(H)\propto 2^{-K_U(H)} \]

requires a further rule connecting description length to probability mass. The construction may be mathematically rigorous, but its rigor reveals rather than eliminates its dependence on external structure. The resulting quantity is more accurately represented as

\[ P(H\mid U,M,K) \]

than as

\[ P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H). \]

The same conclusion follows from representation dependence more generally. If the alleged probability of a hypothesis changes when its coding language, background knowledge, or surrounding hypothesis space changes, then the probability cannot plausibly be intrinsic to the hypothesis itself. At most it is a model-relative assignment. The hidden structure can therefore be summarized as \(H \longrightarrow V(H) \longrightarrow \succeq_V\), followed, only after additional assumptions are introduced, by

\[ V(H),M \longrightarrow P_M(H). \]

What never follows directly is

\[ H \longrightarrow P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H). \]

This distinction is compatible with Bayesian inference. Priors may be necessary, useful, and well justified. Some priors may even be preferable to others on principled grounds. But a prior is prior to some evidence; it is not therefore independent of all background assumptions. The proper expression is often

\[ P(H\mid K,M), \]

where \(K\) and \(M\) contain the assumptions under which the prior is constructed.

The error of intrinsic-probability language is therefore not simply that it uses the wrong terminology. It obscures the source of the probability assignment. It makes a model-relative quantity appear to be a property of the hypothesis itself.

A proposition may have logical, semantic, syntactic, computational, or explanatory properties. It may be more or less coherent with background knowledge, more or less concise under a given representation, or more or less attractive according to a methodological framework. But none of these properties, taken by themselves, constitutes a probability measure.

Theoretical virtues may influence theory choice. They may help determine which hypotheses are worth pursuing, testing, or provisionally accepting. They may even motivate an explicit prior construction. What they do not establish is that a hypothesis possesses a probability independently of the framework used to evaluate it.

The central conclusion is therefore straightforward. The point is not merely that two independently established quantities receive different values; it is that a model-relative prior does not, by itself, establish any probability intrinsic to the hypothesis.

\[ \boxed{ P_M(H)\;\not\Rightarrow\;P_{\text{intrinsic}}(H) } \]

The former is intelligible: it is the probability assigned to a hypothesis under a specified model. The latter would require a probability residing within the hypothesis itself. Once the necessary methodological, representational, and probabilistic assumptions are made explicit, there is no reason to think such a quantity has been established.
