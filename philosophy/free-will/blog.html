Free Will, Responsibility, and Conceptual Confusion

I’ve got to admit, I don’t understand all of the fuss about this concept. Free will often seems like one of those topics philosophers can argue about ad infinitum without making much progress. Outside philosophy, the discussion is frequently reduced just as quickly to a binary: either we are free or we are determined. I think both habits obscure the more useful question.

Core claim. “Will” names a set of capacities characteristic of agents: forming and revising desires, setting goals, interpreting information, making decisions, and acting on an environment. “Freedom” qualifies the degree to which those capacities can be exercised under particular constraints. On this view, free will is not primarily a binary classification; it is a question of degree, structure, and control.

Argument in brief

Define the terms → distinguish agency from automation → distinguish constraint from control → separate predictability, determinism, and randomness → test libertarian agent causation → apply the framework to classical theism and foreknowledge → return to responsibility and decision-making.

Part I A conceptual framework The first problem is semantic and conceptual: the debate often treats gradable and relational ideas as though they were all-or-nothing properties.

What do “free” and “will” mean?

Among non-philosophers especially, there is a tendency to reduce free will to a binary distinction while ignoring the fact that “freedom” itself behaves like a gradient. If we think about the semantics of the lexemes in the phrase, “freedom” can be treated as a degree-term or grade-term relative to some operational criteria. It can also be treated as a rank-term: each rank covers an interval within which freedom can increase or decrease, and a threshold can produce a discontinuous move into a higher or lower rank.

“Will,” by contrast, is not something I imagine as a mysterious object that a person either possesses or lacks. I use it as shorthand for our capacity to interact with an environment in a directed, non-arbitrary manner. That capacity is a brute feature of ourselves as agents. Once the two terms are separated in this way, the useful question becomes: to what degree, and under what conditions, can an agent exercise its capacities?

I am not claiming that this particular diagram is definitive. The point is that a conceptualization resembling it gives us more room to talk about differences among agents, contexts, and kinds of constraint. Instead of asking only whether free will “exists,” we can ask where an agent sits in a possibility space, what restricts movement through it, and what sorts of changes expand or contract the range of action.

Key distinctions

Much of the confusion in the debate comes from treating related concepts as synonyms. The following distinctions organize the rest of the argument.

TermMeaning in this frameworkNot equivalent to
WillA cluster of agential capacities: desire, interpretation, goal formation, intentionality, decision-making, and action.An immaterial substance or an action with no causal history.
FreedomThe degree or range within which an agent can exercise those capacities.The total absence of constraint.
ConstraintA limitation generated by embodiment, history, relations, boundaries, or environmental conditions.Complete control by an external controller.
ControllabilityThe extent to which a system’s state can be driven or fixed by a controlling mechanism.Ordinary causal influence.
PredictabilityAn observer’s ability to forecast the behavior or state of a system.Determinism.
IndeterminismThe denial that every future state is fully entailed by prior states and fixed laws.Randomness or arbitrariness.
AgencyGoal-directed, interpretive, environment-responsive action by a system capable of altering its own behavior and surroundings.Libertarian “agent causation.”

With those distinctions in place, the next question is what sort of system can meaningfully be described as having a will at all.

Part II Agency, constraint, and determinism A systems-science perspective makes it easier to distinguish agency from automation and causal influence from total control.

What makes something an agent?

I think a systems-science perspective is useful here because it gives us vocabulary for distinguishing systems with different degrees of teleology, responsiveness, and autonomy. I take it for granted that human beings are not mere automata. Biological organisms can be modeled as finite-state machines for particular purposes, but the model should not be confused with the thing modeled. The map is not the territory.

What, then, distinguishes an agent from an automaton? Humans possess a cluster of properties that I collectively mean by “the Will.” We have drivenness and can form or reformulate desires in light of information and stimuli. We are sentient and experience qualia. We engage in goal formation and goal setting. We are agents whose behavior need not conform mechanically to normative standards of rationality. We exhibit consciousness and intentionality, and we have a degree of decision-making capacity.

  • Desire: the capacity to form and revise what is wanted.
  • Sentience: the capacity for subjective experience.
  • Goal formation: the ability to organize behavior around ends.
  • Intentionality: behavior that is directed toward objects, states, or purposes.
  • Decision capacity: the ability to select among possibilities in light of information.
  • Interpretation: the ability to encounter a new interpretation and reorganize goals accordingly.

These capacities allow us to alter an environment in pursuit of objectives and, importantly, to revise the objectives themselves. That is what I mean when I say that someone “has a Will.” The claim is deliberately minimal. External pressure can influence our goals, and neurobiological failures can interfere with our capacities, but neither fact shows that the capacities do not exist. It shows that their expression can be constrained.

This matters because an entity that were literally unaffected by its environment would not thereby become more meaningfully free. To act is to stand in relations, and relations make causal influence possible. An agent must be able to receive information from an environment and respond to it. On my view, the distinctive feature is not causal isolation but the complex organization that permits a system to respond to information in ways that are not reducible to a simple input-output automaton.

Constraint is not control

Freedom can therefore be contrasted with controllability. Suppose someone claims that the subconscious completely determines conscious mental states. If “determines” means complete control, the conscious level effectively collapses into the subconscious level. But if consciousness is one of the properties by which we identify a system as having a will, then the more careful claim is that subconscious processes influence conscious processes. We are then arguing about the degree and structure of that influence—not about whether agency exists at all.

A related objection says that because the brain is algorithmic, or because its processes can be represented as something like a distributed graph, the brain must therefore be procedural and deterministic. That inference moves too quickly from a description of lower-level mechanisms to a conclusion about the capacities of the system as a whole. I do not think a will requires an immaterial entity in addition to brain processes; I treat consciousness as an emergent property of a complex biological system. Postulating an invisible additional substance does not solve the problem and, in my view, imports metaphysical folk theories where a systems account is sufficient.

Relation implies constraint; constraint does not imply controllability.

Constraints are not merely obstacles. They are also enabling conditions and are necessary for a system to persist. We live within systems and are ourselves composed of systems. I represent a system schematically as the six-tuple S = {C, N, I, B, K, H}:

ElementRole
C — ComponentsThe components of the system and their properties.
N — NetworkA description of the relations or network among those components.
I — EnvironmentA description of the environment in which the system operates.
B — Boundary conditionsThe boundaries and conditions that delimit the system.
K — Interaction rulesThe interaction rules and state-transition rules among components.
H — HistoryThe history of prior states—the system’s memory.

The existence of relations among components necessarily creates constraints, but relations can also create possibilities that were unavailable at earlier states. A new relation can generate a new choice; a past decision can alter which relations exist later. In this sense, constraint and enablement are two sides of being situated in a system. For a richer description of this kind of indeterminist enablement, see No entailing laws, but enablement in the evolution of the biosphere.

Being radically embodied and contextually bound therefore does not, by itself, undermine free will. It establishes the conditions under which agency is possible. We are situated within contexts that are substantially outside our control while possessing capabilities and dispositions that let us modify those contexts. There is no “free lunch,” but neither does situatedness amount to total controllability.

Determinism, predictability, and randomness

Biological organisms vary in the extent to which their behavior can be modeled as deterministic. Human behavior, however, is often stochastic from an observer’s perspective. This does not mean that behavior is therefore inherently random. It means that the set of possible responses in a situation cannot ordinarily be forecast with high reliability for long periods without imposing a substantial degree of control—and even control can provoke spontaneous responses that were not predicted a priori.

A distinction that the argument needs to keep explicit: unpredictability is not the same thing as metaphysical indeterminism, just as indeterminism is not the same thing as randomness. An unpredictable deterministic system is conceptually possible. So empirical unpredictability can motivate skepticism about simple deterministic models, but it does not by itself prove that determinism is false. The stronger claim about indeterminism has to rest on the account of causation, emergence, agency, and open-ended system dynamics—not on forecasting difficulty alone.

Long-run human behavior is often non-ergodic: the time average of one system is not simply the ensemble average across every system. At times behavior can look superficially like Brownian motion, while collective behavior can undergo rapid regime shifts produced by unstable, divergent dynamics at lower levels. From a systems-modeling perspective, there are no known exogenous predictors that reliably force human system dynamics to deviate in one fixed direction over long periods.

I bring this up because arguments against free will often slide from “behavior has predictors” to “behavior is predictable,” and then from “predictable” to “determined.” Those are different claims. Human individuals and collectives can be highly influenced by prior conditions while remaining difficult to forecast in non-arbitrary ways. Non-arbitrary and deterministic are not synonyms.

My stronger view is that reductive determinism does not adequately capture biological agency. I regard the evolution of living systems, their capacity to alter relations, and their open-ended interaction with environments as evidence pointing toward indeterminism. But that is a stronger thesis than the claim that humans are merely hard to predict, and it should be defended as such rather than smuggled in through the language of unpredictability.

Local constraint does not imply global determinism

The usual definition of free will as the “ability to have acted otherwise in a given situation” can also create confusion if it is interpreted globally. Imagine falling from a cliff. Once the fall is underway, physical constraints sharply restrict what can happen to you. In that local region of the state space, your freedom is extremely limited. But it does not follow that your entire life, the events leading to the fall, or every intervention available to other agents was globally determined by that moment.

The same point applies to decision histories. A chain of earlier decisions may place an agent at a node where only one practical option remains. That local convergence does not establish that every prior or future possibility was globally fixed. We are not free in all situations, and that is precisely why a graded conception of freedom is more useful than an all-or-nothing one.

The graded account gives us a positive model of agency. We can now ask whether the traditional libertarian alternative is coherent on its own terms.

Part III Libertarian free will and agent causation The central objection is not that agents are influenced by causes, but that an agent conceived as causally independent of its environment becomes difficult to place in any multi-agent causal system.

Why libertarian free will is the wrong target

At the conceptual level, I think the historical discussion has often failed to establish enough coherence among its terms. Libertarian free will is usually presented as the claim that human beings can originate choices that are not determined by prior causes or external constraints. Determinism sits at the opposite extreme: all events, including decisions, are causally entailed by preceding events. In my view, both extremes miss the relational structure of agency. As I put it earlier, the extremes are “not even false” until the terms are made coherent enough to evaluate.

Libertarianism often appeals to “agent causation”: the individual agent is treated as an originator of action independently of deterministic forces. The difficulty is that if independence means an absence of causal influence from prior states or the environment, the action threatens to become random or arbitrary. That undermines the very control the theory was introduced to protect. To make the problem more concrete, consider a simple causal graph with two agents.

A visual argument

The following figures are best read as one cumulative argument rather than as isolated diagrams.

Step 1 — Give agent A libertarian freedom

Start with a two-agent system in which A has libertarian agent-causal freedom while B does not.

Step 2 — Apply the same criterion to B

If both entities are genuinely free agents in the same libertarian sense, B must also possess the ability to originate action without being causally determined by its environment.

Step 3 — Let both agents exert causal power

Now causal arrows must extend outward from both source nodes if each agent genuinely has causal power over the environment.

Step 4 — Ask whether either agent can also receive causal influence

On the strong conception I am criticizing, an incoming arrow threatens the claim that the receiving agent originates action independently of external causal influence. If incoming arrows are forbidden for both agents, the graph collapses toward a set of disconnected nodes.

But that result contradicts the motivating idea of agent causation: agents are supposed to exercise genuine causal influence on an environment and on one another. A multi-agent system cannot consist entirely of nodes that causally affect others while never receiving any incoming causal influence themselves.

Step 5 — The single-root exception

There is one structurally different possibility: a root node that does not respond to exogenous influences but can affect every successor node.

On this model, no more than one node can possess the strongest form of agent-causal independence. If libertarian freedom requires that sort of causal status, it cannot be a property possessed symmetrically by a population of mutually interacting human agents. Either the property belongs to at most one privileged root, or ordinary causal relations must be admitted.

Step 6 — The two options

That leaves two broad alternatives. The first allows free agents to be causally influenced by their environments while retaining organized causal power of their own.

The second tries to preserve total causal independence.

In the second scenario, the system begins to look like a collection of random particles in a vacuum: undetermined, yet arbitrary. That is not the kind of freedom I want. The graph exercise is therefore meant to motivate a reconsideration of what “free will” should mean rather than to defend causal isolation as an ideal.

Returning to the freedom gradient

The existence of constraints qualifies the degree, rank, and grade of freedom that a will can express. More importantly, the existence of a top-down controller is a different matter from the mere existence of constraints. If our choices were completely driven by such a controller, our will would occupy the lowest region of the freedom scale even though the underlying agential capacities might remain present.

  • Is freedom path-dependent? Yes.
  • Do humans choose like weak learners using simple reward functions? Presumably not.
  • Do we respond to incentives? Yes, but incentives and responses are often unstable and difficult to forecast.
  • Is the human system fully controllable? I do not think so.

Economists have repeatedly encountered the difficulty of reducing human choice to simple utility maximization under fixed preferences and budget constraints. All of these considerations move an agent up or down the “free” scale. I am not trying to assign humanity a precise coordinate. My narrower point is that we plainly exhibit the capacities I am calling “Will,” while there is no known top-down controller that necessitates every decision. We do not acquire or receive this will as an object; the word is a convenient noun for a set of capacities that are part of what we are. Constraints qualify how freely those capacities can be expressed.

This framework also changes how the familiar connection between libertarian freedom and classical theism looks.

Part IV Theism, foreknowledge, and control Classical theism supplies a useful limiting case because it posits a maximally knowledgeable and powerful agent standing in an asymmetric causal relation to creation.

Theism as the limiting case

I view free will—or at least an approximation of the concept—as fundamental to humanity, but I think the historical concept often obscures what agency actually involves. Libertarian notions of free will have also been closely connected with theism. If the causal-graph argument above is right, the one scenario in which the strongest version of agent causation is structurally possible is one in which at most a single agent occupies the privileged root position. That makes classical theism an especially interesting case.

Classical theism characterizes God as omnipotent, omniscient, and omnibenevolent. Such an entity could exert causal influence on its creation without being reciprocally controlled by it. If it intends to act and cannot fail through lack of power or knowledge, its intentions are executed as intended. Combined with omniscience, this means the agent cannot be surprised by anything within its creation. Nothing can deviate from what it infallibly knows and has the power to bring about unless one of those attributes is qualified. Given that characterization, it is difficult for me not to see the deity as a kind of deterministic controller.

Laplace’s demon and the controller analogy

Many theists assert that atheism entails determinism, often by conflating causal determinism with non-belief. I do not think atheism is itself a worldview, much less a commitment to a particular physics. The comparison with Laplace’s demon is revealing. In the classical picture, if an intellect knew the precise state of every relevant component and the laws governing the system, past and future states would be calculable. The key condition is knowledge: the demon sees the determinism because it is effectively omniscient with respect to the system.

This classical deterministic picture relies on several assumptions:

  1. The universe is governed by physical laws: nature operates through unchanging laws that determine the behavior of every system.
  2. Complete knowledge implies predictability: given all initial conditions and governing laws, past and future states are in principle computable.
  3. Causal closure: every physical event has a physical cause.
  4. Absence of randomness: at least in the classical-mechanical picture, no fundamental indeterminacy interrupts the causal chain.
  5. Sufficient information and computational power: the hypothetical intellect requires perfect information and effectively unlimited computational power, capacities unavailable to ordinary human observers.

Assumptions two through five resemble properties attributed to the deity of classical theism. Nothing lies outside an omnipotent entity’s control, and everything is known to an omniscient entity. On the characterization I am criticizing, Laplace’s demon begins to resemble the entity described by classical theism. The irony is that libertarian free will is often invoked as something atheism supposedly cannot preserve, while the classical theistic model appears to create its own deterministic structure for every non-divine agent.

The tension: if only one causally privileged root can possess the strongest form of libertarian agent causation, classical theism may reserve that freedom for God while making every created agent part of a possibility space already known—and, under omnipotence, ultimately governed—by that root agent.

I think this problem arises from the same confused notions of “free” and “will” discussed earlier. On my account, free agency fits more naturally with the absence of an all-controlling deity of the classical kind. A theist could respond by modifying the classical attributes; Open Theism, for example, is one way of revising the picture. My criticism is therefore aimed at traditional tri-omni conceptions rather than at every possible form of theism, and it is not limited to Calvinism.

Foreknowledge and theological fatalism

This connects with a more familiar family of problems concerning fatalism: the idea that some future event is already inevitable. I reject global fatalism, but divine foreknowledge creates a specific modal problem. The literature on Foreknowledge and Free Will formulates an argument for theological fatalism that can be stated informally and then formally.

For any future act you will perform, if some being infallibly believed in the past that the act would occur, there is nothing you can do now about the fact that he believed what he believed since nobody has any control over past events; nor can you make him mistaken in his belief, given that he is infallible. Therefore, there is nothing you can do now about the fact that he believed in a way that cannot be mistaken that you would do what you will do. But if so, you cannot do otherwise than what he believed you would do. And if you cannot do otherwise, you will not perform the act freely.

  1. Yesterday God infallibly believed T. [Supposition of infallible foreknowledge]
  2. If E occurred in the past, it is now-necessary that E occurred then. [Principle of the Necessity of the Past]
  3. It is now-necessary that yesterday God believed T. [1, 2]
  4. Necessarily, if yesterday God believed T, then T. [Definition of “infallibility”]
  5. If p is now-necessary, and necessarily (p → q), then q is now-necessary. [Transfer of Necessity Principle]
  6. So it is now-necessary that T. [3, 4, 5]
  7. If it is now-necessary that T, then you cannot do otherwise than answer the telephone tomorrow at 9 a.m. [Definition of “necessary”]
  8. Therefore, you cannot do otherwise than answer the telephone tomorrow at 9 a.m. [6, 7]
  9. If you cannot do otherwise when you do an act, you do not act freely. [Principle of Alternate Possibilities]
  10. Therefore, when you answer the telephone tomorrow at 9 a.m., you will not do it freely. [8, 9]

The central idea is that infallible foreknowledge and the ability to do otherwise sit in tension. I think omnipotence intensifies the problem because it adds control over the structure of the possibility space, even though omnipotence is not formally required by the argument above.

The compatibilist reply

There are many responses to theological fatalism, including compatibilist responses. Compatibilism maintains that freedom can be preserved even if determinism is true, and many atheists are compatibilists as well. I am an incompatibilist: if determinism were true in the strong global sense, I find it difficult to see how the kind of freedom developed in this essay could survive. I also think determinism is false, although that claim requires the distinctions developed earlier rather than a simple appeal to unpredictability.

My argumentative strategy is therefore something like a Moorean Shift: if we have stronger reason to reject global determinism than to accept a traditional theistic model that entails it, the pressure shifts onto the model. This resembles a free-will argument for the nonexistence of God, though I think it needs considerably more elaboration.

A common response, illustrated by this theistic reply, is:

God knows what we will choose, but so long as his knowledge is not a cause of our choice our will is free within its constrained perspective.

The strongest version of that reply distinguishes knowledge from causation. I agree that knowledge does not have to cause the choice. My objection is modal rather than merely causal: if an infallible past belief already fixes which proposition about my future act is true, in what sense is the contrary act still genuinely possible? Adding omnipotence makes the issue sharper because the divine agent is also responsible for the structure in which the available possibilities arise.

So the reply does not strike me as sufficient merely by observing that knowledge is not an efficient cause. If God infallibly knows what I will choose, I cannot make the contrary proposition true without making that knowledge mistaken. And if the same deity also engineered the relevant possibility space and its own intentions manifest without failure, the set of alternatives available to created agents begins to look illusory. That is the point the causal and modal arguments are intended to isolate.

The debate ultimately returns to the positive question: what kind of freedom is actually worth defending?

Part V What remains of free will The positive account is neither causal isolation nor total independence, but situated agency operating with varying degrees of freedom inside changing constraints.

A positive account

The view I am defending is therefore not libertarian free will in the traditional sense. Will is the organized capacity of an agent to desire, interpret, deliberate, form goals, revise goals, decide, and act. Freedom is the degree to which those capacities can be exercised across a possibility space. Constraints shape that space; some constraints suppress options, while others make agency possible in the first place. Causal influence is unavoidable in any relational system, but causal influence is not the same thing as complete control.

In compact form: agency requires relations; relations generate both constraints and possibilities; freedom varies with the structure of those constraints; and the loss of freedom occurs not whenever causes exist, but as an agent’s capacity to interpret, choose, and act is progressively bypassed or controlled.

This is why path dependence, incentives, embodiment, history, biological mechanisms, and social context do not automatically negate free will. They are among the variables that determine how much freedom a particular agent has in a particular situation. The interesting empirical and philosophical work begins when we ask how those variables change the available possibility space and the agent’s ability to navigate it.

Responsibility and why the debate matters

The topic remains interesting because the conclusion one reaches depends heavily on how one conceptualizes modality, causality, and time. Part of my motivation for writing this was frustration with the idea that “scientific laws” are simply binding in the same way at every level of a system of systems. The discussion also overlaps with moral responsibility. I think non-belief is often unfairly characterized as entailing determinism and therefore a lack of moral accountability, even though atheism by itself entails neither claim. In religious contexts that association can become a form of fearmongering and contribute to the demonization of non-believers.

At the same time, the account developed here does not by itself settle every question about responsibility. It reframes them. Instead of asking whether a person possessed an impossible, causally isolated freedom, we can ask whether the person had the relevant decision-making capacities, what information was available, which constraints were operative, whether another system bypassed or controlled those capacities, and how much practical room for alternative action remained. That connects the metaphysical discussion more directly to the kinds of distinctions actually used when evaluating responsibility.

So even if the traditional free-will debate sometimes resembles a philosophical language game, it opens useful lines of inquiry into some of our most basic assumptions. I am interested in the topic largely because of Decision Theory and, more broadly, because of the question of how people make choices and what rationality amounts to in systems that are embodied, historically situated, and only partially controllable.
